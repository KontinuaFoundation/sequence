\chapter{Alternating Current}
\index{Alternating Current}
Plug in a phone charger, and something strange is going on inside the
wall. The electrons in the wire are not flowing from the power plant to
your house at all. They are sloshing back and forth, 60 times a second,
going nowhere. Yet they deliver all the energy you use. This chapter
explains how, and why the whole world's power grid works this way.

A battery pushes electrons in one direction at a constant voltage; this is
\newterm{direct current}\index{direct current}, or DC. Most batteries
provide between 1.5 and 12 volts.

The electrical power that comes into your home on wires is
different. If you plotted the voltage over time, it would look like
this:

      \draw[->,thick] (0,-1.8) -- (0, 1.8) node[above] {$volts$};

      \end{tikzpicture}

    \label{fig:acvoltsGraph}

The $x$ axis here represents 0 volts. When you insert a two-prong plug
into an outlet, one prong is ``hot'' and the other is ``neutral.'' The
neutral wire is connected to the earth at your electrical panel, so it
stays at about 0 volts, the same as the dirt under the building. (The
round third hole on many outlets is a separate safety \emph{ground}.)

The voltage is a sine wave at 60Hz. Your voltage fluctuates between
-170v and 170v. Think for a second what that means: the power company pushes electrons
with 170 volts, then pulls them back with 170 volts, alternating 60 times
per second. This is \newterm{alternating current}, or AC. (In Europe and
much of the world, it is 230 volts at 50 Hz.)

\section{Power of AC}



Let us say you turn on your toaster, which has a resistance of 14.4
ohms. How much power (in watts) does it turn into heat? We know that $I = V/R$ and that watts of power are
$IV$. So, given a voltage of $V$, the toaster is consuming $V^2/R$
watts.

However, $V$ is fluctuating. Let us plot the power the toaster is consuming:
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tikzpicture}[
    tl/.style = {% tick labels
        fill=white, inner sep=1pt, font=\scriptsize,
                },                        ]
    % grid
    \draw[sdkblue, very thin, xstep=0.5235, ystep=0.5] (0,0) grid (6.6,2.1);
    
    % y tick label
    \foreach \y in {500, 1000, 1500, 2000}{\node[tl,left=1mm] at (0,{\y/1000}) {$\y$};}
    
    % x tick label
    \foreach \x in {0.004,0.008, 0.012, 0.016}{\node[tl,below=1mm] at ({392.67*\x},0) {$\x$};}
    
    % axes
    \draw[->,thick] (0,0) -- (6.5,0) node[right] {$seconds$};
    \draw[->,thick] (0,0) -- (0, 2.1) node[above] {$watts$};
    % curve
    \draw[<->,thick,draw=black,domain=0:6.5,samples=300,variable=\x]
          plot (\x,{sin(deg(\x))^2 * 2.007});
    \end{tikzpicture}
    \caption{A plot of AC power over time.}
    \label{fig:acpowerGraph}
\end{figure}
\index{Alternating Current ! graphed}
Another wave! It never goes negative, because $V^2$ is never negative.
Its shape comes from a power-reducing identity from the trigonometric
identities chapter: $\sin^2(x) = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2}\cos(2x)$.

This is actually a cosine wave flipped upside down, scaled down by
half the peak power, and translated up so that it is never
negative. Note that it is also twice the frequency of the voltage sine
wave.

If we say the peak voltage is $V_p$ and the resistance of the toaster
is $R$, the power is given by

$$\frac{V_p^2}{2R} - \frac{V_p^2}{2R} \cos \left(2\pi \cdot 120\, t \right)$$
(The power wave repeats 120 times per second, twice the frequency of the
voltage, because it peaks at both the top and the bottom of the voltage
wave.)

As a toaster user and as someone who pays a power bill, you are mostly
interested in the average power.  To get the average power, you take
the area under the power graph and divide it by the amount of time.

Split the area under the curve into two pieces:
\begin{itemize}
\item A constant function of $y = \frac{V_p^2}{2R}$
\item A wave $y = - \frac{V_p^2}{2R} \cos \left(2\pi \cdot 120\, t \right)$
\end{itemize}

\begin{tikzpicture}[
tl/.style = {% tick labels
    fill=white, inner sep=1pt, font=\scriptsize,
            },                        ]
% grid
\draw[sdkblue, very thin, xstep=0.5235, ystep=0.5] (0,-1.1) grid (6.6,1.1);

% y tick label
\foreach \y in {-1000, -500, 500, 1000}{\node[tl,left=1mm] at (0,{\y/1000}) {$\y$};}

% x tick label
\foreach \x in {0.004,0.008, 0.012, 0.016}{\node[tl,below=1mm] at ({392.67*\x},0) {$\x$};}

% axes
\draw[->,thick] (0,0) -- (6.5,0) node[right] {$seconds$};
\draw[<->,thick] (0,-1.1) -- (0, 1.1) node[above] {$watts$};
% curve
\draw[<->,thick,draw=black] (0,1.003) -- (6.5, 1.003) node[right] {Constant: $\frac{V_p^2}{2R}$};
\draw[<->,thick,draw=black,domain=0:6.5,samples=300,variable=\x]
      plot (\x,{-1 * cos(deg(\x) * 2) * 1.003}) node[right]{Wave};
\end{tikzpicture}
    
The area under the constant line over a time $t$ is a rectangle:
$\frac{V_p^2}{2R} \cdot t$.

The area under the wave over a complete cycle is\ldots zero! The part above
the axis exactly cancels the part below.

So the average power is $\frac{V_p^2}{2R}$ watts: exactly half the peak
power. For the toaster, $\frac{170^2}{2(14.4)} \approx 1000$ watts.

Someone at some point said, ``I am used to power being $V^2/R$. Can
we define a voltage measure for AC power such that this is always true?''

So we use the \index{RMS voltage}\newterm{RMS voltage}
$V_{rms} = \frac{V_p}{\sqrt{2}}$. Then the average power is
$\frac{V_{rms}^2}{R}$, just like DC. If you look on the back of anything that plugs
into a standard US power outlet, it will say something like ``For
120v''.  What they mean is, ``For 120v RMS, we expect the voltage to
fluctuate back and forth from 170v to -170v.''

RMS stands for root-mean-square: square the voltage, take the mean
(average), then take the square root. For any sine wave, the RMS value is
$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707$ times the peak.

\textbf{Worked example.} A household outlet has an RMS voltage of $120$ V.
What is its peak voltage?

\textbf{Solution.} $V_{\text{peak}} = \sqrt{2}\,V_{\text{rms}} = 120\sqrt{2} \approx 170$ V.

For current, we do the same thing. If the current is AC, the power
consumed by a resistor is $I_{RMS}^2 R$, where $I_{RMS}$ is the peak
current divided by $\sqrt{2}$.

\section{Power Line Losses}

\index{power line losses}A wire has some resistance. Thinner wires tend to have more resistance
than thicker ones. Aluminum wires tend to have more resistance than
copper wires.

Let us say that the power that comes to your house has to travel 20 km
from the generator in a cable that has about $1 \Omega$ of resistance
per km.  Let us also say that your home is consuming 12 kilowatts of power. If that
power traveled the whole way at 120 V RMS, how much would be lost heating
the power line?

The current would be $I = \frac{P}{V} = \frac{12{,}000}{120} = 100$ amps
RMS. In the 20 $\Omega$ line, $I^2R = (100^2)(20) = 200{,}000$ watts would
be lost to heat. That is more than 16 times the power your home uses! The
line would glow, and the plan is hopeless.

What if the power company moved the power at 120,000 volts RMS? Now only
$\frac{12{,}000}{120{,}000} = 0.1$ amps flow in the line, and the loss is
$I^2R = (0.1^2)(20) = 0.2$ watts. A thousand times the voltage means a
thousandth of the current, and a millionth of the loss.

This is much, much more efficient. The only problem is that 120,000 volts
would be incredibly dangerous.  So the power company moves power long
distances at very high voltages, like 765 kV.  Before the power is
brought into your home, it is converted into a lower voltage using a
\newterm{transformer}\index{transformer}.
\index{Alternating Current ! transformers}
\section{Transformers}

A transformer is a device that converts electrical power from one
voltage to another. A good transformer is more than 95\% efficient. The
details of magnetic fields, flux, and inductance are beyond the scope
of this chapter, so we are going to give a relatively simple (and admittedly incomplete) explanation for now.

A transformer is a ring with two sets of coils wrapped around it.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{transformer.png}
    \caption{A diagram of a transformer from Wikipedia.}
    \label{fig:transformer}
\end{figure}

When alternating current is run through the primary winding, it creates magnetic
flux in the ring. The magnetic flux induces current in the secondary
winding. (This is called \newterm{induction}.)

If $V_P$ is the voltage across the primary winding and $V_S$ is the
voltage across the secondary winding, they are related by the
following equation:

$$\frac{V_P}{V_S} = \frac{N_P}{N_S}$$

where $N_P$ and $N_S$ are the number of turns in the primary and
secondary windings.

There are usually at least two transformers between you and the very
high voltage lines. There are transformers at the substation that make
the voltage low enough to travel on regular utility poles. On the utility poles, you will see gray cans that contain smaller
transformers. Those step the voltage down to the 120 V RMS that your home
uses.

This is the big reason the world uses AC: transformers only work with
alternating current, because they depend on a \emph{changing} magnetic
field.

\begin{Exercise}[title={Turns ratio}, label=ac_transformer]
A pole transformer takes 7,200 V RMS and delivers 240 V RMS to a
neighborhood. The secondary coil has 50 turns. How many turns does the
primary coil have?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=ac_transformer]
$\frac{N_P}{N_S} = \frac{V_P}{V_S} = \frac{7{,}200}{240} = 30$, so
$N_P = 30 \times 50 = 1{,}500$ turns.
\end{Answer}

The voltage on each is $2\pi/3$ out of phase with the other two:

    (\x,{sin(deg(\x) + 120) * 1.7});
    \end{tikzpicture}
    \caption{A diagram of three AC signals, each $2\pi/3$ radians out of phase.}
    \label{fig:ACphase}
\end{figure}

This is nice in two ways:
\begin{itemize}
\item While the power in each wire is fluctuating, the total power is not fluctuating at all.
\item At every instant, the three currents add up to zero, so no return
  wire is needed: three wires do the work of three separate circuits.
\end{itemize}
(Both these assume that each wire is attached to a load with the same constant resistance.)

In big industrial factories, you will see all three wires enter the
building. Large amounts of smooth power delivery means a great deal to an
industrial user.

In residential settings, each home gets its power from one of the three
wires. However, two wires typically carry power into the home. Each
one carries 120V RMS, but they are out of phase by 180 degrees. Lights
and small appliances are connected to one of the wires and ground, so
they get 120V RMS. Large appliances, like air conditioners, clothes dryers, and electric
ovens, are connected across the two wires, so they get 240 V RMS.

How do you get two circuits, 180 degrees out of phase, from one? With a
\index{center-tap transformer}\newterm{center-tap transformer}. Its
secondary coil has an extra wire attached to its exact middle, which
becomes the neutral. Whenever one end of the coil is 170 V above the
middle, the other end is 170 V below it. So the two ends are perfectly out
of phase, each 120 V RMS from the middle and 240 V RMS from each other.

\begin{Exercise}[title={RMS from Peak}, label=ac2]
A signal has a peak voltage of $340$ V. What is its RMS voltage?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=ac2]
$V_{\text{rms}} = \frac{340}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 240$ V.
\end{Answer}

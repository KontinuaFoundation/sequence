\chapter{Tides and Eclipses}
\index{orbital paths}
Your life on earth involves many orbital paths:
\begin{itemize}

\item The Earth is spinning.  If you are standing at the equator,  you are traveling at 1,674 km per hour around the center of the planet.  We are all spinning east,  which is why the sun comes up in the east and sets in the west.

\item  The Earth is orbiting the sun. It takes 365.242 days for the Earth to go once around the sun.  This is why different constellations appear at different times during the year --- we only see the stars at night and the direction of night shifts as the Earth moves around the sun.  

\item The moon is orbiting the Earth. The moon travels once around the Earth once every 27.3 days.  

\end{itemize}

You can see the effects of these orbits on our planet. Let us go over a few.

\section{Leap Years}\index{leap year}



Note that it takes 365.242 days for the Earth to go around the sun.  If we declared "The calendar will \emph{always} be 365 days per year!"  then the seasons would gradually shift by 0.242 days every year.  After a century,  they would have migrated 24 days.

So, we made a rule: "Every fourth year,  we will add an extra day to the calendar!"  The years 2021, 2022,and 2023 get no February 29th, but 2024 does.

That got us a calendar with an average 365.25 days per year, so the seasons would not have migrated as quickly,  but they still would have migrated about three days every four hundred years.

So, we made another rule: `There will be no February 29th in the three century years (multiples of 100) that are not multiples of 400.' So the year 1900 had no Feb 29,  but the year 2000 had one. Now, the average number of days per year is 365.2425, very close to the
true 365.242.

\textbf{Worked example.} Will 2100 be a leap year?

\textbf{Solution.} 2100 is divisible by 4 and by 100, but not by 400, so
it is not a leap year.


\section{Phases of the Moon}
\index{moon ! phases of}
The Earth, the moon, and the sun form a triangle. If you were standing on the moon, you could measure the angle between the light coming from the sun and the light going to the Earth. That angle would fluctuate between 0 degrees and 180 degrees.  
\begin{itemize}
\item When the angle was close to 0,  the people on Earth would see a full moon (fully illuminated disc).  
\item When the angle was close to 90 degrees,  the people on Earth would see a half moon. 
\item When the angle was nearing 180 degrees, the people on Earth would see a slim crescent.
\item When the angle was very close to 180 degrees,  the moon would be dark.  This is called a new moon (fully darked disc).
\end{itemize}

In the 27.3 days that it took the moon to travel around the Earth,  the Earth has moved about 27 degrees around the sun.  To get back into the same triangle configuration takes another 2.2 days.

\section{Eclipses}
\index{moon ! eclipses}
While the Earth orbits the sun and the moon orbits the Earth,  the two orbits are \emph{not in the same plane}.
We call the plane that the Earth orbits the sun in the \index{ecliptic plane}\newterm{ecliptic plane}.   The plane of the moon's orbit is about 5$^\circ$ tilted from the ecliptic plane.

Note that the moon passes through the ecliptic plane only twice every 27.3 days. Imagine that the instant it passed through the ecliptic plane was also the precise instant of a full moon. The sun, the Earth, and the moon would be in a straight line!  The earth would cast a shadow upon the moon --- it would go from a bright full moon to a dark moon until the moon moved back out of the shadow of the earth. This is known as a \index{lunar eclipse}\newterm{lunar eclipse}.
\index{moon!index!lunar}
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.75\textwidth]{lunarEclipse.png}
    \caption{Lunar Eclipses happen when the Earth comes between the moon and the sun, causing the moon to be darkened by the Earth's shadow.}
    \label{fig:example-tides-2}
\end{figure}

The diameter of the moon is a little more than a quarter the diameter of the Earth, so they do not have to be in perfect alignment for the moon to be darkened. Lunar eclipses actually happen once or twice per year.

Now, imagine that the instant the moon passed through the ecliptic plane was also the precise instant of a new moon. The sun,  the moon, and the Earth would be in a straight line!  The moon would cast a shadow upon some part of the Earth.  To a person in that shadow, the sun disappears behind the moon.   This is known as a \index{solar eclipse}\newterm{solar eclipse}.
\index{moon ! eclipse ! solar}
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.75\textwidth]{eclipseShadows.png}
    \caption{Partial and total solar eclipses.}
    \label{fig:eclipseShadows}
\end{figure}



The sun is pretty big, so if the moon blots out just part of it,  we call it a \newterm{partial solar eclipse}, as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:eclipseShadows}. There are a few partial solar eclipses every year. Note that because the moon's shadow is too small to shade the whole Earth, only certain parts of the world will experience any solar eclipses. 


Every 18 months or so, there is a total eclipse of the sun. Once again, only certain parts of the world experience it. You can expect to experience a total eclipse of the sun at your home about once every 375 years.

\section{The Far Side of the Moon}\index{far side of the Moon}

Like the Earth, the Moon spins on its axis. Long ago it spun faster, but
Earth's gravity tugged on the Moon's own tiny tidal bulges and slowed its
spin until it matched its orbit exactly: the Moon turns once on its axis
for every trip around the Earth. Astronomers call this \newterm{tidal
locking}\index{tidal locking}. As a result, we always see the same side of
the Moon.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.7\textwidth]{moonRotate.png}
    \caption{As the Moon moves $30^\circ$ around its orbit, it also turns
    $30^\circ$ on its axis, so the flag keeps pointing at Earth.}
    \label{fig:moonRotate}
\end{figure}
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.7\textwidth]{moonCircleRotate.png}
    \caption{All the way around its orbit, the same face (with the flag)
    points toward Earth.}
    \label{fig:moonCircleRotate}
\end{figure}

No one had seen the far side until the Soviet probe Luna 3 photographed it
in 1959. Some people call it ``the dark side of the Moon,'' but it gets
just as much sunshine as the side that faces us. The name stuck because we
lose radio contact with spacecraft (like the Apollo missions) when they
pass behind the Moon: they ``go dark.''

\section{Tides}

\index{tides}
Twice a day, the ocean rises and falls at the beach. The Moon causes it,
but not in the way most people think. If the Moon simply pulled the ocean
toward it, there would be one high tide a day, on the side facing the Moon.
Instead there are two, one on each side of the planet. Why?

When we say ``the Moon orbits the Earth,'' that is a bit of an
oversimplification. The force of gravity that pulls the Moon toward the
Earth also pulls the Earth toward the Moon. The Earth is about 81 times
heavier than the Moon, so the Moon moves more, but the Moon definitely
moves the Earth. The two swing around their shared center of mass, the
\newterm{barycenter}\index{barycenter}, which is inside the Earth, about
4,700 km from its center.

Here is the key idea. Forget for a moment that the Earth spins once a
day. As the Earth swings around the barycenter each month, it does not
turn; it just shifts around in a small circle, the way you might slide a
coffee mug around in a circle on a table without turning it. Every part of
the Earth, near side and far side alike, moves in an identical circle. So
every kilogram of the Earth needs exactly the same centripetal force, and
that force is supplied by the Moon's gravity at the \emph{center} of the
Earth.

But the Moon's pull is not the same everywhere:
\begin{itemize}
\item On the side facing the Moon, the water is closer to the Moon, so the
      Moon pulls it a little harder than needed. The extra pull draws the
      water toward the Moon.
\item On the far side, the water is farther from the Moon, so the Moon
      pulls it a little \emph{less} than needed. That water falls behind,
      bulging away from the Moon.
\end{itemize}

The water in the oceans bulges a little both toward the Moon and away from
it. The effect is tiny. You lose only about one nine-millionth of your
weight when the Moon is overhead. But the ocean is huge, and a tiny force
on every drop adds up.

The Earth is still rotating under these bulges. If you are at the beach as
your longitude slides into one of them, you say ``Hey, the tide is
rising!'' The peak of a bulge is \newterm{high tide}. Because there is a
bulge on each side of the planet, high tide comes about twice a day.

This is a \index{lunar tide}\newterm{lunar tide}, because it is caused by
the Moon. The Sun makes tides the same way, but it is so far away that its
pull hardly changes from one side of the Earth to the other, so solar tides
are less than half as strong as lunar tides. When the Sun and the Moon line
up and work together, the tides are stronger. This is called a
\index{spring tide}\newterm{spring tide}. Spring tides do not happen in
springtime; they happen near full moons and new moons.

When the Moon and the Sun are at right angles, working against each
other, the tides are weaker. This is called a \index{neap tide}\newterm{neap
tide}. Neap tides happen when you see a half moon in the sky.

\subsection{Computing the Forces}

We are enumerating several forces that shape the water on the planet.  All these forces are pulling on your
body too.  In these exercises, you are going to calculate how each force would affect a 1 kg mass on the surface of the earth.

Here are some numbers you will need:
\begin{itemize}
\item The mass of the Earth: $5.97219 \times 10^{24}$ kg
\item The mass of the sun:  $1.9891 \times 10^{30}$ kg
\item The mass of the moon: $7.347673 \times 10^{22}$ kg
\item Radius of the Earth at the equator: $6,371$ km
\item Average distance from the center of the Earth to the center of the sun:  $149.6 \times 10^6$ km
\item Average distance from the center of the moon to the center of the Earth: $384,467$ km.
\end{itemize}


\begin{Exercise}[title={Life Among the Orbits 1: Earth Gravity}, label=life-orbits1]


If the Earth were still and alone in the universe,  there would still be the force of gravity.  We have said that a kilogram on the surface of the Earth is pulled toward the center of the earth with a force of 9.8 N.   

\textbf{Confirm that the gravity of the Earth pulls a 1kg mass on the surface of the planet with a force of about 9.8 N.}

You will need the formula for gravitation: 

$$F_g = \frac{G m_1 m_2}{r^2}$$

If we measure distance in km and mass in kg, the gravitational constant
$G$ is $6.67430 \times 10^{-17}$. (Usually $G = 6.67430 \times 10^{-11}$ with
distances in meters; a km is $10^3$ m, and $r^2$ is in the denominator, so
we divide by $10^6$.)

\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=life-orbits1]

The Earth and 1 kg on the surface would attract each other with a force of:

$$F_g = \frac{\left( 6.67430 \times 10^{-17} \right) \left(5.97219 \times 10^{24}\right) \left(1\right)}{6,371^2} =  
\frac{3.98597 \times 10^{8}}{4.05896 \times 10^{7}} \approx 9.82 \text{ N}$$

Thus, if the Earth were still and alone in the universe,  the oceans would form a perfect sphere.

\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Life Among the Orbits 2: Earth Centripetal Force}, label=life-orbits2]

What if we add the spinning of the Earth?  The spinning would try to throw the kg into space.  The 
formula for centripetal force is

$$F_c = \frac{m v^2}{r} $$

\textbf{Calculate the centripetal force on a 1 kg mass on the surface of the earth.   It does not fly off into space,  so the force due to gravity must be bigger.  How many times bigger?}

Assume that the mass is on the equator,  thus rotating around the Earth at 465 m/s.

\textbf{Does the centripetal force increase, decrease, or stay the same as you get closer to the north pole?}

\end{Exercise}

\begin{Answer}[ref=life-orbits2]

$$F_c = \frac{(1) (465)^2}{6,371,000} \approx 0.0339 \text{ N}$$

So the spinning of the Earth is trying to throw you into space,  but the force of gravity is about 289 times more powerful.

This centripetal force decreases as you move from the equator to the north pole.   In fact, at the north pole, 
there is no centripetal force.   Thus, the spinning of the Earth makes the oceans an oblate ellipsoid instead of a perfect sphere: the diameter going from pole-to-pole is shorter than a diameter measured at the equator.

You should feel a teensy-tiny bit lighter on your feet at the equator than you do at the north pole: 0.34\% lighter.

\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Life Among the Orbits 3: The Moon's Gravity}, label=life-orbits3]

Now we add the moon's gravitational force to our model.

When the moon is directly overhead,   how strongly will it pull at the 1 kg mass on the equator?

When the moon is directly underfoot,  how strongly will it pull at the 1 kg mass on the equator?

Is that a big difference?

\end{Exercise}

\begin{Answer}[ref=life-orbits3]

Overhead, the moon is $384,467 - 6,371 = 378,096$ km from your 1 kg mass.

$$F_g =  \frac{G m_1 m_2}{r^2} = \frac{\left( 6.67430 \times 10^{-17} \right) \left( 7.347673 \times 10^{22} \right) \left(1\right)}{378,096^2} =  
\frac{4.9040574 \times 10^{6}}{1.42956585216 \times 10^{11}}  = 3.43058 \times 10^{-5} \text{ N}$$

This is a very small force: the force due to Earth's gravity is nearly three hundred thousand times stronger.

Underfoot, the moon is $384,467 + 6,371 = 390,838$

$$F_g =  \frac{G m_1 m_2}{r^2} = \frac{\left( 6.67430 \times 10^{-17} \right) \left( 7.347673 \times 10^{22} \right) \left(1\right)}{390,838^2} =  \frac{4.9040574 \times 10^{6}}{1.52754 \times 10^{11}} =  3.2103 \times 10^{-5} \text{ N}$$

The force due to the moon's gravity is about 7\% stronger when the moon is overhead than when it is underfoot. 

\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Life Among the Orbits 4: The Swing of the Moon}, label=life-orbits4]

Now we add the moon's motion.  The moon and the Earth swing each other around.  This creates a centripetal force.  They both travel in nearly a circle centered at their center of mass.

\textbf{How far is the center of mass of the moon and the Earth from the center of the Earth?}  (You can imagine a see-saw with the center of the Earth on one end and the center of the moon on the other.   Where would the balance point be?)

\textbf{Is the barycenter inside or outside the Earth?}


\end{Exercise}

\begin{Answer}[ref=life-orbits4]

If we let $r$ be the distance (in km) from the center of the Earth to the center of mass,  the distance from
the center of the mass to the center of the moon is $384,467 - r$.

To find the balance point,  multiply each mass by how far it is from the center of mass:

$$\left( 5.97219 \times 10^{24} \right) r = \left( 7.347673 \times 10^{22} \right) \left(384,467 - r\right)$$

Solving for $r$:

$$ r = \frac{4,730.15}{1 + 0.0123} = 4,673 \text{ km} $$

That is less than the Earth's radius of 6,371 km, so the barycenter is
inside the Earth, about 1,700 km below the surface on the side facing the
Moon.

\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Life Among the Orbits 5: The Needed Pull}, label=life-orbits5]
The Earth's center swings around the barycenter once every 27.3 days, in a
circle of radius 4,673 km. Every kilogram of the Earth moves in a circle of
the same size. Using $F_c = m r \omega^2$, find the centripetal force needed
for each kilogram. Compare it with the Moon's pull on 1 kg at the center of
the Earth, $\frac{G m_{moon}}{d^2}$ with $d = 384{,}467$ km.
\end{Exercise}

\begin{Answer}[ref=life-orbits5]
27.3 days is 2,358,720 seconds, so $\omega = \frac{2\pi}{2{,}358{,}720} \approx 2.664 \times 10^{-6}$ rad/s, and
$$F_c = (1)(4{,}673{,}000)(2.664 \times 10^{-6})^2 \approx 3.32 \times 10^{-5} \text{ N}$$
The Moon's pull at the Earth's center is
$$\frac{(6.67430 \times 10^{-17})(7.347673 \times 10^{22})}{384{,}467^2} \approx 3.32 \times 10^{-5} \text{ N}$$
They match, as they must: the Moon's pull on the Earth is exactly what
swings the Earth around the barycenter.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Life Among the Orbits 6: The Tidal Force}, label=life-orbits6]
Using your answers to exercises 3 and 5, find the leftover force on 1 kg at
the point closest to the Moon and at the point farthest from it: the
difference between the Moon's actual pull there and the $3.318 \times
10^{-5}$ N needed. Which way does each point?
\end{Exercise}

\begin{Answer}[ref=life-orbits6]
Closest to the Moon:
$$3.4305 \times 10^{-5} - 3.3177 \times 10^{-5} \approx 1.13 \times 10^{-6} \text{ N, toward the Moon}$$
Farthest from the Moon:
$$3.2104 \times 10^{-5} - 3.3177 \times 10^{-5} \approx -1.07 \times 10^{-6} \text{ N, away from the Moon}$$
The two leftover forces are nearly equal and point in opposite directions:
one pulls the near-side water toward the Moon, the other lets the far-side
water bulge away from it. That is why there are two nearly equal high
tides every day. Both are tiny: Earth's own gravity, 9.82 N, is about 9
million times larger.
\end{Answer}

\subsection{Solar Tidal Forces}

The Sun has a much larger gravitational effect on the Earth than the Moon does:
\begin{itemize}
\item The Sun pulls on a 1 kg mass with a force of about 0.00593 N.
\item The Moon pulls on a 1 kg mass with a force of about 0.0000332 N.
\end{itemize}

So why are lunar tides about twice as strong as solar tides? Tides do not
come from the size of the pull; they come from how much the pull
\emph{changes} from one side of the Earth to the other. The Earth is 1/30
of the way to the Moon, so the Moon's pull changes by several percent
across the planet. The Earth is only 1/12,000 of the way to the Sun, so
the Sun's pull changes far less. Working it out, the Sun's tidal force is
about 0.46 of the Moon's.

\begin{Exercise}[title={The Year 1900}, label=tide7]
Was $1900$ a leap year?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=tide7]
No. It is divisible by $100$ but not by $400$.
\end{Answer}

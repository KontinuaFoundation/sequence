\chapter{Boosting}

After a practice test, a good student does not reread the whole textbook. They look at the problems they
missed and study those. Then they take another practice test, look at what they missed \emph{this} time, and
repeat.

\newterm{Boosting}\index{boosting} trains a sequence of models the same way. Each new model concentrates on the
examples the models before it got wrong, and the final prediction combines them all. Random forests build many
trees independently and let them vote; boosting builds them one after another, each correcting the last.

\section{Weak Learners}\index{weak learner}
Boosting works with \newterm{weak learners}: models that are only a little better than guessing. The usual choice
is a \newterm{decision stump}\index{decision stump}, a decision tree with a single question, like ``body mass
$> 3712.5$ g?'' A stump alone is a poor classifier. The surprise, proved in the 1990s, is that a weighted vote of
many weak learners can be as strong as you like.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{4.5cm} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{4.5cm}}
 & \textbf{Random forest} & \textbf{Boosting} \\
\hline
Trees built & independently & one after another \\
Each tree & deep, overfits on its own & small, underfits on its own \\
Main effect & averages away variance & chips away at bias \\
Too many trees & harmless & can overfit \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\section{AdaBoost}\index{AdaBoost}
\newterm{AdaBoost} (adaptive boosting), invented by Yoav Freund and Robert Schapire in 1995, works with labels
$y_i \in \{-1, +1\}$. It keeps a weight $w_i$ for each training point.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Start with equal weights, $w_i = \frac{1}{n}$.
\item For $t = 1, \ldots, T$: train a weak learner $h_t$ on the weighted data, compute its weighted error
$\varepsilon_t = \sum_{i:\, h_t(x_i) \ne y_i} w_i$, and give it a vote of strength
$$\alpha_t = \frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1 - \varepsilon_t}{\varepsilon_t}$$
\item Increase the weights of the misclassified points and decrease the others,
$$w_i \leftarrow w_i e^{-\alpha_t y_i h_t(x_i)}$$
then divide by the total so the weights sum to 1.
\end{enumerate}
The final classifier is the sign of the weighted vote, $H(x) = \operatorname{sign}\left(\sum_t \alpha_t h_t(x)\right)$.

The exponent does the bookkeeping. When $h_t$ is right, $y_i h_t(x_i) = +1$ and the weight shrinks by the factor
$e^{-\alpha_t}$; when it is wrong, $y_i h_t(x_i) = -1$ and the weight grows by $e^{\alpha_t}$. A learner with a small
error gets a large $\alpha_t$, a strong vote.

\textbf{Example}: Four points have weights $\frac{1}{4}$ each. A weak learner misclassifies only the fourth point.
Find $\alpha_1$ and the new weights.

\textbf{Solution}: $\varepsilon_1 = \frac{1}{4}$, so
$$\alpha_1 = \frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{3/4}{1/4} = \frac{1}{2}\ln 3 \approx 0.549$$
The three correct points get weight $\frac{1}{4} e^{-0.549} \approx 0.144$ before normalizing, and the misclassified
point gets $\frac{1}{4} e^{0.549} \approx 0.433$. The total is $3(0.144) + 0.433 = 0.866$, so after normalizing, the
misclassified point has weight $0.5$ and each correct point has weight $\frac{1}{6} \approx 0.167$. Half of the
next learner's attention goes to the one point that was missed.

\section{AdaBoost on Penguins}
Male and female penguins are hard to tell apart, especially across three species of different sizes. Here is
\filename{adaboost.py}, which boosts stumps to predict a penguin's sex from its four measurements, training on
233 penguins and testing on 100:

\begin{minted}{python}
import numpy as np
from penguins import load_penguins

data = load_penguins()
y = np.where(data["sex"] == "male", 1, -1)             # labels are +1 and -1
features = ["bill_length_mm", "bill_depth_mm", "flipper_length_mm", "body_mass_g"]
X = np.column_stack([data[f] for f in features])
order = np.random.default_rng(1).permutation(len(y))
train, test = order[:233], order[233:]

def best_stump(X, y, w):
    """The one-question rule with the smallest weighted error."""
    best = (np.inf, None, None, None)                 # (error, feature, threshold, sign)
    for j in range(X.shape[1]):
        values = np.unique(X[:, j])
        for t in (values[:-1] + values[1:]) / 2:
            guess = np.where(X[:, j] > t, 1, -1)
            for sign in (1, -1):                      # "big means male" or the reverse
                error = np.sum(w[sign * guess != y])
                if error < best[0]:
                    best = (error, j, t, sign)
    return best

def stump_predict(stump, X):
    _, j, t, sign = stump
    return sign * np.where(X[:, j] > t, 1, -1)

w = np.full(len(train), 1 / len(train))
score_train, score_test = np.zeros(len(train)), np.zeros(len(test))
for t in range(1, 201):
    stump = best_stump(X[train], y[train], w)
    error = stump[0]
    alpha = 0.5 * np.log((1 - error) / error)
    h = stump_predict(stump, X[train])
    w = w * np.exp(-alpha * y[train] * h)
    w = w / w.sum()
    score_train += alpha * h
    score_test += alpha * stump_predict(stump, X[test])
    if t <= 3:
        print(f"round {t}: {features[stump[1]]} > {stump[2]:.2f} means "
              f"{'male' if stump[3] == 1 else 'female'}; error {error:.3f}, alpha {alpha:.3f}")
    if t in (1, 10, 50, 200):
        acc_train = np.mean(np.sign(score_train) == y[train])
        acc_test = np.mean(np.sign(score_test) == y[test])
        print(f"{t:3d} stumps: training {acc_train:.3f}, test {acc_test:.3f}")
\end{minted}

\begin{verbatim}
round 1: body_mass_g > 3712.50 means male; error 0.270, alpha 0.496
  1 stumps: training 0.730, test 0.710
round 2: bill_depth_mm > 17.95 means male; error 0.236, alpha 0.588
round 3: body_mass_g > 5025.00 means male; error 0.258, alpha 0.529
 10 stumps: training 0.936, test 0.880
 50 stumps: training 0.970, test 0.890
200 stumps: training 1.000, test 0.860
\end{verbatim}

The first stump, ``heavier than 3712.5 g means male,'' is right 73\% of the time; its mistakes are mostly
heavy Gentoo females and light Adelie males. The weights shift toward those penguins, and the second stump, on
bill depth, is chosen because it handles them better. The third stump asks about mass again, but with a much
higher threshold, aimed at the Gentoos. The running totals \verb|score_train| and \verb|score_test| hold
$\sum_t \alpha_t h_t(x)$ for every penguin, so checking accuracy after any round is just a matter of taking signs.

Ten stumps already get 88\% of the test penguins right. But look at the end: with 200 stumps, the training
accuracy is a perfect 100\%, and the test accuracy has slipped to 86\%. Boosting kept focusing harder and harder
on the few training penguins it could not get right, and eventually it memorized them.

The test curve in Figure~\ref{fig:adaboost-curve} peaks early and then sags. You might be tempted to pick the
number of stumps at its peak, but that would be choosing a setting by looking at the test set, which turns the
test set into a validation set and its score into an overestimate. Choose the number of rounds with a separate
validation set or cross-validation.

\section{Gradient Boosting}\index{gradient boosted trees}
AdaBoost reweights the points. \newterm{Gradient boosting} takes a different route that works for regression,
too: each new tree is trained to predict the \emph{errors} of the ensemble so far, and its predictions are added
on, scaled down by a \newterm{learning rate}.

\textbf{Example}: Three houses sell for 3, 5, and 10 (in hundreds of thousands of dollars). Start by predicting
the mean, 6, for every house. The residuals are $-3$, $-1$, and $4$. Suppose the first tree learns these residuals
exactly. With a learning rate of $0.5$, each prediction moves halfway toward its target: $6 + 0.5(-3) = 4.5$,
$6 + 0.5(-1) = 5.5$, and $6 + 0.5(4) = 8$. The new residuals are $-1.5$, $-0.5$, and $2$, half as big. The next tree
fits these, and so on.

Why ``gradient''? For squared error, $L = \frac{1}{2}(y - \hat{y})^2$, the negative gradient with respect to the
prediction, $-\partial L / \partial \hat{y} = y - \hat{y}$, is exactly the residual. So fitting each tree to the
residuals is gradient descent, as in the Linear Regression and Gradient Descent chapter, except that each step
adds a whole tree instead of nudging a few parameters. Other losses (such as log loss, for classification) give
other ``residuals,'' and the same machinery works.

Figure~\ref{fig:boost-steps} shows gradient boosting with stumps on a single feature, flipper length. Each
stump adds one small step, and the steps pile up into a staircase that follows the data.

\caption{Gradient boosting with a learning rate of 0.1, predicting body mass from flipper length.}

Here is \filename{gradient\_boost.py}, which predicts body mass from all the features we used for multiple
regression:

\begin{minted}{python}
import numpy as np
from penguins import load_penguins

data = load_penguins()
y = data["body_mass_g"]
data["male"] = (data["sex"] == "male").astype(float)
data["gentoo"] = (data["species"] == "Gentoo").astype(float)
data["chinstrap"] = (data["species"] == "Chinstrap").astype(float)
features = ["flipper_length_mm", "bill_length_mm", "bill_depth_mm",
            "male", "gentoo", "chinstrap"]
X = np.column_stack([data[f] for f in features])
order = np.random.default_rng(1).permutation(len(y))
train, test = order[:233], order[233:]

def fit_stump(X, r):
    """The one-question rule that best predicts the residuals r (least squares)."""
    best = (np.inf, None, None, None, None)
    for j in range(X.shape[1]):
        values = np.unique(X[:, j])
        for t in (values[:-1] + values[1:]) / 2:
            left = X[:, j] <= t
            mean_left, mean_right = r[left].mean(), r[~left].mean()
            sse = np.sum((r[left] - mean_left)**2) + np.sum((r[~left] - mean_right)**2)
            if sse < best[0]:
                best = (sse, j, t, mean_left, mean_right)
    return best

def stump_predict(stump, X):
    _, j, t, mean_left, mean_right = stump
    return np.where(X[:, j] <= t, mean_left, mean_right)

def rmse(pred, rows):
    return np.sqrt(np.mean((y[rows] - pred)**2))

rate = 0.1
pred_train = np.full(len(train), y[train].mean())    # start with the average
pred_test = np.full(len(test), y[train].mean())
for t in range(1, 501):
    residuals = y[train] - pred_train
    stump = fit_stump(X[train], residuals)
    pred_train += rate * stump_predict(stump, X[train])
    pred_test += rate * stump_predict(stump, X[test])
    if t in (1, 10, 50, 100, 200, 500):
        print(f"{t:3d} stumps: training RMSE {rmse(pred_train, train):.0f}, "
              f"test RMSE {rmse(pred_test, test):.0f}")
\end{minted}

\begin{verbatim}
  1 stumps: training RMSE 762, test RMSE 727
 10 stumps: training RMSE 494, test RMSE 482
 50 stumps: training RMSE 294, test RMSE 294
100 stumps: training RMSE 278, test RMSE 289
200 stumps: training RMSE 265, test RMSE 290
500 stumps: training RMSE 249, test RMSE 303
\end{verbatim}

A regression stump predicts the average residual on each side of its threshold, and it chooses the threshold
that makes the squared error smallest, just as a classification stump minimizes impurity.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{gb_curve.png}
\caption{Gradient boosting RMSE as stumps are added. The dashed line is least squares on the same six
features.}
\label{fig:gb-curve}
\end{figure}

The pattern is the same as for AdaBoost: the training error keeps falling, while the test error bottoms out
around 100 stumps and then creeps back up. At its best, gradient boosting gets a test RMSE of 289 g. That is
honest but humbling: least squares on the same six features gets 278 g. Penguin mass is close to a linear
function of these features, and a linear model is hard to beat at being linear. Boosting earns its reputation
on problems with curved relationships and interactions between features, where a straight line cannot follow
the data. Boosted trees win a large share of machine-learning competitions on tables of data.

\section{Cautions}
\begin{itemize}
\item Boosting can overfit if the number of rounds is too large. Choose it with validation data.
\item A small learning rate slows the fit and usually generalizes better, but needs more rounds.
\item Because each model chases the hardest examples, mislabeled data (a male recorded as female) can pull boosting
off course.
\end{itemize}

\begin{Exercise}[label=bst1, title=Compute alpha]
A weak learner has weighted error $\varepsilon = 0.1$. What is $\alpha$?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=bst1]
$\alpha = \frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{0.9}{0.1} = \frac{1}{2}\ln 9 \approx 1.099$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=bst2, title=Which points gain weight?]
After a round of AdaBoost, which points have their weights increased?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=bst2]
The points that $h_t$ misclassified. For those points, $y_i h_t(x_i) = -1$, so
$e^{-\alpha_t y_i h_t(x_i)} = e^{\alpha_t} > 1$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=bst3, title=A coin-flip learner]
A weak learner has weighted error $\varepsilon = 0.5$. What is $\alpha$, and what does that mean?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=bst3]
$\alpha = \frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{0.5}{0.5} = \frac{1}{2}\ln 1 = 0$. A learner that does no better than flipping a coin
gets no vote in the final classifier.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=bst4, title={Half the weight}, space=3cm]
Show that after any AdaBoost round, the points the learner got wrong carry exactly half of the total weight.
(Hint: before normalizing, the wrong points have total weight $\varepsilon e^{\alpha}$ and the right ones have
$(1 - \varepsilon)e^{-\alpha}$.)
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=bst4]
With $\alpha = \frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1 - \varepsilon}{\varepsilon}$, we have $e^{\alpha} =
\sqrt{\frac{1 - \varepsilon}{\varepsilon}}$. So the wrong points have $\varepsilon e^{\alpha} =
\sqrt{\varepsilon(1 - \varepsilon)}$, and the right points have $(1 - \varepsilon)e^{-\alpha} =
\sqrt{\varepsilon(1 - \varepsilon)}$, the same amount. After normalizing, each group has weight $\frac{1}{2}$. That is
why the worked example ended with the missed point at exactly 0.5: the last learner now looks no better than a
coin flip on the new weights, so the next learner must find something new.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=bst5, title={A second round}, space=3cm]
Continue the house example for one more round: the second tree learns the residuals $-1.5$, $-0.5$, and $2$
exactly, and the learning rate is still 0.5. What are the new predictions and residuals?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=bst5]
The predictions become $4.5 - 0.75 = 3.75$, $5.5 - 0.25 = 5.25$, and $8 + 1 = 9$. The residuals are $-0.75$, $-0.25$,
and $1$, half as big again. Each round halves the error, so the predictions approach the true prices.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=bst6, title={Learning rate}]
In \filename{gradient\_boost.py}, change the learning rate to 1.0 and then to 0.01, keeping 500 rounds. What happens
to the training and test RMSE?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=bst6]
With a rate of 1.0, the training RMSE drops very fast (to 180 g after 500 stumps), but the test RMSE is best,
about 298 g, around 20 stumps, and then climbs to 355 g as the model overfits. With 0.01, progress is slow: after
500 stumps, both RMSEs are still falling (296 g training, 295 g test), and the model has not finished learning. A smaller rate needs more rounds, but it gives a gentler
curve that is easier to stop at the right place.
\end{Answer}

\chapter{Introduction to Waves}

Have you ever done ``the wave'' at a stadium? Each fan stands up and sits
down, never leaving their seat, yet a ripple races all the way around the
stadium. That is the essence of every wave in nature. Ripples on a pond,
the sound from a speaker, the light from the Sun, the radio signal reaching
your phone, the shaking of an earthquake: all of them are waves, and all of
them share the same basic properties. Learn those properties once, and you
can understand them all.

\begin{quote}
A \index{wave}\textbf{wave} is a disturbance that carries energy from one
place to another without carrying matter along with it.
\end{quote}

When a ripple crosses a pond, the water does not travel across the pond. A
floating leaf just bobs up and down as the ripple passes. The water
\emph{oscillates} around its resting position while the wave carries energy
outward, just like the fans in the stadium.

\section{Basic Properties of Waves}

The simplest waves have the shape of the sine curves you studied in the
trigonometry chapters. Five terms describe them.

\subsection*{Equilibrium}
The \index{equilibrium}\newterm{equilibrium} position is where the medium
sits when there is no wave: the flat surface of the pond before the pebble
lands, or a rope lying still.

\subsection*{Amplitude}
The \index{amplitude}\newterm{amplitude} is the largest distance the medium
moves away from equilibrium. The highest points of the wave are
\newterm{crests} (or peaks), and the lowest points are \newterm{troughs}
(or valleys). In a simple wave, the crests are as far above equilibrium as
the troughs are below it.

For a water wave, the amplitude is the height of a crest above the resting
water level. A bigger amplitude means more energy: louder sound, brighter
light, a more violent earthquake.

\subsection*{Wavelength}
The \index{wavelength}\newterm{wavelength}, written $\lambda$ (the Greek
letter lambda), is the distance from one point on the wave to the matching
point on the next wave, such as crest to crest. It is measured in meters.

\subsection*{Frequency}
The \index{frequency}\newterm{frequency} $f$ is the number of complete
waves that pass a point each second. It is measured in hertz (Hz):
$1 \text{ Hz} = 1 \text{ wave per second}$. For sound, higher frequency
means higher pitch. For light, frequency determines color.

\subsection*{Period}
The \index{period}\newterm{period} $T$ is the time for one complete
oscillation. If 4 waves pass each second, each one takes $\frac{1}{4}$ of
a second. In general,
$$T = \frac{1}{f}$$

\subsection*{Wave Speed}
In one period, the wave moves forward exactly one wavelength. Since speed
is distance divided by time,
$$v = \frac{\lambda}{T} = f\lambda$$
The speed depends on the medium. Sound travels about 343 m/s in air and
about 1,480 m/s in water. Light travels 300,000 km/s through empty space.

\textbf{Worked example.} The note A above middle C has a frequency of
440 Hz. Its wavelength in air is 0.78 m. How fast does it travel, and what
is its period?

\textbf{Solution.} $v = f\lambda = (440)(0.78) \approx 343$ m/s. The period
is $T = \frac{1}{440} \approx 0.00227$ s, about 2.3 milliseconds.

\begin{Exercise}[title={Label the wave}, label=wave_label]
A wave on a rope is drawn on a grid. The rope's resting position is
$y = 0$. Its crests reach $y = 15$ cm and its troughs reach $y = -15$ cm.
Crests are 80 cm apart, and 5 crests pass a fixed point every 2 seconds.
Find the amplitude, wavelength, frequency, period, and speed.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=wave_label]
Amplitude: 15 cm (not 30; it is measured from equilibrium). Wavelength:
80 cm $= 0.8$ m. Frequency: $\frac{5}{2} = 2.5$ Hz. Period:
$\frac{1}{2.5} = 0.4$ s. Speed: $(2.5)(0.8) = 2$ m/s.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Wavelength of a Low Note}, label=wave1]
A sound wave has frequency $20$ Hz (about the lowest pitch humans can hear)
and travels at $343$ m/s. What is its wavelength?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=wave1]
$\lambda = \frac{v}{f} = \frac{343}{20} = 17.15$ m. That is longer than a
school bus, which is why bass notes travel through walls so easily.
\end{Answer}

\section{Two Main Types of Waves}

Waves come in two kinds, depending on which way the medium moves as the wave
passes.

\subsection*{Transverse Waves}
In a \index{transverse wave}\newterm{transverse} wave, the medium moves
\emph{perpendicular} to the direction the wave travels. Flick one end of a
rope tied to a wall: the rope moves up and down, while the wave runs along
it toward the wall. The stadium wave is transverse, too. Light and other
electromagnetic waves are transverse.

\subsection*{Longitudinal Waves}
In a \index{longitudinal wave}\newterm{longitudinal} wave, the medium moves
back and forth \emph{parallel} to the direction the wave travels. Sound is
the most important example. A speaker cone pushes on the air next to it,
that air pushes on the air next to it, and so on, until the push reaches
your ear. Each bit of air only jiggles back and forth.

Your digital resources include a video of both kinds of waves on a slinky.
Push the end of a stretched slinky in and out, and you get a longitudinal
wave; shake it side to side, and you get a transverse one.

\section{Compression and Rarefaction}

A longitudinal wave has no crests or troughs. Instead, it has regions where
the medium is squeezed together, called \newterm{compressions}
\index{compression}, and regions where it is spread apart, called
\newterm{rarefactions}\index{rarefaction}. In a sound wave, the air
pressure is slightly higher in the compressions and slightly lower in the
rarefactions.

The distance from one compression to the next is the wavelength. If you
graph the air pressure along the wave, you get a sine curve, so all the
properties from the last section still apply.

\section{Creating a Wave in Python}

A traveling wave is a sine curve that slides along as time passes. With
amplitude $A$, wavelength $\lambda$, and frequency $f$, the height of the
medium at position $x$ and time $t$ is
$$y = A \sin\left(2\pi\left(\frac{x}{\lambda} - f t\right)\right)$$
Let us draw the wave at a few moments to watch it move. Create
\filename{wave.py}:

\begin{minted}{python}
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

A = 0.15            # amplitude (m)
wavelength = 0.8    # m
f = 2.5             # Hz

x = np.linspace(0, 2.4, 300)
for t in [0, 0.1, 0.2]:
    y = A * np.sin(2 * np.pi * (x / wavelength - f * t))
    plt.plot(x, y, label=f"t = {t} s")

plt.axhline(0, color="gray", linewidth=0.8)
plt.xlabel("position (m)")
plt.ylabel("displacement (m)")
plt.legend()
plt.show()
\end{minted}

These are the numbers from the rope exercise. Each curve is the same sine
wave, shifted to the right. In 0.1 s, a wave moving at 2 m/s should move
0.2 m, and that is exactly how far the crests shift between curves.

\begin{Exercise}[title={Faster waves}, label=wave_python]
Change \mintinline{python}{f} to 5 and run the program again, leaving the
wavelength alone. How far do the crests move between curves now? What
happened to the wave speed?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=wave_python]
The speed is now $(5)(0.8) = 4$ m/s, twice as fast, so the crests move
0.4 m between curves (each 0.1 s apart), twice as far as before.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Radio waves}, label=wave_radio]
An FM radio station broadcasts at 100 MHz (100 million hertz). Radio waves
travel at the speed of light, $3 \times 10^8$ m/s. What is the wavelength?
What is the period?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=wave_radio]
$\lambda = \frac{3 \times 10^8}{1 \times 10^8} = 3$ m.
$T = \frac{1}{10^8} = 10^{-8}$ s, or 10 nanoseconds.
\end{Answer}

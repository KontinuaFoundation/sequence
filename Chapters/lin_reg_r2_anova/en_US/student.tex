\chapter{Evaluating the Fit of a Linear Regression Model}

\section{Residuals}\index{residual!plot}
The residual for observation $i$ is $e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$, the observed value
minus the predicted value. A \newterm{residual plot} graphs $e_i$ against $\hat{y}_i$
or against $x_i$. If the line fits well, the residuals scatter randomly around
zero with no clear pattern. A curve in the residual plot means a straight line is the wrong model.

\section{Sums of Squares}
Three sums of squares measure the variation in $y$:
$$SS_{tot} = \sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2, \qquad SS_{res} = \sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2, \qquad
SS_{reg} = \sum (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})^2$$
Here $SS_{tot}$ is the total variation, $SS_{res}$ is the variation the line does not
explain, and $SS_{reg}$ is the variation the line explains.

\section{Coefficient of Determination}\index{R-squared}
The coefficient of determination is
$$R^2 = 1 - \frac{SS_{res}}{SS_{tot}}$$
It is the proportion of the variation in $y$ that the line explains. If the
line fits perfectly, $SS_{res} = 0$ and $R^2 = 1$. If the line does no better than
predicting $\bar{y}$ for every point, $SS_{res} = SS_{tot}$ and $R^2 = 0$.

For a simple linear regression, $R^2$ is the square of the correlation $r$ between
$x$ and $y$.

\subsection{Example}
For the quiz data in the simple linear regression chapter, $SS_{res} = 0.107$ and
$SS_{tot} = 39.708$. So
$$R^2 = 1 - \frac{0.107}{39.708} \approx 0.997$$
About 99.7\% of the variation in quiz scores is explained by hours of study.
This is a very high value. In real data, $R^2$ is often much lower.

\section{Root Mean Squared Error}\index{RMSE}\index{root mean squared error}
The root mean squared error (RMSE) measures the typical size of a prediction error,
in the units of $y$:
$$RMSE = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2} = \sqrt{\frac{SS_{res}}{n}}$$
For the quiz data, $RMSE = \sqrt{0.107/5} \approx 0.146$ points. Some textbooks divide by
$n - 2$ instead of $n$ when estimating error variance in regression. This chapter
uses $n$ so that RMSE is the root of the average squared error.

\section{Limits of These Measures}
A high $R^2$ does not mean the model is correct. It can be high for a curved pattern
fit with a line, or when a single extreme point drives the fit. Always look at the
residual plot too. Also, $R^2$ never decreases when you add more predictors, so
it cannot be used alone to compare models with different numbers of predictors.

\begin{Exercise}[label=lr2_1, title=Compute R-squared]
A regression has $SS_{res} = 25$ and $SS_{tot} = 100$. What is $R^2$, and what does it mean?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=lr2_1]
$R^2 = 1 - \frac{25}{100} = 0.75$. The line explains 75\% of the variation in $y$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=lr2_2, title=A model that predicts the mean]
Explain why $R^2 = 0$ for a model that predicts $\bar{y}$ for every observation.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=lr2_2]
Such a model has $\hat{y}_i = \bar{y}$, so $SS_{res} = \sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2 = SS_{tot}$.
Then $R^2 = 1 - \frac{SS_{tot}}{SS_{tot}} = 0$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=lr2_3, title=Compare two residual plots]
A residual plot shows residuals that are large and positive for small $\hat{y}$,
negative in the middle, and large and positive again for large $\hat{y}$. What does
this suggest about the model?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=lr2_3]
The pattern is curved, not random. A straight line does not describe the
relationship, and a model with a curved term, or a transformation of the
variables, would be a better choice.
\end{Answer}

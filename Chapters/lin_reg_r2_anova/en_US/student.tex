\chapter{Evaluating the Fit of a Linear Regression Model}\label{chap:lin_reg_r2_anova}

Give a regression any two columns of numbers, say shoe size and math grade, and it will hand you back a line.
It never refuses. So the real question is not ``What is the line?'' but ``Is the line any good?'' The last
chapter answered one version of that question with a $t$-test for the slope. This chapter adds the other tools
statisticians use to grade a fit: residual plots, $R^2$, RMSE, and the ANOVA table with its $F$-test.

\section{Look at the Residuals First}\index{residual!plot}
The residual for observation $i$ is $e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$. A \newterm{residual plot} graphs $e_i$
against $x_i$ (or against $\hat{y}_i$). If the line is the right shape, the residuals scatter randomly
around zero. If they form a pattern, the model has missed something.

Here is a driver's-education example. Figure~\ref{fig:stopping} shows the stopping distance of a car at
different speeds. (The data is simulated from the physics of braking, plus some noise.) The line looks
fine, and as you will see, it explains 97\% of the variation. But the residuals make a clear U: the line
overestimates in the middle and underestimates at both ends. Braking distance grows with the
\emph{square} of the speed, so a straight line is the wrong model, however good its score.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{stopping.png}
\caption{Left: stopping distance against speed, with the least squares line. Right: the residuals form
a U, so the relationship is curved.}
\label{fig:stopping}
\end{figure}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{7cm}}
\textbf{Residual pattern} & \textbf{What it suggests} \\
\hline
random scatter around 0 & the line is a reasonable model \\
a curve (U or upside-down U) & the relationship is not linear; try $x^2$ or a transformation \\
a fan (spread grows with $x$) & errors are bigger for big $x$; predictions there are less precise \\
groups above and below 0 & a missing category, such as grade level for students or species for animals \\
one point far from the rest & an outlier that may be pulling the line \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\section{Splitting Up the Variation}
Why does a $y$ value differ from the average $\bar{y}$? Part of the reason is that its $x$ differs from
average, which the line accounts for. The rest is the residual. For each point,
$$\underbrace{y_i - \bar{y}}_{\text{total}} = \underbrace{\hat{y}_i - \bar{y}}_{\text{explained}} +
\underbrace{y_i - \hat{y}_i}_{\text{residual}}$$

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{ss_split.png}
\caption{For the point $(3, 8)$, the distance above the mean ($4$) splits into the part the line explains
($3.3$) and the residual ($0.7$).}
\label{fig:ss-split}
\end{figure}

Square these pieces and add them over all the points to get three \newterm{sums of squares}:
$$SS_{tot} = \sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2, \qquad SS_{reg} = \sum (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})^2, \qquad
SS_{res} = \sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$$
For a least squares fit with an intercept, they add up just like the pieces do:
$$SS_{tot} = SS_{reg} + SS_{res}$$
(It is not obvious that the squares should add up, too. It works because the least squares residuals
are uncorrelated with the predictions: in the language of vectors, the residual vector is perpendicular to the
vector of predictions, and this is the Pythagorean theorem in $n$ dimensions.)

\textbf{Example}: For the points $(0,1), (1,3), (2,4), (3,8)$ with line $\hat{y} = 0.7 + 2.2x$, find the three
sums of squares.

\textbf{Solution}: $\bar{y} = 4$. Work row by row:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c c c | c c c}
$x$ & $y$ & $\hat{y}$ & $(y - \bar{y})^2$ & $(\hat{y} - \bar{y})^2$ & $(y - \hat{y})^2$ \\
\hline
0 & 1 & 0.7 & 9 & 10.89 & 0.09 \\
1 & 3 & 2.9 & 1 & 1.21 & 0.01 \\
2 & 4 & 5.1 & 0 & 1.21 & 1.21 \\
3 & 8 & 7.3 & 16 & 10.89 & 0.49 \\
\hline
 & & \textbf{Sum} & $SS_{tot} = 26$ & $SS_{reg} = 24.2$ & $SS_{res} = 1.8$ \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

And indeed $24.2 + 1.8 = 26$.

\section{The Coefficient of Determination}\index{R-squared}\index{coefficient of determination}
The fraction of the total variation that the line explains is the \newterm{coefficient of determination}:
$$R^2 = \frac{SS_{reg}}{SS_{tot}} = 1 - \frac{SS_{res}}{SS_{tot}}$$

\begin{itemize}
\item A perfect fit has $SS_{res} = 0$, so $R^2 = 1$.
\item The laziest possible model ignores $x$ and always predicts $\bar{y}$. It has $SS_{res} = SS_{tot}$, so
$R^2 = 0$. You can read $R^2$ as ``how much better than always guessing the average, on a scale from 0 to 1.''
\item For simple linear regression, $R^2 = r^2$, the square of the correlation between $x$ and $y$, as in the
Regression Line chapter.
\end{itemize}

In the example above, $R^2 = 24.2 / 26 \approx 0.931$. Here is a second example. Five students studied for
$x = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5$ hours and scored $y = 2.1, 3.9, 6.2, 7.8, 10.1$ on a 10-point quiz. The least squares line is
$\hat{y} = 0.05 + 1.99x$, with $SS_{res} = 0.107$ and $SS_{tot} = 39.708$, so
$$R^2 = 1 - \frac{0.107}{39.708} \approx 0.997$$
Hours of study explain 99.7\% of the variation in those five scores. Real data is rarely that tidy.

\section{Root Mean Squared Error}\index{root mean squared error}
$R^2$ has no units, which makes it easy to compare across problems but hard to feel. The \newterm{root mean
squared error} (RMSE) is in the units of $y$:
$$\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{SS_{res}}{n}}$$
For the quiz data, $\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{0.107/5} \approx 0.146$ points: a typical prediction is off by about
a seventh of a point. (The regression output in the last two chapters reports a close cousin,
$s = \sqrt{SS_{res}/(n - 2)}$, which divides by the degrees of freedom to estimate the spread of the errors in
the population. RMSE divides by $n$, so it is exactly the root of the average squared error. For large $n$, the
two are nearly equal.)

The two measures answer different questions. $R^2$ asks, ``What fraction of the variation did we explain?''
RMSE asks, ``How far off is a typical prediction?'' Report both.

\section{The ANOVA Table and the \texorpdfstring{$F$}{F}-Test}\index{ANOVA!regression}\index{F-test}
Could the pattern be a fluke? Even if $y$ had nothing to do with $x$, random noise would give the line a
slope that is not exactly zero and an $R^2$ that is not exactly zero. The \newterm{analysis of variance}
(ANOVA) table tests whether the line explains more than noise would.

Each sum of squares is divided by its \newterm{degrees of freedom} to get a \newterm{mean square}, and the
ratio of the two mean squares is the $F$ statistic:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|c|c|c|c}
Source & SS & df & MS & $F$ \\
\hline
Regression & $SS_{reg}$ & $1$ & $MS_{reg} = SS_{reg}/1$ & $MS_{reg}/MS_{res}$ \\
Residual & $SS_{res}$ & $n - 2$ & $MS_{res} = SS_{res}/(n-2)$ & \\
Total & $SS_{tot}$ & $n - 1$ & & \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The residual degrees of freedom are $n - 2$ because the line used up two numbers, $b_0$ and $b_1$. With
$p$ features, the regression row gets $p$ degrees of freedom and the residual row gets $n - p - 1$.

If $x$ is useless, $F$ tends to be around 1. If the line explains a lot, $F$ is large. How large is large
enough? If $x$ really were useless, $F$ would follow an \newterm{$F$ distribution} with 1 and $n - 2$ degrees
of freedom. The $p$-value is the chance that such a distribution produces a value at least as big as ours.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{f_dist.png}
\caption{The $F$ distribution with 1 and 3 degrees of freedom. If $x$ were useless, $F$ would land above
10.13 only 5\% of the time.}
\label{fig:f-dist}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Example}: For the quiz data, $SS_{reg} = 39.601$, $SS_{res} = 0.107$, and $n = 5$. Find $F$.

\textbf{Solution}: $$F = \frac{39.601/1}{0.107/3} \approx 1110$$
That is far beyond 10.13, off the right edge of Figure~\ref{fig:f-dist}. The $p$-value is about
$0.00006$: strong evidence that the slope is not zero.

\textbf{The $F$-test and the $t$-test.} You already have a test for ``is the slope zero?'': the $t$-test from the
last chapter. With one predictor, the two tests always agree, because $F = t^2$ exactly. Take the sleep study,
where 8 teenagers' screen time and sleep gave $t = -4.01$ with a two-sided $p$-value of 0.007. Its sums of squares
are $SS_{tot} = 1.567$, $SS_{reg} = 1.142$, and $SS_{res} = 0.425$, so
$$R^2 = \frac{1.142}{1.567} \approx 0.729, \qquad F = \frac{1.142/1}{0.425/6} \approx 16.1 \approx (-4.01)^2$$
and the $F$-test's $p$-value is 0.007, the same as the two-sided $t$-test. Why have both? The $t$-test can be
one-sided and gives a confidence interval for the slope. The $F$-test extends to models with several
predictors, where it asks whether \emph{any} of them helps.

\section{Grading a Line for Penguins}\index{Palmer penguins}
Now a bigger data set. From 2007 to 2009, researchers at Palmer Station, Antarctica, measured 344 penguins of three
species; 333 have every measurement recorded. The data is free for anyone to use. Put \filename{penguins.csv} and
\filename{penguins.py} in a folder; the function \verb|load_penguins()| in \filename{penguins.py} returns a
dictionary of NumPy arrays, one per column, so \verb|data["body_mass_g"]| is an array of 333 masses. (The
machine-learning chapters later in the series use this data set throughout.)

Can a penguin's flipper length predict its body mass? SciPy knows the $F$ distribution:
\verb|stats.f.sf(F, df1, df2)| gives the area to the right of \verb|F| (``sf'' stands for \emph{survival
function}). Here is \filename{fit\_quality.py}, which fits the line with \verb|stats.linregress|, as in the last
chapter, and grades it:

\begin{minted}{python}
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
from penguins import load_penguins

data = load_penguins()
x = data["flipper_length_mm"]
y = data["body_mass_g"]
n = len(y)

fit = stats.linregress(x, y)
y_hat = fit.intercept + fit.slope * x

ss_tot = np.sum((y - np.mean(y))**2)
ss_reg = np.sum((y_hat - np.mean(y))**2)
ss_res = np.sum((y - y_hat)**2)
print(f"SS_tot = {ss_tot:.4g}, SS_reg + SS_res = {ss_reg + ss_res:.4g}")

r_squared = 1 - ss_res / ss_tot
print(f"R^2 = {r_squared:.3f}, r^2 = {fit.rvalue**2:.3f}")
print(f"RMSE = {np.sqrt(ss_res / n):.0f} g")

# ANOVA: one predictor, n - 2 residual degrees of freedom
F = (ss_reg / 1) / (ss_res / (n - 2))
p_value = stats.f.sf(F, 1, n - 2)
print(f"F = {F:.0f}, p-value = {p_value:.1e}")

# The t statistic for the slope, from the previous chapter
t = fit.slope / fit.stderr
print(f"t = {t:.2f}, t^2 = {t**2:.0f}")
\end{minted}

\begin{verbatim}
SS_tot = 2.153e+08, SS_reg + SS_res = 2.153e+08
R^2 = 0.762, r^2 = 0.762
RMSE = 392 g
F = 1060, p-value = 3.1e-105
t = 32.56, t^2 = 1060
\end{verbatim}

The line is $\widehat{\text{mass}} = -5872 + 50.15 \times \text{flipper}$. Flipper length explains 76\% of the
variation in body mass, a typical prediction is off by 392 g, and $t^2 = F$ again. The
$p$-value has 104 zeros after the decimal point; with 333 penguins, there is no doubt that the relationship
is real. Notice that ``real'' is not the same as ``strong'': with enough data, even a weak relationship gets a
tiny $p$-value. $R^2$ and RMSE tell you how useful the line is.

\section{More Features, Higher \texorpdfstring{$R^2$}{R-squared}, Worse Predictions}\index{adjusted R-squared}
\index{multiple regression}
A line uses one predictor. \newterm{Multiple regression} uses several:
$\hat{y} = b_0 + b_1 x_1 + b_2 x_2 + \cdots + b_p x_p$, still choosing the $b$'s to make $SS_{res}$ as small as
possible. In NumPy, you stack the predictors as columns of a matrix $X$, with a first column of ones for $b_0$,
and \verb|np.linalg.lstsq(X, y)| finds the least squares coefficients. (The Least Squares chapter in the
machine-learning unit shows how.) The ANOVA table and $R^2$ work the same way, with $p$ and $n - p - 1$ degrees of
freedom.

There is a catch. Adding a predictor can never make $SS_{res}$ go up on the data used to fit the model: least
squares could always set the new coefficient to zero and do exactly as well as before. So $R^2$ never decreases
as you add predictors, even useless ones.

The \newterm{adjusted $R^2$} charges a fee for each of the $p$ features:
$$R^2_{adj} = 1 - (1 - R^2)\,\frac{n - 1}{n - p - 1}$$
Let us test both. The program \filename{noise.py} predicts mass from flipper length plus $k$ columns of
pure random numbers, which carry no information about penguins at all. It fits each model to 250 randomly chosen
penguins, the \emph{training} penguins, and then measures its RMSE on the other 83, which it never saw:

\begin{minted}{python}
import numpy as np
from penguins import load_penguins

data = load_penguins()
y = data["body_mass_g"]
n = len(y)
order = np.random.default_rng(1).permutation(n)
train, test = order[:250], order[250:]

# 40 columns of pure random numbers: no information about penguins
junk = np.random.default_rng(0).normal(size=(n, 40))

print("junk   R^2   adj R^2   test RMSE")
for k in [0, 5, 10, 20, 40]:
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), data["flipper_length_mm"], junk[:, :k]])
    b, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X[train], y[train])
    res = y[train] - X[train] @ b
    r2 = 1 - np.sum(res**2) / np.sum((y[train] - np.mean(y[train]))**2)
    p = k + 1                              # number of features
    adj = 1 - (1 - r2) * (250 - 1) / (250 - p - 1)
    rmse = np.sqrt(np.mean((y[test] - X[test] @ b)**2))
    print(f"{k:4d}  {r2:.3f}   {adj:.3f}    {rmse:.0f}")
\end{minted}

\begin{verbatim}
junk   R^2   adj R^2   test RMSE
   0  0.747   0.746    343
   5  0.750   0.743    344
  10  0.764   0.753    354
  20  0.783   0.763    378
  40  0.807   0.768    414
\end{verbatim}

$R^2$ climbs from 0.747 to 0.807 as we add garbage, while the RMSE on the unseen penguins gets worse by 71 g. The
model is memorizing the random quirks of the training penguins, which is called \newterm{overfitting}.
Adjusted $R^2$ grows more slowly, but even it is fooled here. The safest way to compare models is to measure them
on data they did not see.

\section{Limits of These Measures}
\begin{itemize}
\item A high $R^2$ does not mean the model is the right shape; the stopping-distance line has
$R^2 = 0.97$. Look at the residual plot.
\item A low $p$-value means the relationship is probably not a fluke. It does not mean the relationship is
strong, or causal.
\item $R^2$ on the data used for fitting always rewards more predictors, so compare models on data they did
not see.
\end{itemize}

\begin{Exercise}[label=lr2_1, title=Compute R-squared]
A regression has $SS_{res} = 25$ and $SS_{tot} = 100$. What is $R^2$, and what does it mean?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=lr2_1]
$R^2 = 1 - \frac{25}{100} = 0.75$. The line explains 75\% of the variation in $y$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=lr2_2, title=A model that predicts the mean]
Explain why $R^2 = 0$ for a model that predicts $\bar{y}$ for every observation.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=lr2_2]
Such a model has $\hat{y}_i = \bar{y}$, so $SS_{res} = \sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2 = SS_{tot}$.
Then $R^2 = 1 - \frac{SS_{tot}}{SS_{tot}} = 0$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=lr2_3, title=Read a residual plot]
In a residual plot, the residuals are positive for small $\hat{y}$, negative in the middle, and positive again
for large $\hat{y}$. What does this suggest about the model?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=lr2_3]
The pattern is curved, not random, like the stopping-distance example. A straight line does not describe the
relationship, and a model with a curved term, or a transformation of the variables, would be a better choice.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=lr2_4, title=Fill in an ANOVA table, space=3cm]
A line is fit to 12 points. If $SS_{tot} = 80$ and $SS_{res} = 20$, find $SS_{reg}$, $R^2$, and $F$.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=lr2_4]
$SS_{reg} = 80 - 20 = 60$ and $R^2 = 60/80 = 0.75$. The residual degrees of freedom are $12 - 2 = 10$, so
$F = \frac{60/1}{20/10} = 30$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=lr2_5, title={Correlation from R-squared}]
A line of mass against flipper length has $R^2 = 0.762$. What is the correlation $r$? How do you know its
sign?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=lr2_5]
$r = \pm\sqrt{0.762} \approx \pm 0.873$. The slope is positive (50 g per mm), and $r$ always has the same sign
as the slope, so $r \approx 0.873$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=lr2_6, title={Fix the stopping distance}]
For the stopping distances, the line has $R^2 = 0.973$. Adding a column of squared speeds, so that
$\hat{d} = b_0 + b_1 v + b_2 v^2$, raises $R^2$ to $0.987$. Since $R^2$ always rises when you add a column,
why should you believe this gain, but not the gains from the junk columns?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=lr2_6]
The $v^2$ column comes from physics (braking distance grows with the square of speed), and it removes the U
pattern from the residual plot. The junk columns had no reason to help and made the RMSE on unseen penguins worse. A
column is believable when it fixes a visible pattern and improves performance on data the model did not
see.
\end{Answer}

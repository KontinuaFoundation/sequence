\chapter{Implicit Differentiation}

The equation $x^2 + y^2 = 25$ draws a perfectly good circle, but it is
not a function: most values of $x$ give two values of $y$. You could
split it into two functions, $y = \sqrt{25 - x^2}$ and $y = -\sqrt{25 -
x^2}$, and differentiate each. But some curves, like the leaf-shaped
$x^3 + y^3 = 3xy$ later in this chapter, are nearly impossible to solve
for $y$. \newterm{Implicit differentiation}\index{implicit differentiation}
finds slopes without solving for $y$ at all.

\section{Implicit Differentiation Procedure}

The trick: pretend $y$ is some function of $x$, even if you do not know
which one. Then differentiate both sides of the equation with respect to
$x$. Terms with only $x$ work as usual. Whenever you differentiate
something with $y$ in it, the \index{chain rule!implicit}chain rule tacks
on a factor of $\frac{dy}{dx}$. For example,
$$\frac{d}{dx}y^2 = 2y\frac{dy}{dx}$$
just as $\frac{d}{dx}(g(x))^2 = 2g(x)g'(x)$. Finally, solve the resulting
equation for $\frac{dy}{dx}$.

\textbf{Worked example: a slope on a circle.} Differentiate both sides of
$x^2 + y^2 = 25$ with respect to $x$:
$$2x + 2y\frac{dy}{dx} = 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{x}{y}$$
At the point $(3, 4)$, the slope is $\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{3}{4}$. The
answer depends on both $x$ and $y$, which makes sense: at $(3, 4)$ and $(3,
-4)$ the circle has different slopes.

\section{Example}

Consider the equation of a \index{circle!implicit equation}circle with radius $r$:

$$x^2 + y^2 = r^2$$

First, we will find $\frac{dy}{dx}$ without implicit 
differentiation. Next, we will apply implicit differentiation to get 
the same result. 

\subsection{Without Implicit Differentiation}
First, we need to rearrange the equation to solve for y: $$y^2 = r^2 
- x^2$$
$$y = \pm \sqrt{r^2 - x^2}$$

We take the derivative of $y$ by applying the Chain Rule:
$$\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{1}{2 \left(\pm \sqrt{r^2 - x^2}\right)} \cdot (- 2x) = 
\frac{-x}{\pm \sqrt{r^2 - x^2}}$$ 

Notice the denominator of this fraction is the same as the solution 
we found for $y$, $y=\pm \sqrt{r^2-x^2}$. So, we can also represent 
this as: $$\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{-x}{y}$$

\subsection{With Implicit Differentiation}
With implicit differentiation, we assume $y$ is a function of $x$ and 
apply the Chain Rule. $$\frac{d}{dx}[x^2+y^2]=\frac{d}{dx}[r^2]$$ For 
$x^2$ and $r^2$ (a constant), we take the derivative as we normally would. For 
$y^2$, the chain rule adds a $\frac{dy}{dx}$:
$$2x+2y\frac{dy}{dx}=0$$ Solving for $\frac{dy}{dx}$, we find 
$$\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{-x}{y}$$ which is the same result as we found 
without implicit differentiation. 

\section{Folium of Descartes}

It was relatively easy to rearrange the equation for a circle to solve for $y$, but that is not always the case. To help you understand this better, we will consider a famous curve known as the \emph{\index{Folium of Descartes}Folium of Descartes}, given by the equation,
\[
x^3 + y^3 = 3xy.
\]

The word \emph{folium} comes from the Latin word for “leaf,” describing the curve’s distinctive looped shape. The curve is named after René Descartes, a French mathematician who originally presented the curve as a challenge  to fellow mathematician Pierre de Fermat, asking him to find the tangent line to the curve. Fermat was able to solve the problem with ease!

The Folium of Descartes (seen in Figure~\ref{fig:folium_descartes}) is historically significant because unlike simpler curves such as circles or parabolas, it exhibits more complex behavior, including a self-intersection at the origin (the curve crosses itself!). This complexity makes it difficult to describe the curve using a single equation of the form $y = f(x)$.

To explicitly solve the equation $x^3 + y^3 = 3xy$ for $y$ requires multiple expressions to fully capture the entire curve. As a result, the familiar techniques used for functions defined explicitly in terms of $x$ are no longer sufficient. Instead, to find the slope of the tangent line at a point on the folium, we must use \emph{implicit differentiation}.

\subsection{Example: Tangent to Folium of Descartes}

In this example, we will use implicit differentiation to easily find 
the tangent line at a point on the folium. 

(a) Find $\frac{dy}{dx} \text{ if } x^3+y^3 = 3xy$

(b) \index{tangent line!implicit}Find the tangent to the folium $x^3+y^3=3xy$ at the point $(\frac{3}{2}, \frac{3}{2})$. (Check: $\frac{27}{8} + \frac{27}{8} = \frac{27}{4} = 3 \cdot \frac{3}{2} \cdot \frac{3}{2}$.)

(c) \index{horizontal tangent}Is there any place in the first quadrant where the tangent line 
is horizontal? If so, state the point(s). 

Solution:

(a) $\frac{d}{dx}[x^3+y^3]=\frac{d}{dx}[3xy]$
$$3x^2+3y^2\frac{dy}{dx}=3x\frac{dy}{dx}+3y$$
$$x^2+y^2\frac{dy}{dx}=x\frac{dy}{dx}+y$$
Rearranging to solve for $\frac{dy}{dx}$:
$$\frac{dy}{dx}(y^2-x)=y-x^2$$
$$\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{y-x^2}{y^2-x}$$

(b) We already have the point, $(\frac{3}{2}, \frac{3}{2})$, so to write an 
equation for the tangent line, all we need is the slope. Substituting 
$x = y = \frac{3}{2}$ into our result from part (a):
$$\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{\frac{3}{2}-\frac{9}{4}}{\frac{9}{4}-\frac{3}{2}}=\frac{-3/4}{3/4}=-1$$
This is the slope, $m$. Using the point-slope form of a line, our 
tangent line is $y-\frac{3}{2}=-\left(x-\frac{3}{2}\right)$, or $y = 3 - x$. 

(c) Recall that in the first quadrant, $x > 0$ and $y > 0$. We will 
set our solution for $\frac{dy}{dx}$  equal to 0:
$$\frac{y-x^2}{y^2-x} = 0$$
which implies that $$y - x^2 = 0$$ \\
Substituting $y = x^2$ into the original equation: 
$$x^3 + (x^2)^3 = 3(x)(x^2)$$ 
$$x^3 + x^6 = 3x^3$$
Which simplifies to 
$$x^6 = 2x^3$$
Since we have excluded $x = 0$ by restricting our search to the first 
quadrant, we can divide both sides by $x^3$: 
$$x^3 = 2$$ 
$$x = \sqrt[3]{2} \approx 1.26$$
Substituting $x \approx 1.26$ into our equation for $y$: 
$$y = \sqrt[3]{4} \approx 1.59$$
(The denominator $y^2 - x$ is not zero there, so the slope really is 0.)

Therefore, the folium has a horizontal tangent line at the point 
$(1.26, 1.59)$.

\section{Practice}
\begin{Exercise}[label=implicit1]
	[This problem was originally presented as a no-calculator, 
	multiple-choice question on the 2012 AP Calculus BC Exam.] If 
	$\arcsin{x} = \ln{y}$, what is $\frac{dy}{dx}$?
\end{Exercise}

\begin{Answer}[ref=implicit1]
	Using implicit differentiation, we see that:
	$$\frac{d}{dx}\arcsin{x} = \frac{d}{dx}\ln{y}$$
	$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}} = \frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dx}$$
	Multiplying both sides by $y$ to isolate $\frac{dy}{dx}$, we find that:
	$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{y}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$$
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label = implicit2]
	[This problem was originally presented as a no-calculator, 
	multiple-choice question on the 2012 AP Calculus BC Exam.] The points 
	$(-1, -1)$ and $(1, -5)$ are on the graph of a function $y = f(x)$ 
	that satisfies the \index{differential equation!implicit}differential equation $\frac{dy}{dx} = x^2 + y$. 
	Use implicit differentiation to find $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$. Determine 
	if each point is a local minimum, local maximum, or inflection point 
	by substituting the $x$ and $y$ values of the coordinates into 
	$\frac{dy}{dx}$ and $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$. 
\end{Exercise}

\begin{Answer}[ref=implicit2]
	\index{second derivative!implicit}First, we need to find $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$:
	$$\frac{d}{dx}\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{d}{dx}x^2 + \frac{d}{dx}y$$
	$$= 2x + \frac{dy}{dx} = 2x + x^2 + y$$
	At $(-1,-1)$, $\frac{dy}{dx} = (-1)^2 + (-1) = 0$ and 
	$\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = 2(-1) + (-1)^2 + (-1) = -2 < 0$. Since the 
	slope of y is zero and the graph of y is concave down, $(-1,-1)$ is a 
	local maximum. At $(1, -5)$, $\frac{dy}{dx} = 1^2 + -5 = -4 \neq 0$ 
	and $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = 2(1) + 1^2 + (-5) = -2 \neq 0$. Since 
	neither the first nor second derivative of $y$ are zero, $(1, -5)$ 
	is neither a local extremum nor an inflection point. 
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Slope on a Circle}, label=implicit3]
For $x^2 + y^2 = 25$, find $\frac{dy}{dx}$ at the point $(-4, 3)$.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=implicit3]
$\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{x}{y} = -\frac{-4}{3} = \frac{4}{3}$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Tangent to an Ellipse}, label=implicit4]
Find the equation of the line tangent to the ellipse $x^2 + 4y^2 = 8$ at
the point $(2, 1)$.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=implicit4]
$2x + 8y\frac{dy}{dx} = 0$, so $\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{x}{4y}$. At $(2, 1)$
the slope is $-\frac{1}{2}$, and the tangent line is $y - 1 =
-\frac{1}{2}(x - 2)$, or $y = 2 - \frac{x}{2}$.
\end{Answer}

\section{Derivatives of Parametric Functions}
In the parametric functions chapter, you described curves with a pair of
equations $x(t)$ and $y(t)$. Now that you can take derivatives, you can
find the slope of such a curve.
\index{derivative!parametric}
You could eliminate $t$ and differentiate the result, but there is an
easier way. By the chain rule, $\frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{dy}{dx}\cdot\frac{dx}{dt}$,
so:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}.
\end{equation*}

The tangent is horizontal where $\frac{dy}{dt} = 0$ (and $\frac{dx}{dt}
\neq 0$), and vertical where $\frac{dx}{dt} = 0$ (and $\frac{dy}{dt} \neq
0$).


\subsection*{Example}

Find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ for the parametric equations
\[
    x = t^2 + 1, \quad y = 3t - 2.
\]

\textbf{Solution}

First compute the derivatives with respect to $t$:
\[
    \frac{dx}{dt} = 2t, \quad \frac{dy}{dt} = 3.
\]

Now divide:
\[
    \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{3}{2t}.
\]

As a check, eliminate the parameter. From $x = t^2 + 1$, we get (for $t > 0$)
\[
    t = \sqrt{x - 1}.
\]
Substitute into $y = 3t - 2$:
\[
    y = 3\sqrt{x - 1} - 2.
\]
Differentiating gives
\[
    \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{3}{2\sqrt{x - 1}},
\]
which matches the parametric result.


\begin{Exercise}[label = parametric3]

    Consider the parametric equations
    \begin{equation*}
        x = t^3 - 3t, \quad y = t^2.
    \end{equation*}

    \begin{itemize}
        \item[(a)] Compute $\dfrac{dx}{dt}$ and $\dfrac{dy}{dt}$.
        \item[(b)] Find the values of $t$ for which the curve has horizontal tangents.
        \item[(c)] Find the values of $t$ for which the curve has vertical tangents.
        \item[(d)] Identify the points on the curve where these tangents occur.
    \end{itemize}
\end{Exercise}

\begin{Answer}[ref=parametric3]

    First compute the derivatives:
    \begin{equation*}
        \frac{dx}{dt} = 3t^2 - 3, \quad \frac{dy}{dt} = 2t.
    \end{equation*}

    Horizontal tangents occur when $\dfrac{dy}{dt} = 0$ and $\dfrac{dx}{dt} \neq 0$.
    \begin{equation*}
        2t = 0 \Rightarrow t = 0.
    \end{equation*}

    At $t = 0$, the point on the curve is
    \begin{equation*}
        x = 0, \quad y = 0.
    \end{equation*}

    Vertical tangents occur when $\dfrac{dx}{dt} = 0$ and $\dfrac{dy}{dt} \neq 0$.
    \begin{equation*}
        3t^2 - 3 = 0 \Rightarrow t = \pm 1.
    \end{equation*}

    At $t = 1$, the point is
    \begin{equation*}
        ( -2, 1 ),
    \end{equation*}
    and at $t = -1$, the point is
    \begin{equation*}
        ( 2, 1 ).
    \end{equation*}

\end{Answer}


\begin{Exercise}[label = parametric4]

    Consider the parametric equations
    \begin{equation*}
        x = 2t + 1, \quad y = t^2.
    \end{equation*}

    \begin{itemize}
        \item[(a)] Find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ in terms of $t$.
        \item[(b)] Evaluate $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ at $t = 1$.
        \item[(c)] Interpret your result as the slope of the tangent line at the corresponding point.
    \end{itemize}
\end{Exercise}

\begin{Answer}[ref=parametric4]

    First compute the derivatives with respect to $t$:
    \begin{equation*}
        \frac{dx}{dt} = 2, \quad \frac{dy}{dt} = 2t.
    \end{equation*}

    The derivative of the curve is
    \begin{equation*}
        \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt} = \frac{2t}{2} = t.
    \end{equation*}

    At $t = 1$,
    \begin{equation*}
        \frac{dy}{dx} = 1.
    \end{equation*}

    Thus, the slope of the tangent line at the corresponding point on the curve is $1$.

\end{Answer}

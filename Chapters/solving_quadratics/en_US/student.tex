\chapter{Solving Quadratics}

Toss a ball, and its height follows a quadratic. So does the area of a
square garden as you widen it, and the profit of a business as it raises
prices. Sooner or later, you want to know \emph{when} the ball lands or
\emph{which} price breaks even. That means solving a quadratic equation.

A \newterm{quadratic function} has the form $ax^2 + bx + c$. The numbers
$a$, $b$, and $c$ are its \textit{coefficients}. They can be any
constants, except that $a$ cannot be zero. (If $a$ is zero, the function is
linear.)

Set a quadratic function equal to zero, and you have a \newterm{quadratic
equation}. For example:

$$72x^2 - 12x + 1.2 = 0$$

How can you find the values of $x$ that will make this equation true?

Dividing both sides by $a$ never changes the solutions, so you can always
make the first coefficient 1:

$$x^2 +bx + c = 0$$

For example, to solve $4x^2 + 8x - 19 = -2x^2 - 7$, move everything to
the left side, then divide by 6:
\begin{multline*}
  4x^2 + 8x - 19 = -2x^2 - 7 \\
  6x^2 + 8x -12 = 0 \\
  x^2 + \frac{4}{3}x - 2 = 0
\end{multline*}
Here, $b = \frac{4}{3}$ and $c = -2$.

\section{Completing the Square}
\index{completing the square}
Some quadratics are easy. To solve $(x + 3)^2 = 16$, take the square root
of both sides: $x + 3 = \pm 4$, so $x = 1$ or $x = -7$. (Do not forget the
$\pm$: both $4^2$ and $(-4)^2$ are 16.)

The trick is to make every quadratic look like that. Notice that
$$(x + 3)^2 = x^2 + 6x + 9$$
The middle coefficient, 6, is twice the 3, and the last term, 9, is
$3^2$. So $x^2 + 6x$ is just 9 short of a perfect square. In general,
$x^2 + bx$ is $\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2$ short of the perfect square
$\left(x + \frac{b}{2}\right)^2$. Adding that missing piece is called
\newterm{completing the square}.

\textbf{Worked example.} Solve $x^2 + 6x - 7 = 0$.

\textbf{Solution.} Move the $-7$ over: $x^2 + 6x = 7$. Half of 6 is 3, and
$3^2 = 9$, so add 9 to both sides:
$$x^2 + 6x + 9 = 16$$
$$(x + 3)^2 = 16$$
$$x + 3 = \pm 4$$
So $x = 1$ or $x = -7$.

Now do the same thing with letters. Start with $x^2 + bx + c = 0$:
\begin{align*}
x^2 + bx &= -c \\
x^2 + bx + \frac{b^2}{4} &= \frac{b^2}{4} - c \\
\left(x + \frac{b}{2}\right)^2 &= \frac{b^2 - 4c}{4} \\
x + \frac{b}{2} &= \pm \frac{\sqrt{b^2 - 4c}}{2}
\end{align*}
That gives a formula that solves every quadratic whose first coefficient
is 1:

\begin{mdframed}[style=important]
When $a = 1$, $x^2 + bx + c = 0$ when
\begin{equation*}
x = -\frac{b}{2} \pm \frac{\sqrt{b^2 - 4c}}{2}
\end{equation*}
\end{mdframed}

Completing the square also tells you what the graph looks like. Since
$x^2 + bx + c = \left(x + \frac{b}{2}\right)^2 + \left(c - \frac{b^2}{4}\right)$,
the graph is a parabola that opens upward, and its lowest point, the
vertex, is at $x = -\frac{b}{2}$, where the squared part is zero.
For a quadratic with any value of $a$, the vertex is at $x = -\frac{b}{2a}$,
which is called the vertex formula\index{vertex formula}.

The number under the square root, $b^2 - 4c$, is called the
\index{discriminant}\newterm{discriminant}. It tells you how many solutions
(or \newterm{roots}) there are before you do any more work.

If $b^2 - 4c < 0$, there are no real roots. The parabola never gets low
enough to reach the $x$-axis:

\begin{Exercise}[title={Roots of a Quadratic}, label=solve_quadratic]

  You toss a hammer upward from a height of 2 meters at 12 m/s. Its height
  in meters after $t$ seconds is

  $$p = -\frac{1}{2}9.8 t^2 + 12t + 2$$

  When does the hammer hit the ground?

  
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=solve_quadratic]

  For what $t$ is  $-4.9 t^2 + 12t + 2 = 0$?  Start by dividing both sides of the equation by -4.9.

  $$t^2 - 2.45 t - 0.408 = 0$$

  The \index{roots}roots of this are at

  $$x = -\frac{b}{2} \pm \frac{\sqrt{b^2 - 4c}}{2} = -\frac{-2.45}{2} \pm \frac{\sqrt{(-2.45)^2 - 4(-0.408)}}{2} = 1.225 \pm 1.382$$

  The other root, $1.225 - 1.382 \approx -0.16$, is a time before you threw
  the hammer, so we ignore it.

  $1.225 + 1.382 \approx 2.61$ seconds after releasing the hammer, it will hit the ground.

  
\end{Answer}


\section{The Traditional Quadratic Formula}

Divide $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ by $a$ and complete the square, and you get the
famous \index{quadratic formula}\newterm{quadratic formula}. It works
directly, with no dividing first.

\begin{mdframed}[style=important, frametitle={The Quadratic Formula}]

$ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ when
\begin{equation*}
  x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}
\end{equation*}

\end{mdframed}

This formula works for any quadratic equation, including ones where $a$ is
not 1. Recall the equation from the start of this chapter,
$6x^2 + 8x - 12 = 0$. Here $a = 6$, $b = 8$, and $c = -12$. Substitute these
into the formula:
\begin{equation*}
  x = \frac{-8 \pm \sqrt{8^2 - 4(6)(-12)}}{2(6)}
    = \frac{-8 \pm \sqrt{64 + 288}}{12}
    = \frac{-8 \pm \sqrt{352}}{12}
\end{equation*}
Since $\sqrt{352} \approx 18.762$, the $+$ gives
$x \approx \frac{10.762}{12} \approx 0.897$, and the $-$ gives
$x \approx \frac{-26.762}{12} \approx -2.230$.

Watch the signs. Since $c = -12$, the term $-4ac$ becomes
$-4(6)(-12) = +288$, so the number under the square root grows.

Earlier, you divided this equation by 6 to get $x^2 + \frac{4}{3}x - 2 = 0$.
The formula for $a = 1$ gives the same roots: with $b = \frac{4}{3}$ and
$c = -2$, $-\frac{b}{2} \pm \frac{\sqrt{b^2 - 4c}}{2}$ also comes out to
$x \approx 0.897$ and $x \approx -2.230$. Use whichever form is easier for
the numbers in front of you.

The formula also answers the question at the start of this chapter. For
$72x^2 - 12x + 1.2 = 0$, the number under the square root is
$(-12)^2 - 4(72)(1.2) = 144 - 345.6 = -201.6$. No real number is the square
root of a negative number, so this equation has no real solutions.


\section{Factoring Quadratics}

The quadratic formula always works, but the arithmetic can get heavy,
especially without a calculator. For some quadratics,
\index{factoring}factoring is much faster.

Factoring rewrites a quadratic as a product of two simpler expressions. For
example, $x^2 + 5x + 6$ can be written as $(x + 2)(x + 3)$. Expanding the
product gives back the original: $x^2 + 3x + 2x + 6 = x^2 + 5x + 6$.

The factored form makes the roots easy to read because of the
\newterm{zero product property}\index{zero product property}: if two numbers
multiply to zero, at least one of them must be zero. So
$(x + 2)(x + 3) = 0$ means $x + 2 = 0$ or $x + 3 = 0$, which gives $x = -2$
or $x = -3$. These are the same roots the quadratic formula gives, found
without a square root.

\textbf{Worked example.} Solve $x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0$.

\textbf{Solution.} We need two numbers that multiply to 6 and add to $-5$:
$-2$ and $-3$. So $(x - 2)(x - 3) = 0$, and $x = 2$ or $x = 3$. Check:
$2^2 - 5(2) + 6 = 0$ and $3^2 - 5(3) + 6 = 0$.

Here are the factoring techniques worth knowing.

\subsection*{Types of Factoring Techniques}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Factoring out the GCF (Greatest Common Factor) \\
  Always check for a common factor first:
  \[
  2x^2 + 4x = 2x(x + 2)
  \]

  \item Factoring Trinomials: \( a = 1 \) \\
  For expressions like \( x^2 + bx + c \), find two numbers that:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Multiply to \( c \)
    \item Add to \( b \)
  \end{itemize}
  \[
  x^2 + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3)
  \]

  \item Factoring Trinomials: \( a \neq 1 \) \\
  Use the ``\index{AC method}AC method'':
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Multiply \( a \times c \)
    \item Find two numbers that multiply to \( ac \) and add to \( b \)
    \item Split the middle term and factor by grouping
  \end{enumerate}
  \[
  6x^2 + 11x + 4 = 6x^2 + 3x + 8x + 4 = 3x(2x + 1) + 4(2x + 1) = (3x + 4)(2x + 1)
  \]

  \item Special Cases:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Perfect Square Trinomials: \\
    \( a^2 + 2ab + b^2 = (a + b)^2 \) \\
    \( x^2 + 6x + 9 = (x + 3)^2 \)

    \item \index{difference of squares}Difference of Squares: \\
    \( a^2 - b^2 = (a - b)(a + b) \) \\
    \( x^2 - 16 = (x - 4)(x + 4) \)
  \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Tips}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Always factor out the GCF first.
  \item Check your result by expanding (FOIL). The inverse of factoring is expanding using the FOIL method.
  \item Some quadratics cannot be factored using integers. For those, use the quadratic formula.
\end{itemize}



\begin{Exercise}[title={Difference of Squares}, label=quad1]
Solve $x^2 - 4 = 0$.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=quad1]
Factor as $(x - 2)(x + 2) = 0$. The solutions are $x = 2$ and $x = -2$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Pick your method}, label=quad2]
Solve each equation. Choose factoring, completing the square, or the
quadratic formula, whichever is easiest.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $x^2 + 6x + 5 = 0$
\item $2x^2 + 7x + 3 = 0$
\item $x^2 - 10x + 18 = 0$
\item $x^2 + 2x + 5 = 0$
\end{enumerate}
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=quad2]
\begin{enumerate}
\item Factor: $(x + 1)(x + 5) = 0$, so $x = -1$ or $x = -5$.
\item AC method: $ac = 6 = 6 \times 1$ and $6 + 1 = 7$, so
      $2x^2 + 6x + x + 3 = 2x(x + 3) + (x + 3) = (2x + 1)(x + 3) = 0$.
      So $x = -\frac{1}{2}$ or $x = -3$.
\item Complete the square: $x^2 - 10x + 25 = 7$, so $(x - 5)^2 = 7$ and
      $x = 5 \pm \sqrt{7}$, about 7.65 or 2.35.
\item The discriminant is $2^2 - 4(5) = -16 < 0$, so there are no real
      solutions.
\end{enumerate}
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Nothing but net}, label=quad3]
You shoot a basketball from a height of 2 m. Its height after $t$ seconds
is $h = -4.9t^2 + 8t + 2$. The rim is 3.05 m high. At what times is the
ball at rim height? Which one matters for making the shot?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=quad3]
Solve $-4.9t^2 + 8t + 2 = 3.05$, or $-4.9t^2 + 8t - 1.05 = 0$. With
$a = -4.9$, $b = 8$, $c = -1.05$:
$$t = \frac{-8 \pm \sqrt{64 - 4(-4.9)(-1.05)}}{2(-4.9)} = \frac{-8 \pm \sqrt{43.42}}{-9.8} = \frac{8 \mp 6.59}{9.8}$$
So $t \approx 0.14$ s or $t \approx 1.49$ s. At 0.14 s, the ball is still
rising past rim height. A ball cannot drop through the hoop from below, so
the time that matters is 1.49 s, on the way down.
\end{Answer}

\chapter{Trigonometric Functions}

Sound, light, tides, a swinging pendulum, the alternating current in your
walls: they all rise and fall in the same smooth pattern. That pattern is
the sine wave, and this chapter shows where it comes from.

You already know that in a right triangle with an angle $\theta$, the
\index{sine}sine of $\theta$ is the opposite side divided by the
hypotenuse. That definition only works for angles between 0 and
$\frac{\pi}{2}$. Here is one that works for every real number.

Treat the number $\theta$ as an angle. Draw a ray from the origin at angle
$\theta$, measured counterclockwise from the positive $x$-axis, out to the
\index{unit circle}\newterm{unit circle} (the circle of radius 1 centered
at the origin). The $y$ value of the point where the ray hits the circle is
$\sin(\theta)$. For example, $\sin(\frac{4\pi}{3}) = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$
(see figure \ref{fig:sine}). For angles between 0 and $\frac{\pi}{2}$, this
matches the triangle definition: the hypotenuse is 1, so opposite over
hypotenuse is just the height.

From here on, angles are in radians unless we say otherwise. Engineers and
mathematicians nearly always use radians. Put your calculator in radians
mode now; forgetting is the most common trig mistake there is.

Three facts fall right out of the picture:
\begin{itemize}
\item \index{Pythagorean identity}$\sin^2(\theta) + \cos^2(\theta) = 1$.
      The cosine and sine are the legs of a right triangle whose hypotenuse
      is the radius, 1.
\item $\sin(\theta + 2\pi) = \sin(\theta)$. Go once around the circle, and
      you are back where you started.
\item $\cos(\theta) = \sin(\theta + \frac{\pi}{2})$. Turn the picture a
      quarter turn and see.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Worked example.} Find $\sin(\frac{\pi}{2})$ and
$\cos(\frac{\pi}{2})$.

\textbf{Solution.} An angle of $\frac{\pi}{2}$ points straight up, to
$(0, 1)$. So $\cos(\frac{\pi}{2}) = 0$ and $\sin(\frac{\pi}{2}) = 1$.

\begin{Exercise}[title={Around the circle}, label=trig_unit_circle]
Without a calculator, find $\sin(\pi)$, $\cos(\pi)$, $\sin(\frac{3\pi}{2})$,
and $\cos(2\pi)$. Then use the Pythagorean identity to find
$\cos(\theta)$ if $\sin(\theta) = 0.6$ and $\theta$ is between 0 and
$\frac{\pi}{2}$.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=trig_unit_circle]
An angle of $\pi$ points to $(-1, 0)$, so $\sin(\pi) = 0$ and
$\cos(\pi) = -1$. An angle of $\frac{3\pi}{2}$ points to $(0, -1)$, so
$\sin(\frac{3\pi}{2}) = -1$. An angle of $2\pi$ is a full turn, back to
$(1, 0)$, so $\cos(2\pi) = 1$.

$\cos^2(\theta) = 1 - 0.6^2 = 0.64$, so $\cos(\theta) = 0.8$ (positive,
because the point is in the first quadrant).
\end{Answer}

\section{The slope of a sine wave}

Here is a wonderful fact. At any $\theta$, the slope of the sine graph
equals $\cos(\theta)$.

For example, $\sin(\frac{4\pi}{3}) = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ and
$\cos(\frac{4\pi}{3}) = -\frac{1}{2}$. A line tangent to the sine curve at
that point has slope $-\frac{1}{2}$:

Check it at the top of the wave, $\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$: the curve is
flat there, and sure enough $\cos(\frac{\pi}{2}) = 0$.

In calculus, the slope function is called the \emph{derivative}, so we say
``\index{derivatives!of trigonometric functions}the derivative of sine is
cosine.'' And the slope of the cosine graph? It is $-\sin(\theta)$. You
will prove both in the calculus chapters.

\section{A weight on a spring}

Fill a roller skate with heavy rocks and attach it to a wall with a stiff
spring. Push the skate toward the wall and let go. It rolls back and forth;
engineers say it \emph{oscillates}.

You can probably guess:
\begin{itemize}
\item If the spring is stronger, the skate will oscillate more times per minute.
\item If the rocks are lighter, the skate will oscillate more times per minute.
\end{itemize}

The force from the spring is proportional to how far it is stretched or
squeezed from its relaxed length. Springs are sold by their
``\index{spring rate}spring rate,'' in pounds per inch or newtons per
meter. A spring with a rate of 5 newtons per meter pushes back with 0.5
newtons when you squeeze it 10 cm, and with 1 newton when you squeeze it
20 cm.

Let us simulate the skate. Copy \filename{cos.py} to \filename{spring.py}
and add the simulation. Every hundredth of a second, the code nudges the
position by the velocity, finds the spring force from the new position,
and nudges the velocity by the acceleration:

\begin{minted}{python}
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

until = 8.0

# Constants
mass = 4.0 # kg of skate and rocks
spring_rate = 4.0 # newtons per meter
time_step = 0.01 # s

# Initial state
displacement = 1.0 # meters from the relaxed position
velocity = 0.0
time = 0.0 # seconds

# Lists to gather data
displacements = []
times = []

# Run it for a little while
while time <= until:
    # Record data
    displacements.append(displacement)
    times.append(time)

    # Calculate the next state
    time += time_step
    displacement += time_step * velocity
    force = -spring_rate * displacement
    acceleration = force / mass
    velocity += time_step * acceleration

# Make a plot of cosine
thetas = np.linspace(0, until, 32)
cosines = []
for theta in thetas:
    cosines.append(np.cos(theta))

# Plot the data
fig, ax = plt.subplots()
ax.plot(times, displacements, 'b', label="Displacement")
ax.plot(thetas, cosines, 'r.', label="Cosine")

ax.set_title("Weight on Spring vs. Cosine")
ax.set_xlabel("Time (s)")
ax.set_ylabel("Displacement (m)")
ax.legend()
plt.show()
\end{minted}
Run it. The skate's position and the cosine curve land right on top of
each other.

\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{springpy.png}

\index{simple harmonic motion}Why does the skate follow a cosine curve?
Sine and cosine waves appear whenever an object's acceleration is
proportional to $-1$ times its displacement:

$$a \propto - p$$

where $a$ is acceleration and $p$ is the displacement from equilibrium.

Velocity is the slope of the position graph, and acceleration is the
slope of the velocity graph. So the skate's position must follow a function
$f$ whose slope-of-the-slope is proportional to $-f$:

$$f(t) \propto - f''(t)$$

Cosine does exactly that. Its slope is $-\sin$, and the slope of $-\sin$
is $-\cos$. Take the slope twice, and you get the original function times
$-1$.

That is why sine waves are everywhere. Anything that gets pushed back
toward the middle harder the farther it strays (a spring, a guitar string,
a swing, the air in a flute) moves in a sine wave.

In our simulation, the spring rate and mass are equal, which makes the
wave line up exactly with $\cos(t)$. A stiffer spring or lighter skate
gives a faster wave, just as you guessed.

\section{Integral of sine and cosine}

Now look at area. Take the area between the cosine graph and the $x$ axis
from 0 to $\frac{4\pi}{3}$, counting area below the axis as negative. The
total is $-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, which is exactly $\sin(\frac{4\pi}{3})$.

That is no coincidence. The area under cosine from 0 to any $\theta$ is
always $\sin(\theta)$. In calculus, we say ``\index{integrals!of
trigonometric functions}the integral of cosine is sine.''

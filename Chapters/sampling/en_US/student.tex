\chapter{Sampling Data}


\section{Planning a Study}

Every day, decisions get made based on data. A city council votes on a new transit route based on ridership surveys. 
A school principal adjusts the lunch schedule based on student feedback. A pharmaceutical company decides 
whether to release a new drug based on clinical trial results. In every case, the quality of the decision 
depends entirely on the quality of the data behind it.

A study plan states what question you are answering, how you will gather information, and whom you will gather it from.





\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth]{samp_study_plan.png}
    \caption{A study plan answers three questions: what you want to know, how you will collect data, and whom you will collect it from.}
    \label{fig:samp-study-plan}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Vocabulary}

\begin{description}
    \item[\newterm{population}\index{population}] The entire group of individuals or items that 
    you want to learn about. The population is defined by the question you are asking.

    \item[\newterm{frame}\index{frame}] Also called a sampling frame. A list of all the members 
    of the population from which a sample will be drawn. The frame and the population often differ slightly. A school's enrollment database, a city's voter registration list, and a company's customer records are all examples of frames.

    \item[\newterm{sample}\index{sample}] The part of the population that is actually studied. 
    You collect data from the sample and use it to draw conclusions about the population.

    \item[\newterm{sample survey}\index{sample survey}] The process of collecting information from a 
    sample. The information gathered is then used to make inferences about \index{population parameter}population parameters.

    \item[\newterm{census}\index{census}] The process of collecting information from every member of the population 
    rather than just a sample. A census gives complete information, but it comes at a cost.
\end{description}

\textbf{Worked example.} A survey interviews 200 of the 1,000 households in a
town. What fraction of the population is in the sample? Is this a census?

\textbf{Solution.} $\frac{200}{1000} = 20\%$ of the households. It is not a
census, because 800 households were not asked.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.83\textwidth]{samp_pop_frame_sample.png}
    \caption{The sample is drawn from the sampling frame, which is a working list of the population. Members missing from the frame can never be sampled.}
    \label{fig:samp-pop-frame-sample}
\end{figure}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Identifying the Population and Sample}, label={ex:pop-sample-1}]
A city's parks department wants to understand how often residents use public parks. Staff 
randomly select 200 households from the city's water billing records and conduct phone interviews.

\begin{enumerate} 
    \item What is the population?
    \item What is the frame?
    \item What is the sample?
    \item Is this a census? Explain.
\end{enumerate}
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref={ex:pop-sample-1}]
\begin{enumerate} 
    \item The population is all households in the city.
    \item The frame is the city's water billing records.
    \item The sample is the 200 households selected and interviewed.
    \item No. A census would require interviewing every household in the city. Only 200 were selected here.
\end{enumerate}
\end{Answer}

\subsection{Why Not Always Use a Census?}

A census measures everyone in the population. For a small group, such as a football team, that is easy.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{samp_census_vs_sample.png}
    \caption{A census measures every member of a small group. For a large population, a sample of members is measured instead.}
    \label{fig:samp-census-vs-sample}
\end{figure}

For most populations, a census is impractical:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The population is too large. A company that wants to know what all households watch cannot contact every one.
    \item The measurement destroys the item. If testing a battery drains it, checking every battery would leave none to sell.
    \item The information goes out of date. If a survey of everyone takes months, opinions may have changed by the time it ends.
\end{itemize}

reasoning.

\medskip
\begin{tabular}{|p{0.58\textwidth}|p{0.32\textwidth}|}
\hline
Situation & Census or Sample? Explain \\
\hline
A school librarian wants to know which of her 8 student volunteers prefers morning or afternoon shifts. & \\[2.5em]
\hline

\hline

\begin{itemize}
    \item Library volunteers: Census. With only 8 volunteers, asking all of them takes almost no effort.
    \item Battery lifespan: Sample. Testing can destroy the batteries, so a census would leave nothing to sell.
    \item Commute times: Sample. The population of 40 million is far too large for a full census to be practical.
    \item Dietary restrictions: Census. With only 22 guests, collecting complete information is straightforward 
    and important for planning.
\end{itemize}
\end{Answer}

\subsection{Biased Sampling}

A sample is \newterm{biased}\index{biased} if it favors some people or answers over others. 
Bias does not go away just because the sample is bigger. 

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.76\textwidth]{samp_bias_size.png}
    \caption{A larger biased sample clusters more tightly, but its center stays away from the true population value.}
    \label{fig:samp-bias-size}
\end{figure}

Each of the following methods selects people without chance, so each tends to produce a biased sample.

With \newterm{judgmental sampling}\index{judgmental sampling}, one person picks whoever they believe looks representative. 
A school inspector who visits only the classrooms she already rates highly is using judgmental sampling.

A \newterm{convenience sample}\index{convenience sample} uses whoever is easiest to reach. 
A journalist who interviews only people at the bus stop outside his office will miss commuters who drive, cycle, or travel at other hours.

When people decide for themselves whether to answer, the result is a \newterm{voluntary response sample}\index{voluntary response sample}. 
An online poll on a news site attracts mostly visitors with strong opinions, while people who care less rarely respond.

Researchers using \newterm{quota sampling}\index{quota sampling} recruit people until a preset count for each group, such as an age band or gender, is filled. 
If a population is 60\% female and 40\% male, a quota sample of 100 stops recruiting women at 60 and men at 40. 
The group mix matches the population, but whoever is approached first fills each quota.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{samp_quota.png}
    \caption{Quota sampling fills each group to a fixed number in the order people are approached. Selection within each group is not random.}
    \label{fig:samp-quota}
\end{figure}

For populations that are hard to reach, \newterm{snowball sampling}\index{snowball sampling} starts with a few qualifying participants and asks each of them to refer others who also qualify. 
Researchers use it to study hidden groups, such as people experiencing homelessness or people with a rare disease, but the sample follows social networks rather than chance. 
\textit{Snowball sampling is beyond the scope of the AP Statistics exam.}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.74\textwidth]{samp_snowball.png}
    \caption{Snowball sampling starts with a few initial participants. Each wave of referrals recruits the next, so the sample grows through personal connections.}
    \label{fig:samp-snowball}
\end{figure}

All five methods leave selection to judgment or convenience. Random selection gives every member a fair chance of being chosen and usually produces more trustworthy results.

\section{Simple Random Sampling}

A \newterm{simple random sample}\index{simple random sample} (SRS) is a sample chosen so every group of 
the same size has the same chance of being picked. An SRS can be drawn in two ways:

\begin{itemize}
    \item In \newterm{sampling with replacement}\index{sampling with replacement}, each chosen member returns to the pool and could be picked again.
    \item Each member can be chosen only once in \newterm{sampling without replacement}\index{sampling without replacement}.
\end{itemize}

Chance, rather than convenience or judgment, must decide who is selected. Three ways to do this:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Assign every member of the population a number. Use a \index{random number table}random number table, entering at any random point and reading 
    numbers sequentially. Select members whose numbers appear, skipping values outside your range and any repeats, until you have 
    the sample size you need.
    \item Use a calculator. On a \index{TI-84!randInt}TI-84, \texttt{MATH} $\to$ \texttt{PRB} $\to$ \texttt{5:randInt(} generates random integers. 
    The command \texttt{randInt(1,500,30)} produces 30 random integers between 1 and 500. Ignore repeats.
    \item Use a physical randomization device. Write each member's identifier on an identical slip of paper, mix thoroughly in a 
    container, and draw without looking until the sample is complete.
\end{itemize}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Selecting an SRS}, label={ex:srs}]
A regional transit authority wants to survey 25 of its 300 bus drivers about workplace safety conditions.

\begin{enumerate} 
    \item Describe a complete procedure for selecting an SRS of 25 drivers using a random number table.
    \item A supervisor suggests selecting the 25 drivers who are easiest to reach during the next shift change. What type of 
    sample is this, and what bias might it introduce?
    \item A colleague suggests selecting 5 drivers at random from each of the authority's 5 depots. Is this an SRS? Explain.
\end{enumerate}
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref={ex:srs}]
\begin{enumerate} 
    \item Number the 300 drivers from 001 to 300. Enter a random number table at any point and read three-digit numbers. 
    Select any driver whose number falls between 001 and 300, skipping values from 301 to 999 and any numbers that have already been 
    selected, until 25 drivers have been chosen. Alternatively, use \texttt{randInt(1,300,25)} on a TI-84, ignoring repeats.
    \item This is a convenience sample. Drivers who are easy to reach at shift changes may work particular routes or shifts that 
    differ systematically from others. For example, they may have different safety exposure than drivers on overnight or rural routes.
    \item No. An SRS requires that every possible group of 25 drivers from all 300 has an equal chance of being chosen. This procedure 
    cannot produce a sample where all 25 come from the same depot, so not all groups of 25 are possible. This is a stratified sample, 
    not an SRS.
\end{enumerate}
\end{Answer}

\textbf{Computing in Python}

In this exercise you will load real world population data published by the World Bank, treat all 265 countries and territories as your population, draw a simple random sample, and compare your sample mean to the true population mean.

\begin{enumerate} 
    \item Import the necessary libraries.

\begin{minted}{python}
import pandas as pd
\end{minted}

    \item Load the dataset directly from the web and filter to the year 2020.

\begin{minted}{python}
url = "https://raw.githubusercontent.com/datasets/population/main/data/population.csv"
df = pd.read_csv(url)

pop2020 = df[df["Year"] == 2020][["Country Name", "Value"]].reset_index(drop=True)
print(pop2020.head())
print(f"Total entries: {len(pop2020)}")
\end{minted}

    \item Calculate the true population mean. This is the parameter you are trying to estimate.

\begin{minted}{python}
true_mean = pop2020["Value"].mean()
print(f"True population mean: {true_mean:,.0f}")
\end{minted}

    \item Draw an SRS of 20 countries and compute the sample mean.

\begin{minted}{python}
sample = pop2020.sample(n=20)
sample_mean = sample["Value"].mean()
print(f"Sample mean (n=20): {sample_mean:,.0f}")
\end{minted}

\end{enumerate}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Sampling Real Data from the Web}, label={ex:srs-web}]
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Run step 4 five times and record the sample mean each time. How close is each to the true mean? Are they all the same?
    \item Repeat question 1 with a sample size of 80. How do the results compare? What does this tell you about the relationship between sample size and sampling error?
\end{enumerate}
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref={ex:srs-web}]
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Answers will vary. The five sample means differ from each other and from the true mean, because each SRS contains a different set of entries. A sample that happens to include one or two very large entries lands far above the true mean.
    \item With $n = 80$, the sample means cluster more closely around the true mean. Sampling error shrinks as the sample size grows.
\end{enumerate}
\end{Answer}

\section{Other Random Sampling Methods}

An SRS can be expensive or imprecise for some populations. Three other random methods are common, and all three appear regularly on the AP exam.

In \newterm{systematic sampling}\index{systematic sampling}, you list the population, choose a random start, and then pick 
every $k$th member. To sample 40 students from 800, start at a random number between 1 and 20, then take every 20th student. 
The method needs a complete list of the population.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{samp_systematic.png}
    \caption{Systematic sampling with a random start of 7 and $k = 20$ selects members 7, 27, 47, and so on.}
    \label{fig:samp-systematic}
\end{figure}

For \newterm{stratified random sampling}\index{stratified random sampling}, you split the population into groups called strata 
whose members share a characteristic, such as students in the same grade or patients in the same ward. 
You then take a random sample from each stratum. Every group is guaranteed a place in the sample, which matters whenever groups need to be compared.

You then randomly select some clusters and study everyone inside them.

Strata and clusters are both groups, but they play opposite roles:

\textbf{It is important to understand the difference between strata and clusters.} 
\newterm{Strata}\index{strata} are groups that are different from each other but similar within, and you sample from all of them. 
\newterm{Clusters}\index{clusters} are groups that are similar to the whole population, and you sample only some of them but take 
everyone inside those.

\section{Bias in Surveys}

A random sample can still produce misleading results, because bias can enter after selection through how questions are asked and who responds.

The chance difference between a sample result and the population value is called \newterm{sampling error}\index{sampling error}. 
Different samples give different answers, and this variation shrinks as the sample grows. 
Bias behaves differently: it pushes results in one direction, and a larger sample does not reduce it.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.64\textwidth]{samp_error_vs_bias.png}
    \caption{Each dot is the estimate from one sample. Sampling error shrinks as the sample size grows. Bias moves every estimate away from the true value, whatever the sample size.}
    \label{fig:samp-error-vs-bias}
\end{figure}

Four types of bias are common in surveys.

\begin{description}
    \item[\newterm{response bias}\index{response bias}] Respondents give answers that differ from their true beliefs or behaviors, 
    often because the topic is sensitive or an interviewer creates social pressure. Food-preparation workers asked about hand-washing 
    by a hygiene inspector will likely overstate how often they wash, because they know the answer the inspector wants.

    \item[\newterm{nonresponse bias}\index{nonresponse bias}] People selected for the sample cannot be reached or decline to take part, 
    and they differ systematically from those who respond. Engaged parents return a mailed school-satisfaction survey at higher rates 
    than disengaged parents, so the results overstate satisfaction.

    \item[\newterm{undercoverage bias}\index{undercoverage bias}] Part of the population is missing from the sampling frame. 
    A survey about internet access run through online banner ads cannot reach people without internet access, the group most relevant to the question.

    \item[\newterm{wording effect bias}\index{wording effect bias}] The phrasing of a question shifts the answer. Compare these two versions:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item ``Do you support stricter emissions standards for vehicles?''
        \item ``Do you support stricter emissions standards for vehicles, even if it means higher car prices and fewer model choices?''
    \end{itemize}
    Attaching costs to the second version makes ``no'' more likely, even among people who support the policy.
\end{description}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Diagnosing Bias}, label={ex:bias-types}]
Identify the most likely type of bias in each scenario and explain why it applies.

\begin{enumerate} 
    \item A streaming platform sends a satisfaction survey to all subscribers but receives responses from only 8\% of them. 
    The platform reports that 91\% of subscribers are satisfied.

    \item A survey on recycling habits is conducted by going door-to-door in affluent neighborhoods only, because those streets 
    are easier and safer to walk.

    \item An interviewer asks employees: ``Our new open-plan office is designed to encourage collaboration. How much has it 
    improved your productivity?''

    \item A survey on alcohol consumption is conducted at a community health fair by a team wearing hospital uniforms. 
    Respondents significantly underreport their weekly intake.
\end{enumerate}
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref={ex:bias-types}]
\begin{enumerate} 
    \item Nonresponse bias. The 92\% who did not respond are unlikely to be satisfied in the same proportions as those who did. Highly satisfied and highly dissatisfied subscribers are both more motivated to respond than those with neutral feelings, but the pattern of non-response is unknown and the reported figure cannot be trusted.
    \item Undercoverage bias. Residents of lower-income neighborhoods are excluded from the frame entirely. Recycling behavior may differ systematically by neighborhood type, so the results cannot generalize to the full population.
    \item Wording effect bias. The question assumes the office has improved productivity and asks only ``how much.'' Respondents are not given a neutral opportunity to say productivity has not changed or has declined.
    \item Response bias. The formal appearance of the interviewers creates social pressure to give a health-conscious answer. Respondents adjust their reported behavior to match what they believe the interviewers expect.
\end{enumerate}
\end{Answer}

\chapter{Complex Numbers}

In the last chapter, you met quadratics like $x^2 + 2x + 5 = 0$ that have
no real solutions, because the formula asks for the square root of a
negative number. For centuries, mathematicians simply gave up at that
point. Then a few brave ones asked: what if we just \emph{invent} a square
root of $-1$ and see what happens? What happened was one of the most useful
ideas in all of mathematics. Electrical engineers use it to design the
power grid, and physicists use it to describe the atom.

\section{Definition}

The \index{imaginary unit}\newterm{imaginary unit} $i$ is a number with
the property
$$i^2 = -1$$
No real number does that, which is why $i$ got the (unfortunate) name
``imaginary.'' It is no more imaginary than negative numbers, which
also seemed absurd when they were new.

A \index{complex number}\newterm{complex number}\index{Complex Numbers} is
a number of the form $a + bi$, where $a$ and $b$ are real numbers. The
number $a$ is the \index{real part}\newterm{real part}, and $b$ is the
\index{imaginary part}\newterm{imaginary part}. For example, $3 - 2i$ has
real part 3 and imaginary part $-2$.

Every real number is a complex number with imaginary part 0: $7 = 7 + 0i$.
A complex number with real part 0, like $5i$ or $-3i$, is called an
\index{imaginary number}\newterm{imaginary number}.

With $i$, every negative number has square roots. For example,
$\sqrt{-9} = \sqrt{9}\sqrt{-1} = 3i$. Check: $(3i)^2 = 9i^2 = -9$.

\textbf{Worked example.} Solve $x^2 + 2x + 5 = 0$.

\textbf{Solution.} The quadratic formula gives
$$x = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{4 - 20}}{2} = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{-16}}{2} = \frac{-2 \pm 4i}{2} = -1 \pm 2i$$
Check $x = -1 + 2i$:
$(-1 + 2i)^2 + 2(-1 + 2i) + 5 = (1 - 4i + 4i^2) - 2 + 4i + 5 = 1 - 4 - 2 + 5 = 0$.
It works!

\section{The Complex Plane}
\index{complex plane}
Real numbers live on a line. Complex numbers need a whole plane. Plot
$a + bi$ at the point $(a, b)$: the real part goes left and right, and the
imaginary part goes up and down.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.8]
  \draw[->] (-4,0) -- (4,0) node[right] {real};
  \draw[->] (0,-3) -- (0,3.5) node[above] {imaginary};
  \foreach \x in {-3,...,3} \draw (\x,0.08) -- (\x,-0.08);
  \foreach \y in {-2,...,3} \draw (0.08,\y) -- (-0.08,\y);
  \filldraw[sdkblue] (3,2) circle (2pt) node[above right] {$3 + 2i$};
  \filldraw[sdkblue] (-2,1) circle (2pt) node[above left] {$-2 + i$};
  \filldraw[sdkblue] (0,-2) circle (2pt) node[right] {$-2i$};
  \filldraw[sdkblue] (2,0) circle (2pt) node[below] {$2$};
  \draw[dashed] (0,0) -- (3,2);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

The distance from $0$ to $a + bi$ is the \newterm{magnitude} (or absolute
value) of the number. By the Pythagorean theorem,
$$|a + bi| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$$
For example, $|3 + 4i| = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$.

\section{Adding and Subtracting}

To \index{complex numbers!addition}add complex numbers, add the real parts
and add the imaginary parts:
\begin{equation}
(a + bi) + (c + di) = (a + c) + (b + d)i
\end{equation}
For example, $(2 + 3i) + (4 - i) = 6 + 2i$. Subtraction works the same way:
$(2 + 3i) - (4 - i) = -2 + 4i$.

In the complex plane, adding is just adding vectors, tip to tail.

\section{Multiplying}

To \index{complex numbers!multiplication}multiply, expand just as you would
$(x + 2)(x + 3)$, then replace $i^2$ with $-1$:
\begin{equation}
(a + bi)(c + di) = ac + adi + bci + bdi^2 = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i
\end{equation}

\textbf{Worked example.} Multiply $(2 + 3i)(1 - i)$.

\textbf{Solution.}
$(2 + 3i)(1 - i) = 2 - 2i + 3i - 3i^2 = 2 + i + 3 = 5 + i$.
The key step is $-3i^2 = -3(-1) = +3$.

Here is something beautiful. Multiplying by $i$ \emph{rotates} a number a
quarter turn counterclockwise around 0. Try it: $(2 + i) \cdot i = 2i + i^2
= -1 + 2i$. Plot both points, and you will see a $90^\circ$ turn. Multiply
by $i$ twice, and you turn $180^\circ$, which is the same as multiplying by
$-1$. That is exactly why $i^2 = -1$.

It also explains the powers of $i$:
$$i^1 = i, \quad i^2 = -1, \quad i^3 = -i, \quad i^4 = 1, \quad i^5 = i, \ldots$$
Every four quarter turns bring you back where you started.

\begin{Exercise}[title={Square a Complex Number}, label=cx1]
Find $(1 + i)^2$. Then find $(1 + i)^4$.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=cx1]
$(1 + i)^2 = 1 + 2i + i^2 = 1 + 2i - 1 = 2i$. So
$(1 + i)^4 = (2i)^2 = 4i^2 = -4$.
\end{Answer}

\section{Conjugates and Division}

The \index{complex conjugate}\newterm{conjugate} of $a + bi$ is
$a - bi$: same real part, opposite imaginary part. A number times its
conjugate is always a real number:
$$(a + bi)(a - bi) = a^2 - abi + abi - b^2i^2 = a^2 + b^2$$

That is the trick for dividing. Multiply the top and bottom by the
conjugate of the bottom, and the $i$ disappears from the denominator.

\textbf{Worked example.} Compute $\frac{3 + 4i}{1 + 2i}$.

\textbf{Solution.} The conjugate of $1 + 2i$ is $1 - 2i$:
$$\frac{3 + 4i}{1 + 2i} \cdot \frac{1 - 2i}{1 - 2i} = \frac{3 - 6i + 4i - 8i^2}{1 + 4} = \frac{11 - 2i}{5} = 2.2 - 0.4i$$
Check by multiplying back: $(1 + 2i)(2.2 - 0.4i) = 2.2 - 0.4i + 4.4i - 0.8i^2 = 3 + 4i$.

\section{Complex Numbers in Python}

Python has complex numbers built in. Engineers write $j$ instead of $i$
(because $i$ often stands for electric current), and Python follows them:
\begin{minted}{python}
z = 2 + 3j
w = 1 - 1j
print(z * w)       # (5+1j)
print(abs(3 + 4j)) # 5.0
print(z.conjugate())  # (2-3j)
\end{minted}

\section{Why Complex Numbers Matter}

\textbf{Every polynomial has roots.} The \index{fundamental theorem of
algebra}\newterm{fundamental theorem of algebra} says that every polynomial
of degree $n$ has exactly $n$ complex roots (counting repeated roots more
than once). With real numbers alone, even $x^2 + 1 = 0$ has no solution.
With complex numbers, nothing is ever missing.

\textbf{Rotations and waves.} Since multiplying by a complex number rotates
and stretches, complex numbers are a perfect language for anything that
spins or oscillates. Electrical engineers describe alternating current with
them, and the equations of quantum mechanics are written in them.

\begin{Exercise}[title={Practice}, label=cx2]
Simplify each expression.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $(5 + 2i) - (3 - 4i)$
\item $(2 - i)(3 + 2i)$
\item $i^{27}$
\item $|-5 + 12i|$
\item $\frac{2 + i}{1 - i}$
\end{enumerate}
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=cx2]
\begin{enumerate}
\item $2 + 6i$
\item $6 + 4i - 3i - 2i^2 = 8 + i$
\item $27 = 4 \cdot 6 + 3$, so $i^{27} = i^3 = -i$.
\item $\sqrt{25 + 144} = 13$
\item $\frac{(2 + i)(1 + i)}{(1 - i)(1 + i)} = \frac{2 + 3i + i^2}{2} = \frac{1 + 3i}{2} = 0.5 + 1.5i$
\end{enumerate}
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Complex roots}, label=cx3]
Solve $x^2 + 4x + 13 = 0$. Check one of your answers.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=cx3]
$x = \frac{-4 \pm \sqrt{16 - 52}}{2} = \frac{-4 \pm \sqrt{-36}}{2} = \frac{-4 \pm 6i}{2} = -2 \pm 3i$.

Check $-2 + 3i$: $(-2 + 3i)^2 = 4 - 12i + 9i^2 = -5 - 12i$, and
$4(-2 + 3i) = -8 + 12i$. Adding 13: $-5 - 12i - 8 + 12i + 13 = 0$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Quarter turns}, label=cx4]
Plot $z = 3 + i$. Then plot $iz$, $i^2z$, and $i^3z$. What shape do the
four points make?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=cx4]
$iz = 3i + i^2 = -1 + 3i$, $i^2z = -3 - i$, and $i^3z = 1 - 3i$. Each is a
quarter turn from the last around 0, so the four points are the corners of
a square centered at 0.
\end{Answer}

\chapter{Electromagnetic Induction}
\index{Electromagnetic Induction}

Drop a strong magnet down a copper pipe, and something eerie happens: it
drifts down slowly, as if falling through honey, even though copper is not
magnetic at all. Lay your phone on a wireless charger, and it charges with
no wires touching. Pluck an electric guitar string, and a signal flows to
the amplifier. All three are the same effect: a \emph{changing} magnetic
field makes electricity. Michael Faraday discovered it in 1831, and it is
how nearly all of the world's electricity is generated.

\section{Magnetic Flux}
\index{magnetic flux}
In the charge chapter, electric flux measured how much electric field
passes through a surface. \newterm{Magnetic flux} $\Phi_B$ does the same for
a magnetic field. For a uniform field $B$ passing through a flat loop of
area $A$,
$$\Phi_B = BA\cos\theta$$
where $\theta$ is the angle between the field and the line perpendicular to
the loop. Flux is measured in \newterm{webers} (Wb): 1 Wb = 1 T$\cdot$m$^2$.

You can change the flux through a loop in three ways:
\begin{itemize}
\item Change the field $B$ (move a magnet closer, or switch on a current).
\item Change the area $A$ (stretch or squeeze the loop).
\item Change the angle $\theta$ (spin the loop).
\end{itemize}

\section{Faraday's Law}
\index{Faraday's law}
Faraday found that whenever the flux through a loop of wire changes, a
voltage appears around the loop. This induced voltage is called the
\index{induced emf}\newterm{electromotive force}, or emf, written
$\mathcal{E}$ and measured in volts. For a coil of $N$ turns,
\begin{mdframed}[style=important, frametitle={Faraday's Law}]
$$\mathcal{E} = -N\frac{\Delta\Phi_B}{\Delta t}$$
\end{mdframed}
The faster the flux changes, the bigger the emf. A steady flux, no matter
how large, induces nothing. Hold a magnet still inside a coil, and no
current flows; move it, and current flows. Each turn of the coil adds its
own emf, so more turns mean more voltage. (In calculus notation, the rate
of change is written $\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$.)

\textbf{Worked example.} A coil with $100$ turns has its magnetic flux change
by $0.02$ Wb in $0.5$ s. What emf is induced?

\textbf{Solution.} $\mathcal{E} = N\frac{\Delta\Phi_B}{\Delta t} = (100)\frac{0.02}{0.5} = 4$ V.

If the field changes steadily through a coil of fixed area $A$ held
perpendicular to it, then $\Delta\Phi_B = A\,\Delta B$ and
$$\mathcal{E} = -N \frac{A \Delta B }{\Delta t}$$

\begin{Exercise}[title={Induced EMF in a Coil}, label=induced_emf_coil]
A coil with $100$ turns and area $0.2\,\mathrm{m}^2$ experiences a change in magnetic field from $0$ to $5\,\mathrm{T}$ over $0.1\,\mathrm{s}$. Calculate the induced emf.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=induced_emf_coil]
The change in magnetic flux is
$\Delta \Phi_B = A\,\Delta B = (0.2\,\mathrm{m}^2)(5\,\mathrm{T}) = 1\,\mathrm{Wb}$.
The induced emf is
\[
\mathcal{E} = -N \frac{\Delta \Phi_B}{\Delta t} = -100 \cdot \frac{1\,\mathrm{Wb}}{0.1\,\mathrm{s}} = -1000\,\mathrm{V}
\]
The magnitude of the induced emf is $1000\,\mathrm{V}$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Twice the Turns}, label=ind2]
A coil with $200$ turns has a flux change of $0.01$ Wb in $0.1$ s. What is the induced emf?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=ind2]
$\mathcal{E} = (200)\frac{0.01}{0.1} = 20$ V.
\end{Answer}

\section{Lenz's Law}
\index{Lenz's law}
Which way does the induced current flow? \newterm{Lenz's law} answers:
\emph{the induced current always flows so that its own magnetic field
opposes the change in flux that caused it.} That is what the minus sign in
Faraday's law means. Nature resists changes in flux.

Push the north pole of a magnet toward a coil. The flux through the coil
grows, so the induced current makes the near end of the coil a north pole,
pushing back on your magnet. Pull the magnet away, and the current reverses
to make a south pole that tugs the magnet back.

That explains the magnet in the copper pipe. As the magnet falls, the flux
through each ring of copper below it grows and the flux above it shrinks.
Currents swirl around the pipe (called \newterm{eddy currents}) and their
fields push up on the magnet from below and pull up on it from above. The
faster it falls, the stronger the push, so it settles at a slow, steady
speed.

Lenz's law is really conservation of energy in disguise. If the induced
current \emph{helped} the change instead of opposing it, the magnet would
speed up on its own, and you would get energy from nothing.

\section{Moving Wires}
A wire moving through a magnetic field also gets an emf, even if the field
never changes. Slide a straight rod of length $L$ at speed $v$ along two
rails, perpendicular to a field $B$. The loop formed by the rod and rails
sweeps out an area $Lv$ every second, so the flux changes at a rate $BLv$,
and
$$\mathcal{E} = BLv$$
You can also see this from the magnetic force. Each electron in the rod
moves with the rod, so it feels a force $qvB$ along the rod, which pushes
electrons toward one end.

\begin{Exercise}[title={A sliding rod}, label=ind_rod]
A 0.5 m metal rod slides at 4 m/s along rails, perpendicular to a 0.3 T
field. What emf is induced? If the circuit has a resistance of 2 $\Omega$,
what current flows?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=ind_rod]
$\mathcal{E} = BLv = (0.3)(0.5)(4) = 0.6$ V. The current is
$I = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R} = \frac{0.6}{2} = 0.3$ A.
\end{Answer}

\section{Applications of Electromagnetic Induction}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Electric generators} spin coils in a magnetic field, so
          the angle $\theta$ changes constantly and the flux rises and
          falls. The result is an alternating emf. Almost every power plant,
          whether it burns coal, splits atoms, or catches wind, ends with a
          spinning generator.
    \item \textbf{Transformers} use the changing current in one coil to
          make a changing flux through a second coil. This is why they only
          work with AC.
    \item \textbf{Induction cooktops}\index{induction cooktop} drive a
          rapidly changing field under the pan. Eddy currents in the iron
          pan heat it directly, while the glass top stays cool.
    \item \textbf{Wireless chargers} are transformers with an air gap: a
          coil in the pad induces a current in a coil inside your phone.
    \item \textbf{Guitar pickups} are tiny coils wrapped around magnets.
          The vibrating steel string changes the flux through the coil.
    \item \textbf{Regenerative brakes} in electric cars run the motor as a
          generator. Lenz's law slows the car, and the induced current
          recharges the battery.
\end{itemize}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Which way?}, label=ind_lenz]
You pull the south pole of a magnet away from the end of a coil. Does the
induced current make that end of the coil a north pole or a south pole?
Does the coil attract or repel the magnet?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=ind_lenz]
Pulling the magnet away decreases the flux, so the coil opposes the
decrease by trying to hold on to the magnet: the near end becomes a north
pole, which attracts the retreating south pole.
\end{Answer}

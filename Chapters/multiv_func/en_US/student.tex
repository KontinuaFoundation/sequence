\chapter{Multivariate Functions}

In a video game, the ground is not flat. Wherever your character stands,
at some east-west position $x$ and north-south position $y$, the game
needs to know how high the terrain is. That height is a function of
\emph{two} inputs: $h(x, y)$.

A \index{real-valued function}real-valued \newterm{multivariate
function}\index{multivariable function} takes several real numbers as
input and produces a single real number as output. Mathematicians write
this as $f: \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$: the inputs come from
$\mathbb{R}^n$ (lists of $n$ real numbers) and the output is in
$\mathbb{R}$.\index{multivariable function!domain}

You have used these for years without the name:
\begin{itemize}
\item The area of a rectangle, $A(w, \ell) = w\ell$, depends on two inputs.
\item Your grade might be $G(h, q, t) = 0.2h + 0.3q + 0.5t$: a weighted
average of homework, quizzes, and tests.
\item The temperature in your classroom depends on position and time:
$T(x, y, z, t)$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Worked example.} Let $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2$. Find $f(3, 4)$ and
$f(-1, 2)$.

\textbf{Solution.} $f(3, 4) = 9 + 16 = 25$ and $f(-1, 2) = 1 + 4 = 5$.
Notice that $f(x, y)$ is the square of the distance from $(x, y)$ to the
origin.

\section{Graphs as Surfaces}\index{multivariable function!graph}

To graph a one-variable function, we plot the points $(x, f(x))$ and get a
curve. To graph a two-variable function, we plot the points $(x, y, f(x,
y))$ in three dimensions and get a surface. Think of $f(x, y)$ as the
height of the ground above the point $(x, y)$ on a map.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}
\begin{axis}[width=8cm, view={30}{30}, xlabel=$x$, ylabel=$y$, zlabel=$z$, colormap/viridis]
\addplot3[surf, domain=-2:2, y domain=-2:2, samples=21] {x^2 + y^2};
\end{axis}
\end{tikzpicture}
\caption{The graph of $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2$ is a bowl called a paraboloid.}
\label{fig:paraboloid}
\end{figure}

A function of three variables, like $g(x, y, z) = x^2 + y^2 + z^2$, would
need four dimensions to graph. We cannot draw it, but we can still
understand it with level sets.

\section{Level Curves and Contour Maps}\index{level curve}\index{contour map}

Hikers have a clever way to draw mountains on flat paper: a
\newterm{topographic map}. Each curve on the map connects points at the
same elevation. Where the curves bunch together, the ground is steep;
where they spread apart, it is gentle.

For a function $f(x, y)$, the curve where $f(x, y) = c$ is called a
\newterm{level curve}. A collection of level curves is a \newterm{contour
map}. For $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2$, the level curve $f = c$ is the circle
$x^2 + y^2 = c$, with radius $\sqrt{c}$:

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.5]
\draw[->] (-2.5,0) -- (2.5,0) node[right] {$x$};
\draw[->] (0,-2.5) -- (0,2.5) node[above] {$y$};
\foreach \c in {1,2,3,4} {
  \draw[thick, blue] (0,0) circle ({sqrt(\c)});
  \node[blue, fill=white, inner sep=1pt] at ({sqrt(\c)*cos(45)}, {sqrt(\c)*sin(45)}) {\small \c};
}
\end{tikzpicture}
\caption{Level curves of $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2$ for $c = 1, 2, 3, 4$. They
get closer together as you move outward, because the bowl gets steeper.}
\label{fig:levelcurves}
\end{figure}

For a function of three variables, $g(x, y, z) = c$ is a \newterm{level
surface}\index{level surface}. The level surfaces of $g(x, y, z) = x^2 +
y^2 + z^2$ are spheres centered at the origin.

You can draw contour maps in Python with matplotlib:

\begin{minted}{python}
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

x = np.linspace(-2, 2, 200)
y = np.linspace(-2, 2, 200)
X, Y = np.meshgrid(x, y)     # every (x, y) pair on a grid
Z = X**2 + Y**2

contours = plt.contour(X, Y, Z, levels=[1, 2, 3, 4])
plt.clabel(contours)
plt.gca().set_aspect('equal')
plt.show()
\end{minted}

\section{Limits and Partial Derivatives}

\index{limit!multivariable}Limits, continuity, and derivatives all extend
to multivariate functions, with a twist. On a number line, you can
approach a point only from the left or the right. On a plane, you can
approach $(a, b)$ from any direction, along any path, and the limit
exists only if every path gives the same answer.

The simplest kind of derivative of a multivariate function is the
\index{partial derivative!introduction}\newterm{partial derivative}: the
derivative with respect to one variable, holding the others constant.
Standing on the bowl $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2$, you could walk due east
(changing only $x$) or due north (changing only $y$):
$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2x \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 2y$$
The next chapter explores partial derivatives in detail.

\begin{Exercise}[title={Evaluating}, label=mvf_eval]
Let $f(x, y) = 3x - y^2$ and $V(r, h) = \pi r^2 h$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Find $f(2, 1)$ and $f(0, -3)$.
\item $V$ gives the volume of a cylinder. Find $V(3, 10)$ and explain what
it means.
\item Does doubling $r$ or doubling $h$ change $V$ more?
\end{enumerate}
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=mvf_eval]
\begin{enumerate}
\item $f(2, 1) = 6 - 1 = 5$ and $f(0, -3) = 0 - 9 = -9$.
\item $V(3, 10) = 90\pi \approx 283$: a cylinder with radius 3 and height
10 holds about 283 cubic units.
\item Doubling $h$ doubles $V$. Doubling $r$ multiplies $V$ by 4, because
$r$ is squared.
\end{enumerate}
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Level Curves}, label=mvf_level]
Describe the level curves of each function.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $f(x, y) = x + y$
\item $g(x, y) = y - x^2$
\end{enumerate}
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=mvf_level]
\begin{enumerate}
\item $x + y = c$ is the line $y = c - x$. The level curves are parallel
lines with slope $-1$, evenly spaced: the graph is a tilted plane.
\item $y - x^2 = c$ is the parabola $y = x^2 + c$. The level curves are
copies of $y = x^2$ shifted up and down.
\end{enumerate}
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Reading a Topographic Map}, label=mvf_topo]
On a hiking map, the contour lines are 10 m apart in elevation. On the
east side of a hill, the lines are 5 mm apart on the map; on the west
side, they are 20 mm apart. Which side would you rather climb, and why?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=mvf_topo]
The west side. Closely spaced contour lines mean the elevation changes
quickly over a short distance: the east side is four times as steep.
\end{Answer}

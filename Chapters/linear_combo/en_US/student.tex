\chapter{Linear Combinations of Vectors}

A smoothie shop sells two blends. A cup of the berry blend has 2 g of
protein and 30 g of sugar; a cup of the green blend has 4 g of protein and
10 g of sugar. Write each as a vector, $[2, 30]$ and $[4, 10]$. If you mix
2 cups of berry with 1 cup of green, you get
$2[2, 30] + 1[4, 10] = [8, 70]$. Could you mix them to get exactly 10 g of
protein and 50 g of sugar? Questions like this are what this chapter is
about.

A \emph{linear combination}\index{linear combination} of vectors is a sum
of vectors, each multiplied by a number. For example, $3 \begin{bmatrix}
    2 \\ -1
\end{bmatrix} + 2 \begin{bmatrix}
    3 \\ 5
\end{bmatrix}$ is 
a linear combination of the vectors $\begin{bmatrix}
    2 \\ -1
\end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix}
    3 \\ 5
\end{bmatrix}$. The set of all the vectors you can make this way is called
the \textbf{span}\index{span} of the vectors. Formally:
\index{linear combination of vectors}
\index{linear combinations}
\begin{mdframed}[style = important, frametitle = {Linear Combination of 
Vectors}]
A linear combination of a list of $n$ vectors, $\mathbf{v_1}, \mathbf{v_2}, \dots, \mathbf{v_n}$ takes the form:
$$a_1 \mathbf{v_1} + a_2 \mathbf{v_2} + \dots + a_n \mathbf{v_n}$$

where $a_1, a_2, \cdots, a_n \in \mathbb{R}$. You may see the list of vectors as $\mathbf{x_1}, \mathbf{x_2}, \dots \mathbf{x_n}$, both are acceptable variables.
\end{mdframed}

\index{linear combination!example}\textbf{Example}: Find a linear combination of
$\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}$ and 
$\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$ 
that gives the vector 
$\begin{bmatrix} 17 \\ -4 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$.

\textbf{Solution}: We are looking for $a_1$ and $a_2$ such that:
$$
a_1 \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}
+ 
a_2 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix} 17 \\ -4 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Looking at each dimension separately, we get the system of equations:
$$2 a_1 + 1 a_2 = 17$$
$$1 a_1 - 2 a_2 = -4$$
$$-3 a_1 + 4 a_2 = 2$$

If we can solve this system of equations, we will find $a_1$ and $a_2$. Let us 
multiply the first equation by 2 and add it to the second equation:
$$
2 \left( 2 a_1 + a_2 \right)
+
\left( a_1 - 2 a_2 \right)
=
2(17) + (-4)
$$
$$4 a_1 + 2 a_2 + a_1 - 2 a_2 = 34 - 4$$
$$5 a_1 = 30$$
$$a_1 = 6$$

Now we can take $a_1$ and substitute it back into any equation in our system 
to find $a_2$. Let us use the third equation:
$$-3(6) + 4 a_2 = 2$$
$$-18 + 4 a_2 = 2$$
$$4 a_2 = 20$$
$$a_2 = 5$$

We found $a_1$ from the first two equations combined and $a_2$ from the
third, so we must check that both values also satisfy the first two
equations: $2(6) + 5 = 17$ and $6 - 2(5) = -4$. They do. Always check
every equation: three equations in two unknowns might have no solution at
all, as you will see shortly.

Therefore,
$$
6 \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}
+
5 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix} 17 \\ -4 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}.
$$
\begin{Exercise}[title = {Linear Combinations}, label = combo]
Find a linear combination of the first two vectors that yields the third 
vector. 
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\vecb{1 \\ 2}$, $\vecb{-3 \\ 1}$, $\vecb{4 \\ 5}$
\item $\vecb{9 \\ 4}$, $\vecb{0 \\ 1}$, $\vecb{-5 \\ 3}$
\item $\vecb{7 \\ -2}$, $\vecb{-8 \\ 4}$, $\vecb{6 \\ -2}$
\end{enumerate}
\end{Exercise}

\begin{Answer}[ref = combo]
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We are looking for $a_1$ and $a_2$ such that:
    $$
    a_1 \vecb{1 \\ 2} + a_2 \vecb{-3 \\ 1} = \vecb{4 \\ 5}
    $$
    Which creates the system of equations:
    $$a_1 - 3 a_2 = 4$$
    $$2 a_1 + a_2 = 5$$

    We can multiply the first equation by $-2$ and add it to the second to 
    solve for $a_2$:
    $$-2 \left( a_1 - 3 a_2 \right) + 2 a_1 + a_2 = -2 \left( 4 \right) + 5$$
    $$6 a_2 + a_2 = -8 + 5$$
    $$7 a_2 = -3$$
    $$a_2 = -\frac{3}{7}$$

    Substituting $a_2$ back into an equation and solving for $a_1$:
    $$a_1 - 3 \left( -\frac{3}{7} \right) = 4$$
    $$a_1 + \frac{9}{7} = 4$$
    $$a_1 = \frac{19}{7}$$

    Therefore, $\frac{19}{7} \vecb{1 \\ 2} - \frac{3}{7} \vecb{-3 \\ 1} = \vecb{4 \\ 5}$.

    \item We are looking for $a_1$ and $a_2$ such that:
    $$
    a_1 \vecb{9 \\ 4} + a_2 \vecb{0 \\ 1} = \vecb{-5 \\ 3}
    $$
    Which creates the system of equations:
    $$9 a_1 = -5$$
    $$4 a_1 + a_2 = 3$$

    We can find $a_1$ from the first equation:
    $$a_1 = -\frac{5}{9}$$

    Substituting for $a_1$ back into the second equation and solving for $a_2$:
    $$4 \left( -\frac{5}{9} \right) + a_2 = 3$$
    $$a_2 - \frac{20}{9} = 3$$
    $$a_2 = \frac{47}{9}$$

    Therefore, $-\frac{5}{9} \vecb{9 \\ 4} + \frac{47}{9} \vecb{0 \\ 1} = \vecb{-5 \\ 3}$.

    \item We are looking for $a_1$ and $a_2$ such that:
    $$
    a_1 \vecb{7 \\ -2} + a_2 \vecb{-8 \\ 4} = \vecb{6 \\ -2}
    $$

    Which yields the system of equations:
    $$7 a_1 - 8 a_2 = 6$$
    $$-2 a_1 + 4 a_2 = -2$$

    Doubling the second equation and adding it to the first:
    $$7 a_1 - 8 a_2 + 2 \left( -2 a_1 + 4 a_2 \right) = 6 + 2 \left( -2 
    \right)$$
    $$7 a_1 - 8 a_2 - 4 a_1 + 8 a_2 = 6 - 4$$
    $$3 a_1 = 2$$
    $$a_1 = \frac{2}{3}$$

    Substituting for $a_1$ back into the second equation and solving for $a_2$:
    $$-2 \left( \frac{2}{3} \right) + 4 a_2 = -2$$
    $$-\frac{4}{3} + 4 a_2 = -2$$
    $$4 a_2 = -\frac{2}{3}$$
    $$a_2 = -\frac{1}{6}$$

    Therefore, $\frac{2}{3} \vecb{7 \\ -2} - \frac{1}{6} \vecb{-8 \\ 4} = \vecb{6 \\ -2}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{Answer}
\index{linear combination!no solution}Sometimes, a set of vectors cannot be combined to make a specific vector. Take 
the pair of vectors we have looked at before: $\vecb{2 \\ 1 \\ -3}$ and 
$\vecb{1 \\ -2 \\ 4}$. Can we find a combination to make vector $\vecb{17 \\ -4 \\ 5}$? Let us try. We define $a_1$ and $a_2$ such that:
$$a_1 \vecb{2 \\ 1 \\ -3} + a_2 \vecb{1 \\ -2 \\ 4} = \vecb{17 \\ -4 \\ 5}$$

Which creates the system of equations:
$$2 a_1 + a_2 = 17$$
$$a_1 - 2 a_2 = -4$$
$$-3 a_1 + 4 a_2 = 5$$

We have two variables ($a_1$ and $a_2$) and three equations. Let us use the 
first two to find $a_1$ and $a_2$, then check our answers by substituting our 
solutions into the third equation. First, we will multiply the second equation 
by $-2$ and add that to the first equation:
$$2 a_1 + a_2 + \left( -2 \right) \left( a_1 - 2 a_2 \right) = 17 + \left( -2 
\right) \left( -4 \right)$$
$$2 a_1 + a_2 - 2 a_1 + 4 a_2 = 17 + 8$$
$$5 a_2 = 25$$
$$a_2 = 5$$

Substituting for $a_2$ back into the first equation and solving for $a_1$:
$$2 a_1 + 5 = 17$$
$$2 a_1 = 12$$
$$a_1 = 6$$

Now, let us check if $a_1 = 6$, $a_2 = 5$ is a solution to the third equation:
$$-3 \left( 6 \right) + 4 \left( 5 \right) = 5$$
$$-18 + 20 = 2 \neq 5$$

Therefore, there is no linear combination of the vectors $\vecb{2 \\ 1 \\ -3}$ and $\vecb{1 \\ -2 \\ 4}$ that yields $\vecb{17 \\ -4 \\ 5}$.

\index{linear combination!as a system of equations}\begin{mdframed}[style = important, frametitle = {Linear Combinations as systems.}]
We are aiming to know if $a_1 \mathbf{v}_1 + a_2 \mathbf{v}_2 = \mathbf{w}$. We can rewrite this as a matrix multiplication:
$$\begin{array}{c} [v_1 \ v_2] \begin{bmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{w} \end{array}$$

Then, to check whether $w$ is a linear combination of $v_1$ and $v_2$, solve the system of equations. If a solution exists, then yes; if not, then no. Note that the $a_n$ values are only scalar numbers, while the $v_n$'s remain vectors.
\end{mdframed}
We will talk more about systems of equations in Section~\ref{chap:syseqma}.
\section{Visualizing Linear Combinations}
\index{linear combinations!graphing vectors of}
First, let us look at what vectors can be made from linear combinations of the 
2-dimensional \index{standard basis vectors}unit vectors $\textbf{i} = \vecb{1 \\ 0}$ and $\textbf{j} 
= \vecb{0 \\ 1}$. Suppose we are looking for a linear combination of 
\textbf{i} and \textbf{j} to create the vector $\vecb{3 \\ -4}$. We can 
find such a linear combination:
$$3\textbf{i} + \left( -4 \right) \textbf{j} = 3 \vecb{1 \\ 0} - 4 \vecb{0 \\ 1} = \vecb{3 \\ -4}$$

In fact, with \textbf{i} and \textbf{j}, we can create any 2-dimensional 
vector. To prove this, consider a generic vector, $\textbf{z} = \vecb{a \\ b}$, where $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$. We are looking for a linear combination 
of \textbf{i} and \textbf{j} such that:
$$c_1 \textbf{i} + c_2 \textbf{j} = \vecb{a \\ b}$$

The above equation yields the system of equations:
$$c_1 \left( 1 \right) + c_2 \left( 0 \right) = a$$
$$c_1 \left( 0 \right) + c_2 \left( 1 \right) = b$$

And the solution to this system of equations is:
$$c_1 = a$$
$$c_2 = b$$

Therefore, using \textbf{i} and \textbf{j}, we can construct any vector in 
$\mathbb{R}^2$ (that is, any vector in the $xy$-plane). What about combinations 
of other vectors?

\textbf{Back to the smoothies.} To get 10 g of protein and 50 g of sugar,
solve $a_1[2, 30] + a_2[4, 10] = [10, 50]$: $2a_1 + 4a_2 = 10$ and $30a_1
+ 10a_2 = 50$. The solution is $a_1 = 1$ and $a_2 = 2$: one cup of berry
and two cups of green. Because $[2, 30]$ and $[4, 10]$ are not multiples
of each other, they span $\mathbb{R}^2$, and you can hit any target (as
long as you allow negative cups, which the shop does not).

\begin{Exercise}[title={Span or Not?}, label=lc_span]
Do the vectors span $\mathbb{R}^2$? If not, describe the set of vectors
they do span.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\vecb{3 \\ 1}$ and $\vecb{-6 \\ -2}$
\item $\vecb{1 \\ 1}$ and $\vecb{1 \\ -1}$
\end{enumerate}
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=lc_span]
\begin{enumerate}
\item No. $\vecb{-6 \\ -2} = -2\vecb{3 \\ 1}$, so they are linearly
dependent. They span only the line through the origin in the direction of
$\vecb{3 \\ 1}$: the line $y = \frac{x}{3}$.
\item Yes. They are not multiples of each other. (For any $\vecb{a \\ b}$,
take $\frac{a + b}{2}\vecb{1 \\ 1} + \frac{a - b}{2}\vecb{1 \\ -1}$.)
\end{enumerate}
\end{Answer}

\section{Span in Three Dimensions}\index{span}
Imagine flying a drone whose controller has one stick that moves it
forward and back. You can reach any spot on one straight line, and nowhere
else. Add a second stick for left and right, and you can reach any point
at that altitude: a whole plane. Add a third for up and down, and the
whole sky is yours. Each stick is a vector, and the places you can reach
are their span.

\index{span!definition}Here is the formal definition. If
$S = \{\mathbf{v_1}, \mathbf{v_2}, \dots, \mathbf{v_n}\}$ is a set of vectors, then
the span of $S$ is the set of all their linear combinations:
\begin{equation}
\text{Span}(S) = \{a_1 \mathbf{v_1} + a_2 \mathbf{v_2} + \dots + a_n
\mathbf{v_n} : a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n \in \mathbb{R}\}
\end{equation}
A vector is in Span$(S)$ exactly when it can be written as a linear
combination of the vectors in $S$.

line. The same is true in three dimensions. Consider

shown in figure \ref{fig:3d_vectors-span}.

\end{figure}

Since $\mathbf{v} = 2\mathbf{u}$, the two vectors lie on the same line through
the origin, and every combination $a_1\mathbf{u} + a_2\mathbf{v} = (a_1 + 2a_2)\mathbf{u}$
lies on that line too. The span of \textbf{u} and \textbf{v} is a line. In fact,
if every vector in a list lies on one line through the origin, the span of the
list is that line, no matter how many vectors are in the list.

\subsection{All of Space}
To reach every point in $\mathbb{R}^3$, add a third vector that is \emph{not} on
that plane. A vector is on the plane exactly when it is a linear combination of
the first two. A third vector that is not a combination of the first two lets
you move off the sheet, and together the three vectors span all of $\mathbb{R}^3$.

Think of walking around a city. Moving forward and backward on one street covers a line. Adding a sideways direction covers a flat field, which is a plane. Adding a third direction that is not in that field lets you move up and down, so you cover all of $\mathbb{R}^3$.

So, to describe the span of a list of vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item If all the vectors are multiples of one nonzero vector, the span is a \textit{line}.
\item Otherwise, pick two that are not multiples of each other; they span a plane.
If every other vector in the list is a linear combination of those two, the span
is that \textit{plane}.
\item If some vector is not a linear combination of those two, the span is all of $\mathbb{R}^3$.
\end{enumerate}
Step 3 is the question this chapter started with: can a vector be written as a
linear combination of others? You answer it by solving a system of equations.

\index{span!example}\textbf{Example}: Do the vectors $\mathbf{r} = \left[ 5, 4, -6 \right]$,
$\mathbf{s} = \left[ 0, -5, -10 \right]$, and $\mathbf{t} = \left[ 0, 2, 4 \right]$
span a line, a plane, or $\mathbb{R}^3$?

\textbf{Solution}: \textbf{r} and \textbf{s} are not multiples of each other
(\textbf{s} has a 0 where \textbf{r} has a 5), so they span a plane. Is
\textbf{t} on that plane? We look for $a_1$ and $a_2$ with
$$a_1 \left[5, 4, -6 \right] + a_2 \left[ 0, -5, -10 \right] = \left[ 0, 2, 4 \right]$$
which gives the system
$$5a_1 = 0$$
$$4a_1 - 5a_2 = 2$$
$$-6a_1 - 10a_2 = 4$$
The first equation gives $a_1 = 0$. The second then gives $-5a_2 = 2$, so
$a_2 = -\frac{2}{5}$. Check the third: $-6(0) - 10\left(-\frac{2}{5}\right) = 4$. It
works, so $\mathbf{t} = -\frac{2}{5}\mathbf{s}$ lies on the plane, and
$\text{Span}(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{s}, \mathbf{t})$ is a plane.

Be careful: checking only that no two vectors are multiples of each other is
\emph{not} enough. Three vectors can be pairwise non-multiples and still lie on
one plane, such as $[1, 0, 0]$, $[0, 1, 0]$, and $[1, 1, 0]$.

\begin{Exercise}[title = {Determining Span}, label = span1]
Geometrically describe (line, plane, or $\mathbb{R}^3$) the span of the list
of vectors.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\left[ 1, 2, 4 \right]$ and $\left[-2, -4, -8 \right]$
\item $\left[ 2, 0, 0 \right]$ and $\left[ 0, 1, 3 \right]$
\item $\left[ 3, 0, 0 \right]$ and $\left[ 0, 3, 3 \right]$ and $\left[3, 3, 2 \right]$
\end{enumerate}
\end{Exercise}

\begin{Answer}[ref = span1]
\begin{enumerate}
\item The second vector is $-2$ times the first, so the span is a \textit{line}.
\item Neither vector is a multiple of the other, so the span is a \textit{plane}.
\item The first two are not multiples of each other, so they span a plane. Is
$[3, 3, 2]$ a linear combination of them? Solving
$a_1[3, 0, 0] + a_2[0, 3, 3] = [3, 3, 2]$ gives $3a_1 = 3$, $3a_2 = 3$, and
$3a_2 = 2$. The last two equations disagree, so there is no solution. The third
vector is off the plane, and the span is $\mathbb{R}^3$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title = {Span of Three Vectors}, label = span2]
Do the vectors $\left[ 1, 1, 0 \right]$, $\left[ 0, 1, 1 \right]$, and $\left[ 1, 0, 1 \right]$ span a line, a plane, or $\mathbb{R}^3$? Explain your answer.
\end{Exercise}

\begin{Answer}[ref = span2]
The first two are not multiples of each other, so they span a plane. Solve
$a_1[1, 1, 0] + a_2[0, 1, 1] = [1, 0, 1]$: the first entry gives $a_1 = 1$, the
third gives $a_2 = 1$, and then the second entry would be $a_1 + a_2 = 2$, not $0$.
There is no solution, so $[1, 0, 1]$ is off the plane, and the three vectors span
$\mathbb{R}^3$. (In the next chapter, row reduction will give you a faster way to
answer questions like this.)
\end{Answer}

\section{Summary}
\begin{itemize}
\item A linear combination is a sum of scalar multiples of vectors.
\item Asking whether $\mathbf{w}$ is a linear combination of $\mathbf{v}_1$
and $\mathbf{v}_2$ is the same as asking whether a system of equations has
a solution.
\item The span of a set of vectors is everything you can build from them.
Two vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$ that are not multiples of each other span
the whole plane; two that are multiples span only a line.
\item In $\mathbb{R}^3$, a span is a line, a plane, or all of space. A vector
adds a new dimension to the span only if it is not a linear combination of the
vectors already there.
\end{itemize}

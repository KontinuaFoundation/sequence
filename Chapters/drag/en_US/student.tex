\chapter{Drag}

One of the first electronic computers, ENIAC, was built during World War
II to calculate how artillery shells fly. Why did the army need a computer
for that? You already know the projectile formulas. The trouble is that a
shell flies hundreds of meters per second, and at that speed the air
pushes back hard. With air resistance, the neat formulas stop working, and
the only way to get answers is to simulate the flight one tiny step at a
time. That is exactly what you will do in this chapter.

\section{Wind resistance}


\index{wind resistance}
Engineers start by putting a shell in a wind tunnel and measuring the
force when air blows past it at 1 m/s. Say it is 0.1 newtons.

One of the interesting things about the drag from the air (often
called \newterm{wind resistance}) is that it increases with the
\index{drag!quadratic}\emph{square} of the speed. Thus, if the wind pushing on the shell is
3 m/s, instead of 1 m/s, the resistance is $3^2 \times 0.1 = 0.9$
newtons.

(Why? Intuitively, three times as many air molecules are hitting the
shell and each molecule is hitting it three times harder.)

Drag always points exactly opposite the velocity. If $\mu$ is the drag
force at 1 m/s, then at speed $|v|$ the drag has magnitude $\mu |v|^2$.
As a vector, that is
$$\vec{F}_{\text{drag}} = -\mu |v| \vec{v}$$
(The vector $\vec{v}$ points along the motion and has length $|v|$, so
$\mu|v|\vec{v}$ has length $\mu|v|^2$; the minus sign flips it backward.)

\section{Initial velocity and acceleration due to gravity}

Let us say a shell is shot out of a tube at $s$ m/s, and the tube
is tilted $\theta$ radians above level. The initial velocity
will be given by the vector $[s \cos(\theta), s \sin(\theta)]$

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.5\textwidth]{cannonvi.png}
    \caption{The initial velocity vector of the shell.}
    \label{fig:cannonvi}
\end{figure}



(The velocity of the shell is actually a 3-dimensional vector, but we
are only going to worry about height and horizontal distance; we are
assuming that the operator pointed it in the right direction.)

To figure out the path of the shell, we need to compute its acceleration. We remember that

$$F = m a$$

(Note that $F$ and $a$ are vectors.)  Dividing both sides by $m$, we get:

$$a = \frac{F}{m}$$

Let us figure out the net force on the shell, so that we can calculate the acceleration vector.

If the shell has a mass of $m$, the force of gravity points down with
a magnitude of $9.8 m$ newtons.

To get the net force, we will need to add the force due to gravity
with the force due to wind resistance.

\section{Simulating artillery in Python}

Create a file called \filename{artillery.py}.

\begin{minted}{python}
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Constants
mass = 45 # kg
start_speed = 300.0 # m/s
theta = np.pi/5 # radians (36 degrees above level)
time_step = 0.01 # s
wind_resistance = 0.05 # newtons in 1 m/s wind
force_of_gravity = np.array([0.0, -9.8 * mass]) # newtons

# Initial state
position = np.array([0.0, 0.0]) # [distance, height] in meters
velocity = np.array([start_speed * np.cos(theta), start_speed * np.sin(theta)])
time = 0.0 # seconds

# Lists to gather data
distances = []
heights = []
times = []

# While shell is aloft
while position[1] >= 0:
    # Record data
    distances.append(position[0])
    heights.append(position[1])
    times.append(time)

    # Calculate the next state
    time += time_step
    position += time_step * velocity

    # Drag points opposite the velocity, with size wind_resistance * speed**2
    speed = np.linalg.norm(velocity)
    force = force_of_gravity - wind_resistance * speed * velocity

    # Calculate the current acceleration vector
    acceleration = force / mass

    # Update the velocity vector   
    velocity += time_step * acceleration

print(f"Hit the ground {position[0]:.2f} meters away at {time:.2f} seconds.")

# Plot the data
fig, ax = plt.subplots()
ax.plot(distances, heights)
ax.set_title("Distance vs. Height")
ax.set_xlabel("Distance (m)")
ax.set_ylabel("Height (m)")
plt.show()
\end{minted}

When you run it, you should get a message like:
\begin{minted}{python}
Hit the ground 1519.46 meters away at 19.34 seconds.
\end{minted}

Without air, the same shell would travel
$\frac{300^2 \sin 72^\circ}{9.8} \approx 8{,}700$ meters. Air resistance
cuts the range by more than 80\%! You should also see a plot of the
shell's path. Notice that it is not a parabola: it comes down more
steeply than it went up.

\section{Terminal velocity}
\index{terminal velocity}
If you shot the shell very, very high in the sky, it would keep accelerating 
toward the ground until the force of gravity and the force of the wind resistance were equal.
The speed at which this happens is called the \newterm{terminal
velocity}. A skydiver falling belly-down reaches about 53 m/s (190 km/h).
Opening the parachute increases the drag so much that the terminal
velocity drops to about 5 m/s, a safe landing speed.

\textbf{Worked example.} A skydiver and gear have a mass of 80 kg and a
drag of $\mu = 0.28$ N at 1 m/s. What is the terminal velocity?

\textbf{Solution.} At terminal velocity, drag equals weight:
$0.28 v^2 = (80)(9.8) = 784$, so $v = \sqrt{\frac{784}{0.28}} = \sqrt{2800}
\approx 53$ m/s.
%FIXME expand 

Note that kinematic equations do not apply to terminal velocity, because the acceleration is not constant. Instead, we can use the fact that at terminal velocity, the force of wind resistance equals the force of gravity.
\begin{Exercise}[title={Terminal velocity}, label=terminal_velocity]
    What is the terminal velocity of the shell described in our example?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=terminal_velocity]
The force of gravity is $9.8 \times 45 = 441$ newtons.

At any speed $s$, the force of wind resistance is $0.05 \times s^2 = 0.05 s^2$ newtons.

At terminal velocity, $0.05 s^2 = 441$. 

Solving for $s$, we get $s = \sqrt{\frac{441}{0.05}}$

Thus, terminal velocity should be about 94 m/s.

\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Parachute}, label=drag2]
When the skydiver from the worked example opens the parachute, the drag
constant becomes $\mu = 30$ N at 1 m/s. What is the new terminal velocity?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=drag2]
$30 v^2 = 784$, so $v = \sqrt{\frac{784}{30}} \approx 5.1$ m/s.
\end{Answer}

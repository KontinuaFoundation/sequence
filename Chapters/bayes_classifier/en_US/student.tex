\chapter{Bayesian Classifiers}\index{Bayesian classifier}

A text arrives: ``Congratulations! You won a \$1000 gift card. Claim your reward before it expires tonight.''
You know it is spam before you finish reading. How? Certain words, like ``won,'' ``claim,'' and ``gift card,''
are far more common in spam than in texts from your friends. Each one nudges your belief a little further
toward ``spam.''

That nudging has a precise mathematical form: Bayes' rule. This chapter builds two classifiers on it. The first
is a spam filter for the messages from the last chapter. The second identifies penguin species from
measurements, and it shows what to do when the features are numbers instead of words.

\section{Bayes' Rule for Classification}\index{Bayes' rule}
We want the probability of each class $C$ given the features $\mathbf{x}$ we observed. Bayes' rule says
$$P(C \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{P(\mathbf{x} \mid C)\,P(C)}{P(\mathbf{x})}$$
Each piece has a name:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{6cm}}
\textbf{Term} & \textbf{Name} & \textbf{Meaning for spam} \\
\hline
$P(C)$ & prior\index{prior probability} & how common spam is before reading the message \\
$P(\mathbf{x} \mid C)$ & likelihood & how likely these words are in a spam message \\
$P(C \mid \mathbf{x})$ & posterior & how likely this message is spam, after reading it \\
$P(\mathbf{x})$ & evidence & the same for every class; it just makes the posteriors add to 1 \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Since $P(\mathbf{x})$ is the same for every class, a classifier can compute $P(\mathbf{x} \mid C)\,P(C)$ for each
class, pick the biggest, and divide by the total only if it wants actual probabilities.

\section{The Naive Assumption}\index{naive Bayes}\index{conditional independence}
The hard part is the likelihood. A message is a list of words $w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_m$, and there are far too
many possible messages to estimate $P(w_1, \ldots, w_m \mid \text{spam})$ directly. So we make a bold
simplification: assume that, once you know the class, the words are independent of each other. Then the
likelihood is a product of one-word probabilities:
$$P(w_1, \ldots, w_m \mid C) = P(w_1 \mid C)\,P(w_2 \mid C) \cdots P(w_m \mid C)$$
This is the \newterm{naive Bayes} assumption. It is ``naive'' because it is false: in real spam, ``gift'' and
``card'' tend to appear together. But the classifier only needs to rank the classes correctly, not to get the
probabilities exactly right, and naive Bayes is famous for working well anyway.

\section{Estimating the Word Probabilities}
From the training messages, $P(\text{spam})$ is just the fraction of messages that are spam: $22/60$ in our
data. For the word probabilities, count. Pour all the spam messages into one big bag of words; then
$P(\text{free} \mid \text{spam})$ is the fraction of the words in that bag that are ``free.''

After stemming and dropping stop words, the 22 spam messages contain 215 words, and the word ``free'' is 15 of
them. The 38 ham messages contain 255 words, and none is ``free.''

That zero is a problem. It would make $P(\text{free} \mid \text{ham}) = 0$, so any message containing ``free''
would have zero chance of being ham, no matter what else it said. One word would veto everything. The fix is
\newterm{Laplace smoothing}\index{Laplace smoothing}: pretend you saw every word in the vocabulary one extra time
in each class. With a vocabulary of $V = 271$ stems,
$$P(w \mid C) = \frac{\text{count of } w \text{ in class } C + 1}{\text{total words in class } C + V}$$
So $P(\text{free} \mid \text{spam}) = \frac{16}{486}$ and $P(\text{free} \mid \text{ham}) = \frac{1}{526}$:
small, but not zero.

\section{A Worked Example}
The student council texts everyone: ``Free pizza at the game tonight.'' Is it spam?

After preparing the text, the stems are free, pizza, game, and tonight. ``Pizza'' never appears in the
training messages, so the model knows nothing about it, and we skip it. The counts for the others:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l c c c c}
\textbf{Stem} & \textbf{In spam} & \textbf{In ham} & $P(w \mid \text{spam})$ & $P(w \mid \text{ham})$ \\
\hline
free & 15 & 0 & $16/486$ & $1/526$ \\
game & 1 & 3 & $2/486$ & $4/526$ \\
tonight & 1 & 2 & $2/486$ & $3/526$ \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Multiply the prior by the likelihoods:
$$\text{spam: } \frac{22}{60} \cdot \frac{16}{486} \cdot \frac{2}{486} \cdot \frac{2}{486} \approx 2.04 \times 10^{-7}
\qquad
\text{ham: } \frac{38}{60} \cdot \frac{1}{526} \cdot \frac{4}{526} \cdot \frac{3}{526} \approx 5.22 \times 10^{-8}$$
Dividing by the total,
$$P(\text{spam} \mid \text{message}) = \frac{2.04 \times 10^{-7}}{2.04 \times 10^{-7} + 5.22 \times 10^{-8}} \approx 0.797$$
The filter calls it spam, almost entirely because of the word ``free'': $P(\text{free} \mid \text{spam})$ is about 17 times
$P(\text{free} \mid \text{ham})$, while ``game'' and ``tonight'' each lean toward ham by less than 2 to 1. A real filter would learn
from more data, including legitimate messages about free pizza, and the same rule could balance that out.

\section{Working in Logarithms}
Multiplying many small probabilities quickly produces numbers too small for a computer to store. Each word
probability here is around $\frac{1}{500}$, so a message of a few hundred words gives a product below
$10^{-308}$, the smallest number ordinary floating-point arithmetic can hold, and the computer rounds it to
zero. The cure is to add logarithms
instead of multiplying probabilities:
$$\ln\big[P(C)\,P(w_1 \mid C) \cdots P(w_m \mid C)\big] = \ln P(C) + \sum_{j=1}^{m} \ln P(w_j \mid C)$$
The class with the biggest log score is the class with the biggest product. To recover probabilities at the
end, subtract the biggest log score from each score before calling \verb|np.exp|, so the largest becomes
$e^0 = 1$ and nothing underflows.

\section{A Spam Filter in Python}
Put \filename{messages.csv} and \filename{text.py} from the last chapter next to \filename{spam\_filter.py}:

\begin{minted}{python}
import csv
import numpy as np
from collections import Counter
from text import prepare

with open("messages.csv", newline="") as f:
    rows = list(csv.DictReader(f))
texts = [row["text"] for row in rows]
labels = [row["label"] for row in rows]

def train(texts, labels):
    counts = {"spam": Counter(), "ham": Counter()}
    for text, label in zip(texts, labels):
        counts[label].update(prepare(text))
    vocab = set(counts["spam"]) | set(counts["ham"])
    model = {}
    for label in ("spam", "ham"):
        total = sum(counts[label].values())
        log_prior = np.log(labels.count(label) / len(labels))
        # Laplace smoothing: add 1 to every count
        log_like = {w: np.log((counts[label][w] + 1) / (total + len(vocab)))
                    for w in vocab}
        model[label] = (log_prior, log_like)
    return model

def spam_probability(model, text):
    scores = {}
    for label, (log_prior, log_like) in model.items():
        # Words the model has never seen are skipped
        words = [w for w in prepare(text) if w in log_like]
        scores[label] = log_prior + sum(log_like[w] for w in words)
    # Turn the two log scores back into probabilities that add to 1
    biggest = max(scores.values())
    spam = np.exp(scores["spam"] - biggest)
    ham = np.exp(scores["ham"] - biggest)
    return spam / (spam + ham)

# Leave-one-out: train on 59 messages, test on the one left out, 60 times
correct = 0
for i in range(len(texts)):
    model = train(texts[:i] + texts[i + 1:], labels[:i] + labels[i + 1:])
    guess = "spam" if spam_probability(model, texts[i]) >= 0.5 else "ham"
    correct += guess == labels[i]
    if guess != labels[i]:
        print("Missed:", labels[i], "|", texts[i])
print(f"Leave-one-out accuracy: {correct}/{len(texts)}")

model = train(texts, labels)
for text in ["Free pizza at the game tonight",
             "Click here to claim your prize",
             "Can you send me the notes from history class?"]:
    print(f"{spam_probability(model, text):.3f}  {text}")
\end{minted}

\begin{verbatim}
Missed: ham | Is the essay supposed to be double spaced?
Missed: ham | My phone is about to die. Call me at home later
Leave-one-out accuracy: 58/60
0.797  Free pizza at the game tonight
0.999  Click here to claim your prize
0.010  Can you send me the notes from history class?
\end{verbatim}

\verb|Counter| is a dictionary that counts things; \verb|update| adds one for each word in a list, and a missing
word counts as 0. With only 60 messages, we cannot afford to hold out a big test set, so we use
\newterm{leave-one-out} cross-validation\index{leave-one-out cross-validation}: 60 rounds, each training on 59
messages and testing on the last one. (That is $k$-fold cross-validation with $k$ equal to the number of
examples.) The filter gets 58 of 60 right. Both misses are short messages made of words the training data barely
contains, so the prior and a few weak clues decide. ``Free pizza'' gets 0.797, as we computed by hand.

\section{Numbers Instead of Words}\index{Gaussian naive Bayes}
Bayes' rule works for any features, as long as you can estimate $P(\mathbf{x} \mid C)$. For a number, like a
penguin's bill length, the standard choice is a normal distribution. For each species, estimate the mean
$\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$ of each feature from the training penguins, and use the normal density
$$p(x \mid C) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\, e^{-(x - \mu)^2 / (2\sigma^2)}$$
as the likelihood. With the naive assumption, multiply the densities of the features. This is \newterm{Gaussian
naive Bayes}.

\section{When Features Move Together}\index{multivariate Gaussian distribution}\index{covariance matrix}
The naive assumption says that, within a species, bill length tells you nothing about bill depth. That is not
true: within Chinstraps, the correlation between the two is 0.78. A Chinstrap with a short bill usually has a
shallow one, too.

The \newterm{multivariate normal distribution} handles this. Instead of a separate $\mu$ and $\sigma$ for each
feature, it has a mean vector $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and a \newterm{covariance matrix} $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$, whose
diagonal holds the variances and whose off-diagonal entries hold the covariances. Its density for a vector
$\mathbf{x}$ with $d$ features is
$$p(\mathbf{x}) = (2\pi)^{-d/2}\det(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})^{-1/2} \exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} -
\boldsymbol{\mu})^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})\right)$$
For the Chinstraps, for example,
$$\boldsymbol{\mu} = \begin{bmatrix} 49.13 \\ 18.48 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \begin{bmatrix} 10.16 & 2.87 \\ 2.87 & 1.32 \end{bmatrix}$$
Naive Bayes is the special case where all the off-diagonal entries are zero.

In Figure~\ref{fig:gaussians}, the tilted Chinstrap oval reaches down toward the mystery penguin. The program
\filename{gaussian\_bayes.py} fits both versions and compares them with 5-fold cross-validation:

\begin{minted}{python}
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
from penguins import load_penguins

data = load_penguins()
names, y = np.unique(data["species"], return_inverse=True)
features = np.column_stack([data["bill_length_mm"], data["bill_depth_mm"]])
order = np.random.default_rng(1).permutation(len(y))
dev, test = order[:266], order[266:]

def fit(rows):
    """For each species: prior, mean vector, and covariance matrix."""
    model = []
    for c in range(3):
        group = features[rows][y[rows] == c]
        model.append((len(group) / len(rows), group.mean(axis=0),
                      np.cov(group.T, bias=True)))
    return model

def log_posteriors(model, X, naive):
    scores = []
    for prior, mean, cov in model:
        if naive:   # independent normal distributions, one per feature
            sd = np.sqrt(np.diag(cov))
            log_like = stats.norm.logpdf(X, mean, sd).sum(axis=1)
        else:       # one multivariate normal, with covariance
            log_like = stats.multivariate_normal.logpdf(X, mean, cov)
        scores.append(np.log(prior) + log_like)
    return np.column_stack(scores)

def probabilities(scores):
    p = np.exp(scores - scores.max(axis=1, keepdims=True))
    return p / p.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)

folds = np.array_split(dev, 5)
model = fit(dev)
mystery = np.array([[45.0, 17.0]])        # bill length 45 mm, depth 17 mm
for naive in (True, False):
    accuracy = []
    for i in range(5):
        rest = np.concatenate([folds[j] for j in range(5) if j != i])
        scores = log_posteriors(fit(rest), features[folds[i]], naive)
        accuracy.append(np.mean(scores.argmax(axis=1) == y[folds[i]]))
    guess = log_posteriors(model, features[test], naive).argmax(axis=1)
    test_acc = np.mean(guess == y[test])
    p = probabilities(log_posteriors(model, mystery, naive))
    print("naive" if naive else "full ", f"CV {np.mean(accuracy):.3f}",
          f"test {test_acc:.3f}  mystery:", np.round(p, 3))
\end{minted}

\begin{verbatim}
naive CV 0.936 test 0.925  mystery: [[0.213 0.35  0.437]]
full  CV 0.977 test 0.925  mystery: [[0.033 0.954 0.013]]
\end{verbatim}

\verb|np.cov| expects one variable per row, hence the transpose; \verb|bias=True| divides by $n$, matching the
maximum likelihood estimate (NumPy's default divides by $n - 1$). The naive version uses only the diagonal of the
covariance matrix.

The naive model reproduces our hand calculation. The full model is much more confident, and it changes its
mind: Chinstrap, at 95\%. Because Chinstrap bill length and depth rise and fall together, a Chinstrap with a
shorter-than-average bill is expected to have a shallower bill, which is just what this penguin has. Taking the
covariance into account raises the cross-validation accuracy from 93.6\% to 97.7\%. (On the 67 test penguins,
the two happen to tie.)

With 271 word features, a full covariance matrix would have $271^2 = 73{,}441$ entries, far more than 60
messages could estimate. That is why spam filters stay naive.

\begin{Exercise}[label=bayes_classifier1, title={Posterior by hand}, space=3cm]
A class $A$ has prior 0.3 and class $B$ has prior 0.7. For an observation $\mathbf{x}$,
$P(\mathbf{x} \mid A) = 0.08$ and $P(\mathbf{x} \mid B) = 0.02$. Find $P(A \mid \mathbf{x})$.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=bayes_classifier1]
$0.3 \times 0.08 = 0.024$ and $0.7 \times 0.02 = 0.014$. So $P(A \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{0.024}{0.024 + 0.014}
\approx 0.632$. The evidence favors $A$ by 4 to 1, enough to overcome the prior that favors $B$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=bayes_classifier2, title={Without smoothing}]
If the filter did not use Laplace smoothing, what would it say about ``Free pizza at the game tonight''? Why is
that a bad idea?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=bayes_classifier2]
``Free'' never appears in the ham messages, so $P(\text{free} \mid \text{ham}) = 0$, the ham product is 0, and the
message is spam with probability 1. A single word would overrule everything else in the message, and the model
could never change its mind about a message containing ``free,'' however many friendly words it had.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=bayes_classifier3, title={Call me tonight}, space=3cm]
Use the counts in this chapter to find the spam probability of ``Call me tonight.'' (``Me'' is a stop word.
``Call'' appears once in spam and once in ham.)
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=bayes_classifier3]
Spam: $\frac{22}{60} \cdot \frac{2}{486} \cdot \frac{2}{486} \approx 6.21 \times 10^{-6}$. Ham:
$\frac{38}{60} \cdot \frac{2}{526} \cdot \frac{3}{526} \approx 1.37 \times 10^{-5}$. So
$P(\text{spam}) \approx \frac{6.21}{6.21 + 13.7} \approx 0.31$. The filter calls it ham.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=bayes_classifier4, title={How much smoothing?}]
Change the 1 in the Laplace smoothing to a variable \verb|alpha| (it must be added to every count, so the
denominator becomes \verb|total + alpha * len(vocab)|). Find the leave-one-out accuracy for alpha = 0.1, 0.5, 1,
2, and 5.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=bayes_classifier4]
The accuracies are 57, 57, 58, 58, and 58 out of 60. Some smoothing is essential, but the exact amount barely
matters on this data. Like $k$ in $k$-nearest neighbors, alpha is a setting you could choose by cross-validation.
\end{Answer}

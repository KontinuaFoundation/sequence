\chapter{Electromagnetic Waves}

Your phone is bathed in invisible waves right now: Wi-Fi, cell signals,
GPS from satellites 20,000 km overhead, and the radio of every station in
range. They are all the same kind of wave as the light from this page, the
warmth from a campfire, and the X-rays at the dentist. The only difference
among them is frequency.

Sound is a compression wave: to travel, it needs a medium to compress,
such as air or water. (Whatever the movies show, there is no sound in the
vacuum of space!)

\index{electromagnetic wave}Light is different. It is an
\newterm{electromagnetic wave}: a ripple in electric and magnetic fields
themselves. It needs no medium at all, which is how sunlight crosses 150
million kilometers of empty space to reach us.

\section{Where Light Comes From}
In the induction chapter, you saw that a changing magnetic field creates an
electric field. In the 1860s, James Clerk Maxwell realized that the reverse
is also true: a changing electric field creates a magnetic field. Put those
together, and something amazing happens. Shake a charge back and forth, and
its changing electric field makes a changing magnetic field, which makes a
changing electric field, which makes a changing magnetic field\ldots The
two fields keep regenerating each other and race away from the charge as a
wave. The electric and magnetic fields point at right angles to each other
and to the direction the wave travels, so it is a transverse wave.

Maxwell calculated how fast this wave should travel, using only two
constants measured with magnets and charged plates in the lab:
$$c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0 \epsilon_0}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(4\pi \times 10^{-7})(8.854 \times 10^{-12})}} \approx 3.00 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}$$
That was exactly the measured speed of light. In one stroke, Maxwell
showed that light is an electromagnetic wave.

\index{speed of light}Electromagnetic waves travel at about 300 million
meters per second in a vacuum: fast enough to circle Earth seven times in
one second. They travel slower through matter. For example, light travels
at 225 million meters per second in water.

Electromagnetic waves come in different frequencies. For example, the
light coming out of a red laser pointer is usually about $4.75 \times
10^{14}$ Hz.  The wifi data sent by your computer is carried on an
electromagnetic wave too. It is usually close to $2.4 \times 10^9$ Hz
or $5 \times 10^9$ Hz (2.4 or 5 gigahertz).

Because we know how fast the waves are moving, we sometimes talk about
their \index{wavelength}wavelengths instead of their frequencies.  Since we know its speed
and its oscillation rate, we can calculate its wavelength. Since we want 
the distance per oscillation, we can divide the speed by the frequency. The light coming out
of a laser pointer is $300 \times 10^6 / 4.75 \times 10^{14} = 630
\times 10^{-9}$ m, or 630 nm.

\begin{Exercise}[title={Wavelengths}, label=wave_length_green]

  A green laser pointer emits light at $5.66 \times
10^{14}$ Hz. What is its wavelength in a vacuum?
  
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=wave_length_green]
$$\frac{300 \times 10^6}{5.66 \times 10^{14}} = 530 \times 10^{-9} = 530\text{ nm}$$
\end{Answer}

In general,

$$\lambda = \frac{c}{f}$$
where $c = 3 \times 10^8$ m/s is the speed of light in a vacuum.

\textbf{Worked example.} A Wi-Fi signal has frequency $2.4$ GHz. What is its
wavelength?

\textbf{Solution.} $\lambda = \frac{3.0 \times 10^8}{2.4 \times 10^9} = 0.125$ m,
or $12.5$ cm. That is why Wi-Fi antennas are a few centimeters long.

(You may have heard of \index{cosmic rays}``cosmic rays'' and wonder why they are
not listed in this table. Cosmic rays are not electromagnetic waves at all. They are particles,
mostly protons and the nuclei of atoms stripped of their electrons, racing
through space at nearly the speed of light. Some come from the Sun, but the
most energetic ones come from exploding stars far outside our solar
system. They were originally thought to be electromagnetic waves and were
named ``rays'' by mistake.)

Different frequencies pass through different materials. A radio wave can
pass through the walls of your house, but visible light cannot. X-rays, at
much higher frequency, pass through skin and muscle but are stopped by
bone, which is why they make such good medical images. Microwave ovens use
2.45 GHz because water in food absorbs those waves well, so they heat the
first few centimeters of the food.

\section{The greenhouse effect}



Humans have dug up a bunch of long carbon-based molecules (like oil
and coal) and burned them, releasing large amounts of $CO_2$ into the
atmosphere. \index{greenhouse effect}It may not be obvious why that has made the planet warmer, but the
answer is electromagnetic waves.

A warm object gives off \index{infrared radiation}infrared electromagnetic waves. That is why,
for example, motion detectors in security systems are actually
infrared detectors: even in a dark room, your body gives off a lot of
infrared radiation.

You may have heard of ``heat-seeking missiles.'' These are more
accurately called ``Infrared homing missiles'' because they follow
objects giving off infrared radiation -- hot things like jet engines.

The sun beams a lot of energy to our planet in the form of
electromagnetic radiation: visible light, infrared, ultraviolet. (How
much? At the top of the atmosphere directly facing the sun, we get
1,360 watts of radiation per square meter. That is a lot of power!)

About 29\% of that radiation just reflects back into space: roughly 23\%
from clouds and the atmosphere, and 6 or 7\% from the surface (especially
snow and ice).

The other 71\% is absorbed. 48\% is absorbed by the surface and 23\%
is absorbed by the atmosphere. All of that energy warms the planet and
the atmosphere so that it gives off infrared radiation. The planet
lives in equilibrium; the infrared radiation leaving our atmosphere is
exactly the same amount of energy as that 71\% of the radiation that
it absorbs.

(If the planet absorbs more energy than it releases, the planet gets
hotter. Hotter things release more infrared. When the planet is in
equilibrium again, it stops getting hotter.)

So, what is the problem with $CO_2$ and other large molecules in the
atmosphere? They absorb the infrared radiation instead of letting it
escape into space. This means the planet must be hotter to maintain
equilibrium.

The planet is getting hotter, and it is creating a multitude of
problems:
\begin{itemize}
\item Weather patterns are changing, which leads to extreme floods and
  droughts.
  
\item Ice and snow in places like Greenland are melting and flowing
  into the oceans. This is raising sea levels.
  
\item Biomes with biodiversity are resilient. Rapidly changing climate
  is destroying biodiversity everywhere, which is making these ecosystems
  very fragile.
  
\item In many places, permafrost, which has trapped large amounts of
  methane in the ground for millennia, is melting.
\end{itemize}

That last item is particularly scary, because methane is a large gas
molecule --- it absorbs even more infrared radiation than $CO_2$. As it
escapes the permafrost, the problem will get worse.

Scientists are working on four kinds of solutions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Stop increasing the amount of greenhouse gases in our
    atmosphere.} It is hoped that non-carbon based energy systems like
    solar, wind, hydroelectric, and nuclear could let us stop burning
    carbon. Given the methane already being released, it may be too
    late for this solution to work on its own.

  \item \textbf{Take some of greenhouse gases out of our atmosphere and
    sequester them somewhere.} The trunk of a tree is largely made up of carbon
    molecules. When you grow a tree where there had not been one
    before, you are sequestering carbon inside the tree.  There are
    also scientists that are trying to develop a process that pulls
    greenhouse gases out of the air and turn them into solids.

  \item \textbf{Decrease the amount of solar radiation that is
    absorbed by our planet and its atmosphere.} Clouds reflect a lot of radiation back into
    space. Could we increase the cloudiness of our atmosphere? Or
    maybe launch mirrors into orbit around our planet?

  \item \textbf{Adapt to the changing climate.} These scientists are
    assuming that global warming will continue, and are working to
    minimize future human suffering. How will we protect or relocate the hundreds of millions of people who
    live near today's coastlines as sea levels rise?  When massive heat waves
    occur, how will we keep people from dying?  As biodiversity
    decreases, how can we make sure that species that are important to
    human existence survive?
    
\end{itemize}

What are the greenhouse gases and how much does each contribute to
keeping the heat from exiting to space?  These numbers are still being
debated, but this will give you a feel:
\begin{tabular}{c | c | c}
  Water vapor & $H_2O$ & 36 - 72 \% \\
  Carbon dioxide & $CO_2$ & 9 - 26 \% \\
  Methane & $CH_4$ & 4 - 9 \% \\
  Ozone & $O_3$ & 3 - 7 \%
\end{tabular}

Notice that while we talk a lot about carbon dioxide, the most important
greenhouse gas is actually water vapor. Why do we not talk about it more?
Because we do not control it directly. The amount of water vapor in the air
is set by temperature: warm air holds more of it, and any extra rains out
within days. Carbon dioxide, on the other hand, stays in the air for
centuries. So $CO_2$ sets the temperature, and the temperature sets the
water vapor. In fact, water vapor makes the problem worse: a little warming
from $CO_2$ puts more water vapor in the air, which traps still more
heat.

\begin{Exercise}[title={FM radio}, label=em_fm]
An FM radio station broadcasts at 98.7 MHz. What is its wavelength? A good
FM antenna is about half a wavelength long. How long is that?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=em_fm]
$\lambda = \frac{3 \times 10^8}{98.7 \times 10^6} \approx 3.04$ m. Half a
wavelength is about 1.5 m.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Frequency from Wavelength}, label=em2]
An electromagnetic wave has a wavelength of $0.5$ m. What is its frequency?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=em2]
$f = \frac{c}{\lambda} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^8}{0.5} = 6.0 \times 10^8$ Hz, or $600$ MHz.
\end{Answer}

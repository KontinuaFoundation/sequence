\chapter{Introduction to Linear Algebra}

Every time you rotate your phone and the screen turns, apply a filter to a
photo, or play a video game where the camera swings around a 3D world, a
computer is doing linear algebra: millions of multiplications and
additions on grids of numbers, every second. Linear algebra is the
mathematics of vectors, matrices, and linear transformations. You have met
all three already; in this book you will see how they work together.  \index{linear algebra}

Let us review what you know.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Vectors}. Earlier, you saw how vectors can represent 
forces, as well as how to add and multiply them to figure out such things as 
rocket engine force and direction. 
\item \textbf{Matrices}. In the Basic Statistics and Spreadsheets chapter, you learned to use spreadsheets to 
solve problems numerically, such as how to figure out the number of barrels a 
cooper has to produce to achieve a certain take-home pay. Spreadsheets are 
essentially matrices --- a row by column structure that contains values. 
\item \textbf{Linear transformations}. When you studied congruence, you met transformations such as rotation, reflection, and translation. Rotations and reflections
that keep the origin fixed are linear; translation, as you will see, is not. 
\end{itemize}

\section{Linear Combinations}

A \newterm{linear combination} uses only two operations: adding things
together and multiplying them by numbers (scalars). For example, $3\mathbf{u}
+ 2\mathbf{v}$ is a linear combination of the vectors $\mathbf{u}$ and
$\mathbf{v}$. No squaring, no square roots, no $\sin$ of a variable. An
operation is \newterm{linear}\index{linear operation} if it plays nicely
with both: doing it to a sum gives the sum of the results, and doing it to
a scaled input gives a scaled result. You will see that linear combinations allow you to 
solve many types of problems in science and engineering. Before we get deep 
into the numbers, let us take a look at a few linear operations you can perform 
on images. This will give you an intuition for the underlying math. After 
that, we will take a look at some numbers.

\section{Image Operations}
\index{linear image operations}
\index{bitmap}The simplest image, a bitmap, can be represented by a two-dimensional matrix 
of values --- either 0 for black, or 1 for white. Grayscale images are also 
represented by a two-dimensional matrix of values, but the values typically 
range from 0 to 255. 0 is black, 255 is white, and the values in between 
represent shades of gray. 

Color images are more complex. The simplest color image is a three-dimensional 
matrix of values. You can think of it as three 2D matrices, one to represent 
red values (R), another for green values (G), and the third for blue values 
(B). The combination of R, G, and B determines the color you see.

Working with images means working with millions of pixels. Fortunately, 
modern techniques make this a snap. Let us look at some common operations on an 
image of a rocket.
Flipping is a linear operation: each pixel just moves to a new spot.

Next, the image is rotated 90 degrees. Rotation is linear too, at any angle.
To rotate by an angle that is not a multiple of 90, you will need $\cos\theta$
and $\sin\theta$, but those are just fixed numbers once you pick the
angle. The rotation itself only multiplies and adds coordinates.

Inversion replaces each red, green, and blue value with 1.0 minus the old
value. The white background turns black, and the rocket seems to fly
through deep space. Strictly speaking, inversion is not linear: because of
the ``1.0 minus,'' inverting a black pixel (0) gives white (1), not 0.
Operations like this, a linear operation plus a shift, are called
\newterm{affine}\index{affine transformation}. Translation is affine for the
same reason.

\section{Applications of Linear Algebra}

So far you have seen how linear operations on matrices can process images by:
\begin{itemize}
\item multiplying a matrix using a scalar (e.g., normalize, change the range)
\item adding one matrix to another to get a composite image 
\item multiplying two matrices to perform a transform (e.g., flipping)
\item multiplying entry by entry (isolating a column or a channel)
\end{itemize}

Many areas in engineering and science rely on the matrix operations defined by 
linear algebra. Besides image processing, linear algebra is used for: 
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Computer Graphics}. When you play a video game or watch the latest CG 
animation, matrix operations transform objects in the scene to make them appear 
as if moving, getting closer, and so on. You can represent the vertices of 
objects as vectors, and then apply a transformation matrix.
\item \textbf{Data Analysis}. We live in an era in which it is easy to collect so much 
data that it is difficult to make sense of the data by just looking at it. You 
can represent the data in matrix form and then find a solution vector. For 
example, scientists use this technique to figure out the effectiveness of drug 
treatments on disease.
\item \textbf{Economics}. Take a look at financial section of any news organization and 
you will see headlines such as "Economic Data Points to Faster Growth" or "Is 
the Inflation Battle Won?" Economists can use systems of linear equations to 
represent economic indicators, such as consumer consumption, government 
spending, investment rate, and gross national product. By using various methods 
that you will learn about later, they can get a good idea of the state of the 
economy.
\item \textbf{Engineering}. Engineers could not do without linear algebra. For example, 
the orbital dynamics of space travel relies on it. Engineers must predict and 
calculate the motion of planetary bodies, satellites, and spacecraft. By 
solving systems of linear equations engineers can make sure that a spacecraft 
travels to its destination without running into a satellite or space rock.
\end{itemize}

\section{Let us Observe the Sun!}
In 2023, India launched the Aditya-L1 spacecraft to study the Sun. 
Without a thorough understanding of linear algebra (among other things), the 
engineers would not have accomplished the amazing feat of getting Aditya in a 
stable orbit around a Lagrange point. 

In previous chapters you learned about gravity and its effects. A \index{Lagrange point}Lagrange point is a spot where the pulls of two bodies
(here, the Earth and the Sun) balance so that a spacecraft can keep pace
with the Earth around the Sun. Aditya-L1 went to L1, about 1.5 million km
from Earth toward the Sun. With the right trajectory, a 
spacecraft will orbit around a Lagrange point in a stable position that 
does not require much energy to maintain. This is called a \index{Halo orbit}halo orbit. Because 
there are no fueling stations in space, a Halo orbit will allow Aditya to 
maintain position for about 5 years. Pretty good mileage!

\section{Exercise}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Linear or Not?}, label=la_linear]
An operation $T$ is linear if $T(a + b) = T(a) + T(b)$ and $T(ca) = cT(a)$
for all inputs $a$, $b$ and numbers $c$. Which of these operations on numbers
are linear?
\begin{enumerate}
\item $T(x) = 3x$
\item $T(x) = 3x + 1$
\item $T(x) = x^2$
\item $T(x) = -x$
\end{enumerate}
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=la_linear]
\begin{enumerate}
\item Linear: $3(a + b) = 3a + 3b$ and $3(ca) = c(3a)$.
\item Not linear: $T(1 + 1) = 7$, but $T(1) + T(1) = 8$. (It is affine.)
\item Not linear: $T(1 + 1) = 4$, but $T(1) + T(1) = 2$.
\item Linear. (This is a reflection.)
\end{enumerate}
A quick test: a linear operation always sends 0 to 0. That alone rules out
$3x + 1$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Matrix Operations in Python}, label=la_matrices]
Using NumPy, create the matrices $f = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ and $g = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Compute $f + g$, the matrix product $f g$, and the transpose $f^T$.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=la_matrices]
\begin{minted}{python}
import numpy as np

f = np.array([[1, 2], [3, 4]])
g = np.array([[0, 1], [1, 0]])
print(f + g)
print(f @ g)
print(f.T)
\end{minted}
The output is:
\begin{minted}{text}
[[1 3]
 [4 4]]
[[2 1]
 [4 3]]
[[1 3]
 [2 4]]
\end{minted}
\end{Answer}

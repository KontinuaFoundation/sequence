\chapter{Conic Sections}

Shine a flashlight straight at a wall, and the spot of light is a circle.
Tilt the flashlight, and the circle stretches into an ellipse. Tilt it
more, and the edge of the light opens up into a parabola and then a
hyperbola. You are slicing a cone of light with the flat wall.

Those four curves are the \index{conic sections}\newterm{conic sections}:
the curves you get where a plane cuts through a cone. They show up
everywhere. Planets move in ellipses, satellite dishes are parabolas, and
the path of a comet that visits the sun once and never returns is a
hyperbola.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=7cm]{Conic_Sections.png}
  \caption{A plane slicing a cone. A level cut gives a circle; a tilted cut
  gives an ellipse; a cut parallel to the side of the cone gives a parabola;

\section{Circle}

A \index{circle}circle is the set of all points in a plane at a fixed
distance (the radius $r$) from a fixed point (the center $(h,k)$). The
distance formula turns that sentence straight into an equation:

\begin{equation}
(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2
\end{equation}

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}
  \draw[thick] (0,0) circle (2cm);
  \draw[dashed] (0,0) -- (-2,0) node[midway, above] {$r$};
  \filldraw[red] (0,0) circle (2pt) node[above right] {Center $(h,k)$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\textbf{Worked example.} Find the center and radius of the circle
$x^2 - 6x + y^2 + 4y = 12$.

\textbf{Solution.} Complete the square in $x$ and in $y$. Add 9 and 4 to
both sides:
$$(x^2 - 6x + 9) + (y^2 + 4y + 4) = 12 + 9 + 4$$
$$(x - 3)^2 + (y + 2)^2 = 25$$
The center is $(3, -2)$ and the radius is 5.

\begin{Exercise}[title={Radius of a Circle}, label=conic1]
What are the center and radius of each circle?
\begin{enumerate}
\item $x^2 + y^2 = 4$
\item $(x + 1)^2 + (y - 5)^2 = 49$
\item $x^2 + y^2 + 10y = 0$
\end{enumerate}
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=conic1]
\begin{enumerate}
\item Center $(0, 0)$; $r^2 = 4$, so $r = 2$.
\item Center $(-1, 5)$, radius 7.
\item Add 25 to both sides: $x^2 + (y + 5)^2 = 25$. Center $(0, -5)$,
      radius 5.
\end{enumerate}
\end{Answer}

\section{Ellipse}

An \index{ellipse}ellipse is the set of all points whose distances to two
fixed points, the \newterm{foci}\index{foci}, add up to the same total.
You can draw one: push two thumbtacks into a board, drop a loop of string
over them, pull the string tight with a pencil, and trace all the way
around.

For an ellipse centered at the origin with semi-major axis $a$ (half the
long width) and semi-minor axis $b$ (half the short width), with $a > b$,
the equation is

\begin{equation}
\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1
\end{equation}

The foci sit on the major axis, a distance $c$ from the center, where
$$c^2 = a^2 - b^2$$
When $a = b$, the foci merge at the center and the ellipse is a circle.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}
  \draw[thick] (0,0) ellipse (4cm and 2cm);
  \draw[dashed] (-4,0) -- (4,0) node[anchor=west] {Major Axis};
  \draw[dashed] (0,-2) -- (0,2) node[anchor=south] {Minor Axis};
  \filldraw[black] (-3.464,0) circle (2pt) node[below] {$F_1$};
  \filldraw[black] (3.464,0) circle (2pt) node[below] {$F_2$};
  \filldraw[red] (0,0) circle (2pt) node[above right] {Center};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Where are the thumbtacks?}, label=conic_ellipse]
An ellipse has equation $\frac{x^2}{25} + \frac{y^2}{9} = 1$. How wide and
how tall is it? Where are its foci? How long is the string loop you would
need to draw it?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=conic_ellipse]
$a = 5$ and $b = 3$, so it is 10 wide and 6 tall. $c^2 = 25 - 9 = 16$, so
the foci are at $(\pm 4, 0)$.

At the right end of the ellipse, $(5, 0)$, the distances to the foci are 1
and 9, which add to 10. So the string from tack to pencil to tack is 10
long. The loop also runs straight between the tacks, another 8, so the
loop is 18 long.
\end{Answer}

\section{Hyperbola}

A \index{hyperbola}hyperbola is the set of all points whose distances to
two foci \emph{differ} by the same amount. It comes from slicing a
\emph{double cone} (two cones tip to tip) with a plane steep enough to cut
both cones, so a hyperbola has two separate branches.

A hyperbola centered at the origin that opens left and right has the
equation

\begin{equation}
\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1
\end{equation}

Swap the roles of $x$ and $y$, and it opens up and down:

\begin{equation}
\frac{y^2}{a^2} - \frac{x^2}{b^2} = 1
\end{equation}

Far from the center, the branches hug two straight lines called
\index{asymptote}asymptotes. For the left-right hyperbola, they are
$y = \pm \frac{b}{a}x$. The foci are a distance $c$ from the center, where
$c^2 = a^2 + b^2$.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.7]
  \draw[->] (-5,0) -- (5,0) node[right] {$x$};
  \draw[->] (0,-3.5) -- (0,3.5) node[above] {$y$};
  \draw[dashed, gray] (-5,-3.75) -- (5,3.75);
  \draw[dashed, gray] (-5,3.75) -- (5,-3.75);
  \draw[thick, sdkblue, domain=-1.6:1.6, samples=60, variable=\t]
    plot ({2*cosh(\t)}, {1.5*sinh(\t)});
  \draw[thick, sdkblue, domain=-1.6:1.6, samples=60, variable=\t]
    plot ({-2*cosh(\t)}, {1.5*sinh(\t)});
  \filldraw[black] (2.5,0) circle (2pt) node[below] {$F_2$};
  \filldraw[black] (-2.5,0) circle (2pt) node[below] {$F_1$};
\end{tikzpicture}

The hyperbola $\frac{x^2}{4} - \frac{y^2}{2.25} = 1$ with its asymptotes
$y = \pm 0.75x$ (dashed) and foci $(\pm 2.5, 0)$.
\end{center}

\section{Parabola}

A \index{parabola}parabola is the set of all points that are the same
distance from a fixed point (the \newterm{focus}) and a fixed line (the
\index{directrix}\newterm{directrix}). A parabola that opens up or down
has the equation

\begin{equation}
y = a(x - h)^2 + k
\end{equation}

and one that opens left or right has the equation

\begin{equation}
x = a(y - k)^2 + h
\end{equation}

where $(h,k)$ is the \newterm{vertex}, the turning point. The focus is
inside the curve, a distance $p = \frac{1}{4|a|}$ from the vertex, and the
directrix is the same distance away on the other side.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}
  \draw[thick] (0,0) parabola (4,4);
  \draw[thick] (0,0) parabola (-4,4);
  \filldraw[red] (0,0) circle (2pt) node[below] {Vertex $(h,k)$};
  \filldraw[black] (0,1) circle (2pt) node[right] {Focus};
  \draw[dashed] (-4,-1) -- (4,-1) node[right] {Directrix};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

Here is the parabola's superpower. Every ray of light that comes in
parallel to the axis bounces off the curve straight to the focus. That is
why a satellite dish is a parabola, with the receiver at the focus. Run it
backward, and a bulb at the focus of a flashlight's mirror sends out a
straight beam.

\textbf{Worked example.} Where is the focus of $y = x^2$?

\textbf{Solution.} Here $a = 1$ and the vertex is $(0,0)$, so
$p = \frac{1}{4}$. The focus is at $\left(0, \frac{1}{4}\right)$ and the
directrix is $y = -\frac{1}{4}$. Check with the point $(1, 1)$: its
distance to the directrix is $1 + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{5}{4}$, and its
distance to the focus is $\sqrt{1^2 + \left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^2} =
\sqrt{\frac{25}{16}} = \frac{5}{4}$. They match.

\begin{Exercise}[title={Where does the receiver go?}, label=conic_dish]
A satellite dish has a cross section shaped like $y = \frac{1}{8}x^2$,
measured in decimeters. How far above the bottom of the dish should the
receiver be mounted?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=conic_dish]
$a = \frac{1}{8}$, so $p = \frac{1}{4 \cdot \frac{1}{8}} = 2$. Mount the
receiver at the focus, 2 decimeters (20 cm) above the bottom of the dish.
\end{Answer}

Only the parabolas that open up or down are graphs of functions. Circles,
ellipses, hyperbolas, and sideways parabolas all fail the
\index{vertical line test}vertical line test, so we describe them with
equations instead.

\begin{Exercise}[title={Name that conic}, label=conic_name]
Name the conic each equation describes:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $4x^2 + 4y^2 = 36$
\item $4x^2 + 9y^2 = 36$
\item $4x^2 - 9y^2 = 36$
\item $y = 4x^2 - 9$
\end{enumerate}
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=conic_name]
\begin{enumerate}
\item Circle: dividing by 4 gives $x^2 + y^2 = 9$, radius 3.
\item Ellipse: dividing by 36 gives $\frac{x^2}{9} + \frac{y^2}{4} = 1$.
\item Hyperbola: dividing by 36 gives $\frac{x^2}{9} - \frac{y^2}{4} = 1$.
\item Parabola: only $x$ is squared.
\end{enumerate}
\end{Answer}

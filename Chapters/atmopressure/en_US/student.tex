\chapter{Atmospheric Pressure}

Right now, the air is pressing on the palm of your hand with a force of
about 1,000 newtons, the weight of a 100 kg sack of rocks. You do not
feel a thing. This chapter explains why, and how that invisible push
lifts juice up a straw, empties a fish tank, and flushes a toilet.

The air you breathe is a blend of gases:
\begin{enumerate}
\item 78\% nitrogen in the form of $N_2$
\item 21\% oxygen in the form $O_2$
\item 1\% other gases (mostly argon)
\end{enumerate}

Fill a balloon with helium ($He$), and the helium pushes outward on every
bit of the balloon's skin. That push, spread over an area, is
\newterm{pressure}\index{pressure}: force divided by area. Force is measured
in newtons, and pressure in \newterm{pascals}. A pascal\index{pascal} is 1
newton per square meter.

The air outside is pushing back on the balloon, too. This is
\newterm{atmospheric pressure} (also called \newterm{barometric
pressure}). It comes from gravity pulling down on all the air above
us.\index{barometric pressure}\index{atmospheric pressure}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{balloon.png}
    \caption{Air pushes both ways on a balloon.}
    \label{fig:balloon}
\end{figure}


Picture a square meter of ground at sea level, and the column of air above
it reaching all the way to the top of the atmosphere.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{aircolumn.png}
    \caption{The column of air above you has a lot of weight.}
    \label{fig:aircolumn}
\end{figure}

The air in that column has a mass of about 10,340 kg, more than a loaded
school bus. Each kilogram weighs 9.8 N, so the column pushes down on its
square meter with $10{,}340 \times 9.8 \approx 101{,}332$ N. The
atmospheric pressure at sea level is about 101,332 pascals, or 101.3
kilopascals (kPa).

That is a lot of pressure! Why does your rib cage not collapse? Because
the air \emph{inside} your lungs pushes out just as hard as the air outside
pushes in. The two forces cancel, so you never notice them.

You can see the push when the balance breaks. Suck the air out of an empty
plastic water bottle, and the atmosphere crushes it.

\begin{Exercise}[title={The push on your hand}, label=atm_palm]
Your palm has an area of about 0.01 square meters (10 cm by 10 cm). What
force does the atmosphere exert on it at sea level? What mass would weigh
that much?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=atm_palm]
$F = pA = (101{,}332)(0.01) \approx 1{,}013$ N. A mass of
$\frac{1{,}013}{9.8} \approx 103$ kg weighs that much. The air pushes on
the back of your hand just as hard, so the forces cancel.
\end{Answer}

\section{Altitude and Atmospheric Pressure}

Let go of the balloon. As it rises, there is less and less air above it,
so the atmospheric pressure on it drops. (The helium inside pushes the
balloon bigger and bigger, until it pops.) 
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{balloonColumn.png}
    \caption{As a balloon rises, it reaches a point of less and less outer pressure.}
    \label{fig:balloonColumn}
\end{figure}

What would be the atmospheric pressure at $h$ meters above sea level?  Here is a handy \index{barometric formula}formula for that:

$$p = 101,332 \times \left(1 - \left( 2.25577 \times 10^{-5} \times h\right) \right)^{5.25588}$$

where $p$ is the atmospheric pressure in pascals.

\begin{Exercise}[title={Atmospheric Pressure},  label=atmos_pressure]
  
You are thinking about riding your bicycle to the top of Mount Everest.
You worry that when the pressure outside the tire drops, the tire will
burst. (A bursting tire is very, very loud.)

Calculate the atmospheric pressure at the top of Mount Everest (8,849
meters above sea level). How much does the outside pressure on the tire
drop?

\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=atmos_pressure]

$$p = 101,332 \times \left(1 - 2.25577 \times 10^{-5} \times h\right)^{5.25588}$$

and $h = 8{,}849$. Thus,

$$p \approx 31.4 \text{ kPa}$$

That is less than a third of the pressure at sea level. The pressure
outside the tire drops by about $101.3 - 31.4 \approx 70$ kPa, so the tire
pushes outward 70 kPa harder than before. A road bike tire is pumped to
about 600 kPa above the outside air, so this is a big change, but most
tires can take it. Climbers' bigger worry is breathing: each breath holds
less than a third as much oxygen.

\end{Answer}

\section{How a Drinking Straw Works}

When you suck on a straw, why does the drink rise? You might say you are
pulling it up. Actually, the atmosphere is pushing it up. \index{straw!drinking}

Before you sip, the atmosphere presses evenly on the whole surface of the
drink, inside the straw and out, so the surface stays level.

When you suck some air out of the straw,  the pressure on the surface inside the straw drops.  The atmospheric pressure on the surface outside the straw pushes into the straw and the beverage rises.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{straw.png}
    \caption{You use pressure to counteract gravity in a straw.}
    \label{fig:straw}
\end{figure}

Gravity still pulls down on the liquid in the straw. The higher the liquid
rises, the more liquid there is to hold up, and the harder you must
suck.

\subsection{The Longest Usable Straw}
  
Assuming you are drinking water in a place with 100 kPa of atmospheric pressure, how high could 
you suck water with a perfect vacuum?  That is,  given a very, very long and very, very stiff drinking straw,  if you created a pressure of 0 Pa inside,  how far above the surface of the glass 
could you get the water?

Let us say a cross-section of the straw has an area of $a$ square meters, and the very top of the 
column of water is $h$ meters above the surface in the glass. 

\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{tallStraw.png}


With how many newtons of force is the atmosphere pushing the water upward?  100 kPa = 100,000 newtons per square meter.  So:

$$F_u = (100,000)a$$

With how many newtons of force is gravity pulling the water in the straw downward?  The volume of the water is $ah$.  A cubic meter of water has a mass of 1000 kg.  The force of gravity is 9.8 Newtons per kg.

$$F_d = (ah)(1000)(9.8)$$

The water will stop rising when $F_u = F_d$.  So, to find $h$, we substitute in:

$$(100,000)a = (ah)(1000)(9.8)$$

Notice that we can divide both sides by $a$ getting:

$$h = \frac{100,000}{9,800} = 10.2 \text{ meters}$$

Even a perfect vacuum could lift water only 10.2 meters, about three
stories. That is why old-fashioned hand pumps cannot raise water from a
well deeper than about 10 meters: they work by suction, and the atmosphere
can only push so hard. Deep wells need a pump at the bottom that pushes
the water up.

\subsection{Millimeters Mercury}

The density of mercury is 13,500 kg per cubic meter.  How far up a straw would a perfect vacuum pull mercury? \index{millimeters mercury}

$$h = \frac{100,000}{(9.8)(13,500)} \approx 0.756 \text{ meters}$$

When the atmospheric pressure is 100kPa,  the mercury will rise 756 mm into a vacuum.

\index{barometer}This is how a classic \newterm{barometer} measures
atmospheric pressure. Fill a long glass tube with mercury, close one end,
and stand it upside down, open end in a dish of mercury. The mercury drops
until the atmosphere can hold it up. Read the height of the column off the
millimeter marks. At sea level, it is about 760 mm, which is why weather
reports sometimes give pressure in ``millimeters of mercury.''

\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{barometer.png}


Atmospheric pressure rises and falls with the weather. Falling pressure
often means a storm is on the way; rising pressure usually brings clear
skies. A weather fan with a barometer at home might say, ``The barometer
went from 752 to 761 millimeters in the last hour. A high-pressure system
is moving in.''

\begin{Exercise}[title={A water barometer}, label=atm_water_baro]
Why do barometers use mercury instead of water? How tall would a water
barometer's column be at a sea-level pressure of 101,332 Pa?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=atm_water_baro]
$h = \frac{101{,}332}{(1000)(9.8)} \approx 10.3$ meters. A water barometer
would be taller than a three-story building. Mercury is 13.5 times as
dense, so its column is 13.5 times shorter, small enough to fit on a
wall.
\end{Answer}


\section{How a Siphon Works}

Put two cups on a table, one full of water and one empty, and connect them
with an empty upside-down U of tubing. Nothing happens. The air pressure on
both ends is the same, and climbing over the hump would take energy.

Now fill the tube with water first. Gravity pulls the water down on both
sides of the hump. If the outlet side is longer (it goes lower), it pulls
harder. As that water falls, it leaves behind lower pressure at the top of
the hump, and the atmosphere pushing on the full cup shoves more water up
the short side to fill the gap.

The flow keeps itself going. Water runs from the full cup to the empty one
until their surfaces are at the same level. Then the two sides balance, and
the flow stops.

This is a \newterm{siphon}\index{siphon}. Gravity drives it, but the
atmosphere makes it possible. On a planet with gravity but no atmosphere,
the water at the top of the hump would just break apart, and the siphon
would fail.

Siphons are handy for emptying containers too big to tip, like a fish
tank. Put one end of a hose in the tank, fill the hose with water (by
dunking it all the way under, not by sucking on it), cover the end with your
thumb, and lower that end into a bucket below the tank. Let go, and the
tank drains into the bucket. (Never start a siphon by mouth with gasoline
or any other chemical. Swallowing even a little gasoline can kill you.)

Siphons follow two rules:
\begin{itemize}
\item The peak of the siphon needs to be low enough for atmospheric pressure to push the liquid that high.  For water at sea-level, for example,  the peak of the siphon cannot be more than 10.2 meters above the surface of the source liquid. 

\item  The tube has to carry the liquid to a lower level than the surface of the source liquid.   If the destination end of the siphon is submerged,  the surface of the liquid it is submerged in must be lower than the surface of the source liquid.  If the destination end of the siphon is not submerged,  its opening must be lower than the surface of the source liquid.
\end{itemize}

Follow those two rules, and you can get creative. In fact, every toilet
has a siphon in it.

\section{How a Toilet Works}

First, a word about P-traps. Look under a sink, and you will see the drain
pipe dip down and curve back up. This is a \newterm{P-trap}\index{P-trap}.
Water stays trapped in the dip and blocks stinky (and flammable!) sewer
gases from coming up into your house.

\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{ptrap1.png}
\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{ptrap2.png}


If a drain that has not been used in a while smells like sewage, the
water in its trap has probably evaporated. Run some water to refill it.

Now, the toilet. The bowl drains through a curved tube that rises and
then falls into the sewer: a siphon. Most of the time, that tube is
partly full of air. When you flush, water rushes from the tank into the
bowl and fills the tube. The siphon starts and sucks the bowl nearly
empty, until air gets into the tube with a gurgle and the flow stops. That
gurgle is the siphon breaking.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{toilet.png}
    \caption{The steps a toilet takes when siphoning out waste.}
    \label{fig:toiletSteps}
\end{figure}


The tank is simple. A float rides on the water. When the water level
drops, the float drops and opens a valve that refills the tank. When the
water is high enough, the float closes the valve. So the tank always holds
the same amount of water, ready for the next flush.

When you flush, a flap in the bottom of the tank opens and the water rushes
into the bowl. When the tank is empty, the flap closes and the tank
refills.

\begin{Exercise}[title={Will it siphon?}, label=atm_siphon]
You want to siphon water from a pond over a wall into a field. The top of
the wall is 12 meters above the pond's surface, and the field is 15 meters
below the pond. Will the siphon work? Explain using the two rules.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=atm_siphon]
No. The outlet is lower than the pond, so the second rule is satisfied. But
the hump is 12 meters above the pond's surface, and the atmosphere can push
water up only about 10.2 meters. The water column will break at the top,
and the siphon will not run.
\end{Answer}

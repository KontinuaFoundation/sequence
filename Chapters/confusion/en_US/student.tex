\chapter{Evaluating Classification Systems}

A model that predicts ``no one has the flu'' is right most days in July. That
does not make it a good model. To judge a classifier, you need to count not just
how often it is right, but \emph{how} it is wrong. This chapter shows you how,
first for a single yes-or-no decision rule and then across every rule the
classifier could use.

\section{The Confusion Matrix}\index{confusion matrix}
A \newterm{confusion matrix} compares a classifier's predictions with the true classes.
For a binary problem, one class is called positive and the other negative. The four cells are:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|}
\hline
 & Actual Positive & Actual Negative \\
\hline
Predicted Positive & True Positive (TP) & False Positive (FP) \\
\hline
Predicted Negative & False Negative (FN) & True Negative (TN) \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A false positive is a false alarm. A false negative is a missed case.

\section{Performance Metrics}\index{accuracy}\index{precision}\index{recall}
Each metric answers a different question:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Accuracy} is the fraction of all predictions that are correct:
$\frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN}$.
\item \textbf{Precision} is the fraction of positive predictions that are correct:
$\frac{TP}{TP + FP}$.
\item \textbf{Recall} (also called sensitivity) is the fraction of actual positives that are found:
$\frac{TP}{TP + FN}$.
\item \textbf{Specificity} is the fraction of actual negatives that are correctly identified:
$\frac{TN}{TN + FP}$.
\item \textbf{$F_1$ score} is the harmonic mean of precision and recall:
$F_1 = \frac{2 \cdot \text{Precision} \cdot \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}}$.
\end{itemize}

\section{A Worked Example}
A screening test is run on 200 people. The results are $TP = 40$, $FP = 10$,
$FN = 20$, and $TN = 130$. Then:
\begin{itemize}
\item Accuracy $= \frac{40 + 130}{200} = 0.85$.
\item Precision $= \frac{40}{50} = 0.80$. Of the 50 people flagged, 40 were truly positive.
\item Recall $= \frac{40}{60} \approx 0.667$. The test found 40 of the 60 true positives.
\item Specificity $= \frac{130}{140} \approx 0.929$.
\item $F_1 = \frac{2(0.80)(0.667)}{0.80 + 0.667} \approx 0.727$.
\end{itemize}
The accuracy looks good, but the test misses one third of the people who have the condition.
For this reason, accuracy alone can hide a poor classifier, especially when one class is rare.

\section{Choosing a Metric}
The right metric depends on the cost of each error. For a screening test, a missed case
(a false negative) is often the more serious error, so recall matters most. For a spam
filter, a false positive means a real email is lost, so precision matters most. The
$F_1$ score balances the two, and it is useful when both matter. Think of a smoke detector. A false positive means burnt toast sets off the alarm.
A false negative means a real fire goes unnoticed. The second error is far worse,
so smoke detectors are built for high recall, and we put up with the occasional
toast alarm.

\section{Beyond a Single Threshold}\index{threshold}
A weather app that says ``70\% chance of rain'' has not decided anything yet.
You decide: maybe you bring an umbrella above 50\%, while your friend waits for
80\%. Same forecast, different thresholds, different mistakes.

Many classifiers work the same way. They output a score, such as a
probability, and predict the positive class when the score is above a
threshold. Every number in the previous sections, from accuracy to $F_1$,
comes from one confusion matrix, and that matrix depends on the threshold.
Change the threshold and you get a new matrix. The measures below look across
\emph{all} thresholds at once, so they grade the forecast itself.

\subsection{The Precision--Recall Tradeoff}\index{precision-recall curve}
Lowering the threshold catches more true positives, so recall rises. It also flags more false positives, so precision
usually falls. Raising the threshold does the opposite. A \newterm{precision--recall curve} plots precision against
recall as the threshold changes. It is most informative when positives are rare, because accuracy then favors
predicting negative.

\subsection{The ROC Curve and AUC}\index{ROC curve}\index{AUC}
The \newterm{receiver operating characteristic} (ROC) curve plots the true positive rate (recall) against the false
positive rate $\frac{FP}{FP + TN}$ as the threshold changes. A perfect classifier passes through the top-left corner.
A random guess lies on the diagonal. Back to the smoke detector: a sensitive
setting rings at every candle (high true positive rate, but also a high false
positive rate), and a dull setting misses small fires. Each setting of the
threshold gives one point on the ROC curve.

The \newterm{area under the ROC curve} (AUC) summarizes the curve in one number. There is an equivalent meaning:
the AUC is the probability that a randomly chosen positive example gets a higher score than a randomly chosen
negative example. Ties count as half.

\subsubsection{A Worked Example}
Two positive examples have scores $0.9$ and $0.6$. Three negative examples have scores $0.7$, $0.2$, and $0.1$.
There are $2 \times 3 = 6$ pairs. The positive $0.9$ beats all three negatives, which is 3 pairs. The positive $0.6$
beats $0.2$ and $0.1$, which is 2 pairs, and loses to $0.7$. So $5$ of the $6$ pairs are correctly ordered, and
$$\text{AUC} = \frac{5}{6} \approx 0.833$$
You can also see this as an area. Sweep the threshold down through the sorted
scores $0.9$ (P), $0.7$ (N), $0.6$ (P), $0.2$ (N), $0.1$ (N). Each positive moves
the curve up by $\frac{1}{2}$ and each negative moves it right by $\frac{1}{3}$:
$$(0,0) \to (0, \tfrac{1}{2}) \to (\tfrac{1}{3}, \tfrac{1}{2}) \to (\tfrac{1}{3}, 1) \to (\tfrac{2}{3}, 1) \to (1, 1)$$
The area under this staircase is $\frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{2} + \frac{2}{3}\cdot 1 = \frac{5}{6}$, the same answer.

\section{Which Measure to Use?}
When classes are balanced, ROC and AUC are reasonable summaries. When positives are rare, the precision--recall
curve is more informative, because the false positive rate stays small even when there are many false positives.
Always report the threshold used for a final decision, since no single number describes every threshold.

\begin{Exercise}[label=conf1, title=Compute the metrics]
A classifier has $TP = 30$, $FP = 5$, $FN = 15$, and $TN = 50$. Compute the accuracy and the precision.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=conf1]
Accuracy $= \frac{30 + 50}{100} = 0.80$. Precision $= \frac{30}{35} \approx 0.857$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=conf2, title=Which error matters?]
A test detects a dangerous disease. Which metric should be the main goal, and why?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=conf2]
Recall (sensitivity). A false negative means a sick person is told they are healthy,
which is the more serious error in screening.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=conf3, title=Accuracy can mislead]
In a dataset where 95\% of cases are negative, a classifier always predicts negative. What is its accuracy, and what is its recall?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=conf3]
Accuracy is 0.95, which sounds high. Recall is $\frac{0}{TP + FN} = 0$, because it
finds none of the positive cases.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=prc1, title=Compute the AUC]
Positive scores are $0.8$, $0.4$. Negative scores are $0.5$, $0.3$. Find the AUC.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=prc1]
There are 4 pairs. $0.8$ beats both negatives (2 pairs). $0.4$ beats $0.3$ but loses to $0.5$ (1 pair).
So 3 of 4 pairs are correct, and the AUC is $\frac{3}{4} = 0.75$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=prc3, title=Moving the threshold]
Use the scores from the ROC worked example: positives $0.9$ and $0.6$, negatives
$0.7$, $0.2$, and $0.1$. Predict positive when the score is at least the threshold.
For a threshold of $0.5$ and then $0.8$, find TP, FP, FN, and TN, along with the
precision, the recall, and the false positive rate.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=prc3]
Threshold $0.5$: the scores $0.9$, $0.7$, and $0.6$ are predicted positive, so
$TP = 2$, $FP = 1$, $FN = 0$, $TN = 2$. Precision $= \frac{2}{3}$, recall $= 1$,
and the false positive rate $= \frac{1}{3}$.

Threshold $0.8$: only $0.9$ is predicted positive, so $TP = 1$, $FP = 0$, $FN = 1$,
$TN = 3$. Precision $= 1$, recall $= \frac{1}{2}$, and the false positive rate $= 0$.

Raising the threshold made the classifier more careful: precision went up and
recall went down. The two thresholds give the ROC points $(\frac{1}{3}, 1)$ and
$(0, \frac{1}{2})$, both on the staircase in the worked example.
\end{Answer}

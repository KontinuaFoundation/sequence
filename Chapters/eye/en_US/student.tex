\chapter{How Eyes Work}
\index{eye}
You have been carrying two cameras around your whole life. They focus
automatically, adjust to sunlight a billion times brighter than
starlight, and fix themselves when they get scratched. In this chapter, we
will see how they work, what goes wrong with them, and why your TV only
needs three colors to fool them.

Dr. Craig Blackwell has made a great video on the mechanics of the eye:
\url{https://youtu.be/Z8asc2SfFHM}

Mechanically, your eye works a lot like the camera from the last chapter.
The eye is a sphere with two lenses on the front. The outer lens is called
the \index{eye!cornea}\newterm{cornea}, and it does most of the bending of
light. The second lens is simply called ``the lens.'' Tiny muscles squeeze
or relax the lens to change its shape, which fine-tunes the focus for near
or far objects. (A camera does this by moving its lens instead.)
\index{eye!iris}
\index{eye!pupil}
Between the two lenses is an aperture that opens wide when there is
very little light, and closes very small when there is bright light.
The opening is called the \newterm{pupil} and the tissue that forms
the pupil is called the \newterm{iris}. When people talk about the color
of your eyes, they are talking about the color of your iris. The
blackness at the center of your iris is your pupil.

\index{rods and cones}There are two types of photoreceptor cells in your retina: rods and
cones. The rods are more sensitive; in very dark conditions, most of
our vision is provided by the rods. The cones are used when there is
plenty of light, and they let us see colors.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{conerod.png}
    \caption{Rods and cones, the light-sensing cells of the retina.}
    \label{fig:conerod}
\end{figure}

\index{eye!sclera}
The white part around the outside of the eyeball? That is called the
\newterm{sclera}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{eyeDiagram.png}
    \caption{The eye works just like a camera's lens.}
    \label{fig:eyeDiagram}

\index{eye!retina}
The walls of the eye are lined inside with the \newterm{retina}, which has 
sensors that pick up the light and send impulses down the optic
nerve to your brain.

Just like a camera, the image is flipped upside down when it lands on the
back of the eye (see figure~\ref{fig:eyeDiagram}). Your brain flips it back.

Where the optic nerve leaves the eye, there are no light sensors at all.
Every eye has this \newterm{blind spot}, but you never notice it, because
your brain fills in the gap with whatever is around it.

\section{Eye problems}



Now that you know the mechanics of the eye, let us go over a few
things that commonly go wrong with the eye.

\subsection{Glaucoma}
\index{eye!glaucoma}
The space between your cornea and lens is filled with a fluid called
\newterm{aqueous humor}. To feed the cells of the cornea and lens,
the \index{aqueous humor}aqueous humor carries oxygen and nutrients like blood would, but unlike blood,
it is transparent so you can see. Aqueous humor is constantly being
pumped into and out of that chamber. If aqueous humor has trouble
exiting, the pressure builds up and can damage the eye. This is known
as \newterm{glaucoma}. See Figure~\ref{fig:cataractGlaucoma}.

\subsection{Cataracts}
\index{eye!cataract}
The lens should be clear. As a person ages, the proteins in the lens break down and clump
together, becoming opaque. This can also be accelerated by diabetes, too much exposure to sunlight, obesity, and high blood pressure. 
From the outside, the eye will look cloudy. 
This is called a \newterm{cataract}, and it makes it difficult for the person to see.

This problem can be corrected, however. The person's cloudy lens is removed and
replaced with a clear, manufactured lens.

\subsection{Nearsightedness, farsightedness, and astigmatism}
\index{eye!nearsightedness}\index{eye!farsightedness}\index{eye!myopia}\index{eye!astigmatism}
If you are in a dark room and a tiny LED is turned on, the photons
from that LED can pass through your cornea in many different places.
If your eye is focusing on that light correctly, all the photons
should meet up at the same place on the retina.

Figure~\ref{fig:nearfarSight} shows what happens when they do not.

If the lenses are bending the light too much, the photons meet up before they hit the
retina and get smeared a bit across it. To the person, the LED
would appear blurry. The eye is said to be \newterm{nearsighted} or
\newterm{myopic}. This signals that near objects appear correctly, but farther objects appear blurry. 

If the lenses are not bending it enough, the photons would meet up
behind the retina. Once again, they get smeared a bit across the
retina and the LED looks blurry to the person. The eye is said to be
\newterm{farsighted} or \index{eye!hyperopia}\newterm{hyperopic}. The objects which are distant can appear clearly, while closer objects are heavily blurred. 

Your lenses are supposed to bend the photons the same amount
vertically and horizontally. If one dimension is focused, but the
other is myopic or hyperopic, the eye is said to have \newterm{astigmatism}.

Myopia, hyperopia, and astigmatism can be corrected with glasses or contact
lenses. A nearsighted eye bends light too much, so its glasses use
\emph{diverging} lenses that spread the light a little before it enters
the eye. A farsighted eye needs \emph{converging} lenses for extra
bending. Doctors can also do surgical corrections, usually by reshaping
the cornea with a laser.

Eye doctors describe lenses by their \newterm{power} in
\newterm{diopters}: $P = \frac{1}{f}$, with $f$ in meters. Converging
lenses have positive power, and diverging lenses have negative power.

\textbf{Worked example.} A pair of reading glasses has converging lenses
with a focal length of 0.5 m. What is their power?

\textbf{Solution.} $P = \frac{1}{0.5} = +2$ diopters. A nearsighted person's
prescription might read $-2.5$, meaning diverging lenses with
$f = -0.4$ m.

\section{Seeing colors}
\index{color}
TED-Ed has made a good video on how we see color. Watch it here: \url{https://youtu.be/l8_fZPHasdo}

\index{rainbow}When a rainbow forms, you are seeing different wavelengths separating from each other. In the rainbow:
\begin{itemize}
\item Red is about 650 nm.
\item Orange is about 600 nm.
\item Yellow is about 580 nm.
\item Green is about 550 nm.
\item Cyan is about 500 nm.
\item Blue is about 450 nm.
\item Violet is about 400 nm.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{Color_Waves.png}
    \caption{All colors have different wavelengths.}
    \label{fig:colorwavelengths}
\end{figure}

If you shine a light with a wavelength of 580 nm on a white piece of
paper, you will see yellow.

However, if you shine two lights with wavelengths of 650 nm (red) and
550 nm (green), you will also see yellow.

Why? Our ears can hear two different frequencies at the same time.
Why cannot our eyes see two colors in the same place?

As mentioned above, the cone photoreceptors in our eyes let us see
colors. There are three kinds of cones. Each responds to a wide range of
wavelengths, but each is most sensitive near a different one:
\index{rgb}
\begin{itemize}
  \item ``Red'' (long-wavelength) cones: most sensitive near 560 nm, and
        still responding out to about 700 nm.
  \item ``Green'' (medium-wavelength) cones: most sensitive near 530 nm.
  \item ``Blue'' (short-wavelength) cones: most sensitive near 420 nm.
\end{itemize}
The red and green cones overlap a lot, which is why light between them
excites both.

When a wavelength of 580 nm hits your retina, it excites the red
and green receptors, and your brain interprets that mix as yellow.

Similarly, when light that contains both 650 nm and 550 nm waves hits
your retina, it excites the red and green receptors, and your brain
interprets that mix as yellow.

You cannot tell the difference!

Now we know why the sensors on the camera are RGB. The camera is
recording the scene as closely as necessary to fool your eye.

A TV or a color computer monitor only has three colors of pixels: red,
green, and blue.  By controlling the mix of them, it creates the
sensation of millions of colors. (With 256 levels for each of red, green,
and blue, a screen can make $256^3 \approx 16.7$ million colors.)

\section{Pigments}\index{pigment}

A color printer works in the opposite fashion. Instead of radiating
colors, it puts pigments on the paper that absorb certain frequencies.
A pigment that absorbs only wavelengths near 650 nm (red) will appear
to your eye as cyan. This makes sense, because the sensation of cyan is
created when your blue and green receptors are activated.

Thus, pigment colors come in:
\begin{itemize}
\item Cyan: absorbs wavelengths around red
\item Magenta: absorbs wavelengths around green
\item Yellow: absorbs wavelengths around blue
\end{itemize}

\index{cmyk}
If you buy ink for a color printer, you know there is typically a
fourth ink: black. If you put cyan, magenta, and yellow pigments on
paper, the mix will not absorb all the visible spectrum in a consistent
manner. Our eyes are pretty sensitive to this, so we would see
brown. This is why we add black ink to get pretty grays and blacks.

We call this approach to color CMYK (as opposed to RGB). If an artist
is creating an image to be viewed on a screen, they will typically
make an RGB image.  If they are creating an image to be printed using
pigments, they typically create a CMYK image. (Most of us do not care
so much --- we just let the computer do conversions between the two
color spaces for us.)

\begin{Exercise}[title={Mixing light}, label=eye_mix]
A screen turns on its red and blue pixels at full brightness and leaves
green off. What color do you see? What if a printer puts magenta and
yellow ink on white paper?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=eye_mix]
Red plus blue light looks magenta. Magenta ink absorbs green, and yellow
ink absorbs blue, so only red light is reflected: the paper looks red.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Stronger Lens}, label=eye1]
A corrective lens has focal length $0.25$ m. What is its power in diopters?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=eye1]
$P = \frac{1}{0.25} = 4$ diopters.
\end{Answer}

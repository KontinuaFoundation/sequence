How many people must be in a room before two of them probably share a
birthday? Most people guess around 180. The real answer is 23. Your
intuition is a wonderful tool, but it can be fooled, and the places where
it gets fooled are some of the most interesting places in math and
science.

A \newterm{paradox} is a statement or situation that seems to contradict
itself or defies intuition.\index{paradox} Some paradoxes are real
contradictions, which tell us that some assumption must be wrong. Others
only \emph{feel} impossible, and studying them sharpens your intuition.

Some of the puzzles in this chapter are \newterm{thought
experiments}\index{thought experiment}: imaginary scenarios that test an
idea when a real experiment would be hard or impossible. You have already
used them. Newton's cannon on a mountaintop is a thought experiment, and so
is every frictionless surface in your physics problems.

Paradoxes are also closely tied to \newterm{proof by contradiction}, from
the proofs chapter: assume a statement, show that it leads to a
contradiction, and conclude that the assumption was false. Let us start
with a contradiction you can picture.

\section{The Barber}

\index{Barber's paradox}The Barber's Paradox is a self-referential paradox that arises in set theory and logic.

The paradox is often stated as follows
\begin{theorem}
    In a town, there is a barber who shaves all and only those men who do not shave themselves. The question is: Does the barber shave himself?
\end{theorem}
Well there are two cases to consider:
\begin{itemize}
    \item If the barber shaves himself, then according to the definition, he is now someone who \textit{shaves himself}. The rule says the barber only shaves people who do not shave themselves. This is a contradiction because the barber cannot both shave himself and not shave himself at the same time.
    \item If the barber does not shave himself, then according to the definition, he is someone who does not shave himself. The rule says the barber shaves all those who do not shave themselves. This means the barber must shave himself, which again leads to a contradiction because he cannot both shave himself and not shave himself at the same time.
\end{itemize}
No matter which case you try, you reach a contradiction. That is a proof
by contradiction: assuming such a barber exists leads to nonsense, so no
such barber can exist. Paradoxes like this one often come from statements
that refer to themselves.

\section{Mathematical Paradoxes}
\subsection{Russell's Paradox}\index{Russell's Paradox}\index{set theory}
Russell's Paradox is a paradox within set theory. It arises from the statement that
\begin{theorem}
    Consider the set of all sets that do not contain themselves. Does this set contain itself?
\end{theorem}
Most sets do not contain themselves. The set of all apples is not an
apple. But some sets seem to: the set of all ideas is itself an idea.
For Russell's statement, let us create a master set $R$ that contains only the sets that do not contain themselves. There are two cases:
\begin{itemize}
    \item If $R$ is not a member of itself, then its definition entails that it is a member of itself. $\rightarrow \leftarrow$
    \item If $R$ is a member of itself, then it is not a member of itself, since it is the set of all sets that are not members of themselves. $\rightarrow \leftarrow$
\end{itemize}
Using symbols from our sets chapter,
\[\text{Let } R = \{x \mid x \not \in x\}\text{. Then }  R\in R\iff R\not \in R.\]
This is the barber's paradox in set form. Bertrand Russell found it in
1901, and it shook the foundations of mathematics. Mathematicians fixed it
by making the rules for building sets stricter, so that ``the set of all
sets that do not contain themselves'' is simply not allowed.

\subsection{Simpson's Paradox}
Simpson's Paradox is a phenomenon in statistics where a trend or correlation that appears in several different groups of data can disappear or reverse when the groups are combined. Let us look at an example.\index{Simpson's paradox}

Imagine you are choosing between two hospitals for an operation, and you
compare their survival rates. We have the following data:

\begin{table}[!ht]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{|l|l|l|l|}
        \hline
        \textbf{Hospital} & \textbf{Total Patients} & \textbf{Survived} & \textbf{Survival Rate} \\ \hline
        Hospital A        & 1000                    & 800               & 80\%                   \\ \hline
        Hospital B        & 1000                    & 890               & 89\%                   \\ \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

From this, we see that the same number of patients were treated at both hospitals, but Hospital B has a higher survival rate. Hospital B looks better. However, let us look at the data when separated by those with \textit{high-risk} surgeries versus those with \textit{low-risk} surgeries.

Patients that had low-risk surgeries, or were in good health, are given by following data
\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{|l|l|l|l|}
        \hline
        \textbf{Hospital} & \textbf{Patients} & \textbf{Survived} & \textbf{Survival Rate} \\ \hline
        Hospital A        & 100               & 98                & 98\%                   \\ \hline
        Hospital B        & 900               & 850               & 94.4\%                 \\ \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}
And those with poor health and high-risk surgeries:
\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{|l|l|l|l|}
        \hline
        \textbf{Hospital} & \textbf{Patients} & \textbf{Survived} & \textbf{Survival Rate} \\ \hline
        Hospital A        & 900               & 702               & 78\%                   \\ \hline
        Hospital B        & 100               & 40                & 40\%                   \\ \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Looking at the data based on the health categorization, Hospital A is actually better in both categories. This ``paradox'' occurs because of an unaccounted \textbf{confounding variable}: the incoming health of the patient. Hospital A took on the harder cases (90\% of their patients are high-risk). Hospital B, although it initially was thought to be the better hospital, mainly took on those with good health (90\% were low-risk). You will learn how scientists guard against confounding variables in the Experimental Design chapter.

\subsection{Monty Hall Problem}
This video introduces the \href{https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cXqDIFUB7YU}{Monty Hall Problem}.\index{Monty Hall Problem}

The problem is as follows:
\begin{theorem}
    You are on a game show and are given the choice of three doors. Behind one door is a car; behind the others, goats. You pick a door, say No. 1, and the host, who knows what is behind the doors, opens another door, say No. 3, which has a goat. He then says to you, ``Do you want to switch to door No. 2?'' Is it to your advantage to switch your choice?
\end{theorem}

Two doors are left, so it seems like a 50/50 coin flip. It is not. You
should switch, and switching wins $\frac{2}{3}$ of the time.

Here is why. When you first pick, you have a $\frac{1}{3}$ chance of
picking the car and a $\frac{2}{3}$ chance of picking a goat. Now think
about what the host does. He knows where the car is, and he always opens a
goat door that you did not pick.
\begin{itemize}
\item If you picked the car ($\frac{1}{3}$ of the time), switching loses.
\item If you picked a goat ($\frac{2}{3}$ of the time), the host must open
      the \emph{other} goat door, so the remaining door has the car.
      Switching wins.
\end{itemize}
The host's choice is not random, and that is what gives you information.
When this puzzle appeared in a magazine in 1990, thousands of readers,
including many with math PhDs, wrote in to insist the answer was wrong. It
was right.

\begin{Exercise}[title={Let the computer play}, label=par_monty]
Write a Python program that plays the Monty Hall game 100,000 times with
the always-switch strategy and prints the fraction of wins. Hint: switching
wins exactly when your first pick was not the car.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=par_monty]
\begin{minted}{python}
import random

wins = 0
games = 100_000
for _ in range(games):
    car = random.randint(1, 3)
    pick = random.randint(1, 3)
    if pick != car:   # switching wins when the first pick was a goat
        wins += 1
print(wins / games)
\end{minted}
It prints a number close to 0.667 (for example, 0.6672).
\end{Answer}

The next chapter, Monte Carlo Simulation, plays the full game, with a host who really
opens doors, and uses simulation to explore both of these puzzles further. Later, the Bayes' Theorem
chapter proves the $\frac{2}{3}$ answer with conditional probability.


\subsection{Birthday Problem}
\index{birthday problem}The birthday problem is a famous problem in probability and combinatorics. It goes as follows:
\begin{theorem}
    In a group of $n$ people, what is the probability that at least two people share the same birthday?
\end{theorem}
With just 23 people, the chance that two share a birthday is better than
50\%. (We ignore February 29 and assume every birthday is equally
likely.) Let us prove it.

Let $P(A)$ be the probability that no two of $k$ people share a birthday,
and let $P(B) = 1 - P(A)$ be the probability that at least two do. We want to find the smallest $k$ such that $P(B) > 0.5$, as this constitutes a majority.

If there are only two people in a room(meaning $k=2$), the probability that there does not exist a shared birthday is $P(A) = \tfrac{364}{365}$, meaning the probability that there is a shared birthday is $P(B) = 1 - P(A) = \tfrac{1}{365}$. If there are three people in a room, the probability that there does not exist a shared birthday is $P(A) = \tfrac{364}{365} \cdot \tfrac{363}{365}$, meaning the probability that there is a shared birthday is $P(B) = 1 - P(A) = 1 - \tfrac{364}{365} \cdot \tfrac{363}{365}$.

This pattern continues, and we can generalize the probability that there does not exist a shared birthday for $k$ people as follows:
\[P(A) = \frac{365}{365} \cdot \frac{364}{365} \cdot \frac{363}{365} \cdots \frac{365-k+1}{365} = \prod_{i=0}^{k-1} \frac{365 - i}{365}.\]
Therefore, the probability that there is at least one shared birthday is:
\[P(B) = 1 - P(A) = 1 - \prod_{i=0}^{k-1} \frac{365 - i}{365}.\]

We now seek the smallest $k$ such that
\[
    1-\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{365-i}{365} > 0.5.
\]
Equivalently,
\[
    \prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{365-i}{365} < 0.5.
\]

Computing this product gives
\[
    P(A)\approx 0.5243 \quad \text{when } k=22,
\]
so
\[
    P(B)\approx 1-0.5243=0.4757<0.5.
\]
But for $k=23$,
\[
    P(A)\approx 0.4927,
\]
so
\[
    P(B)\approx 1-0.4927=0.5073>0.5.
\]

So 23 people is the smallest group with a better-than-even chance of a
shared birthday. (Multiplying the probabilities this way works because each person's birthday is
independent of the others. The Discrete Probability and Random Variables chapter explains this multiplication rule
carefully.) Why so few? Because with 23 people there are
$\frac{23 \times 22}{2} = 253$ different \emph{pairs} of people, and any
one of those pairs could match.

\end{Exercise}

About 71\%. With 50 people, it is about 97\%, and with 70 people it is

\subsection{Zeno's Paradoxes}
\index{Zeno's paradoxes}Zeno of Elea was a Greek philosopher, around 450
BCE, famous for thought experiments that question our understanding of
motion, space, and time. Zeno's paradoxes are often used to illustrate the concept of \textit{infinity} and the nature of space and time.

\subsubsection{The Dichotomy Paradox}
His most famous is the \index{dichotomy paradox}\textit{Dichotomy
Paradox}:
\begin{quote}
    That which is in locomotion must arrive at the half-way stage before it arrives at the goal.
\end{quote}
This paradox suggests that before an object can reach its destination, it must first reach the halfway point, and before it can reach the halfway point, it must reach the quarter point, and so on, leading to an infinite number of steps that must be completed before reaching the destination. This seems to imply that motion is impossible, as it would require completing an infinite number of tasks in a finite amount of time.

So then, how is motion possible?

The resolution is that an infinite number of steps can add up to a finite
total if the steps shrink fast enough. (In the calculus volumes, this idea
becomes \newterm{limits} and \newterm{infinite series}.) Each step also
takes less time than the one before, so the total time is finite, too. If we consider the remaining distance to be covered as $1 \, \mathrm{unit}$, the first step is $\frac{1}{2}$, the second step is $\frac{1}{4}$, the third step is $\frac{1}{8}$, and so on. The total distance covered after $n$ steps can be expressed as a \newterm{geometric series}:
\[
    S = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2^n} = 1
\]
For proof's sake, let
$$S = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{8} + \cdots$$
Then, multiplying both sides by $\frac{1}{2}$ gives
$$\frac{1}{2}S = \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{8} + \frac{1}{16} + \cdots$$
$$S - \frac{1}{2}S = \frac{1}{2}$$
$$\frac{1}{2}S = \frac{1}{2}    \quad \implies \quad    S = 1$$

All the infinitely many steps add up to exactly the whole distance.

\begin{Exercise}[title={Another infinite sum}, label=par_zeno]
Use the same trick to find
$S = \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{9} + \frac{1}{27} + \cdots$.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=par_zeno]
Multiply by $\frac{1}{3}$: $\frac{1}{3}S = \frac{1}{9} + \frac{1}{27} +
\cdots$. Subtract: $S - \frac{1}{3}S = \frac{1}{3}$, so $\frac{2}{3}S =
\frac{1}{3}$ and $S = \frac{1}{2}$.
\end{Answer}


\section{Physics and Time Travel Paradoxes}
Now for some physics paradoxes and thought experiments that may mess with
your brain a bit.

\subsection{Schrödinger's Cat}
Erwin Schrödinger was one of the founders of quantum mechanics. In 1935,
in letters with Albert Einstein, he invented a thought experiment to show
how strange the theory becomes when you apply it to everyday objects.

The thought experiment is as follows:
\begin{quote}
    Schrödinger imagined placing a cat in a sealed box with a device triggered by a quantum event (such as the decay of a radioactive atom). The event has a 50\% chance of happening and a 50\% chance of staying dormant. If the event occurs, the cat dies; if not, it lives. However, you do not know whether the cat is alive or not until the box is opened. According to quantum mechanics, until the box is opened, the cat is in a
mix of dead and alive.
\end{quote}
Why? In quantum mechanics, a tiny particle like an electron can be in a
\textbf{superposition} of states: it does not have one definite position
or energy until it is measured. Instead, it is described by a
\emph{wavefunction} that holds all the possibilities at once. This is not
just theory. Fire electrons one at a time at a barrier with two narrow
slits, and the screen behind slowly fills with bright and dark bands, an
interference pattern that only waves make. Each electron somehow goes
through both slits.

The atom in the box is in a superposition of decayed and not decayed. If
the cat's fate is tied to the atom, the cat must be in a superposition too.
Schrödinger thought that was absurd, and that was his point: something
about how quantum rules connect to the everyday world needed explaining.
Physicists still debate what really happens. \index{superposition}\index{Schrödinger's cat}

There is a great TED-Ed video on Schrödinger's cat in your digital
resources.

\subsubsection{Arrow Paradox}
Zeno has another paradox about motion, the \textit{Arrow Paradox}:\index{arrow paradox}
\begin{quote}
    If everything when it occupies an equal space is at rest at that instant of time, and if that which is in locomotion is always occupying such a space at any moment, the flying arrow is therefore motionless at that instant of time and at the next instant of time but if both instants of time are taken as the same instant or continuous instant of time then it is in motion.
\end{quote}

In short: take a snapshot of a flying arrow. In that single instant, it
sits in one spot, not moving. But the flight is made of nothing but
instants. So when does it move?

The resolution is that motion is not something that happens \emph{in} an
instant; it is the change in position \emph{across} instants. Velocity is
the change in position divided by the change in time, and you need two
moments to measure it. Each instant is like a single point on a line: it
has no length, yet the line made of points does. In calculus, you will
learn how to talk about speed \emph{at} an instant, using limits.

\subsection{Time Travel Paradoxes}\index{time travel paradoxes}\index{grandfather paradox}
As far as anyone knows, traveling back in time is impossible, but it shows
up in movies all the time. Time travel raises logical problems because it
lets events change their own causes.

The most well-known example is the \textbf{grandfather paradox}. Suppose a person travels back in time and prevents their grandfather
from ever having children. This would mean the time traveler is never born, as one of the time traveler's parents are never conceived. The time traveler, then, could not have gone back in time to interfere in the first place. The sequence of events becomes logically inconsistent: the same action both must and must not occur. This is a contradiction ($\rightarrow \leftarrow$), as we talked about, and many people use this as an argument to prove that time travel cannot logically exist.

Another time travel paradox is preventing Abraham Lincoln's assassination. Imagine a time traveler goes back to 1865 and prevents Lincoln's assassination. At first glance, this seems like a straightforward intervention. However, if Lincoln survives, the historical conditions that motivated the time traveler to go back, such as learning about the assassination in the first place, no longer exist. This creates a contradiction: if Lincoln was never assassinated, why would the traveler have made the journey to stop it?


More generally, these are consistency paradoxes, where altering the past creates contradictions in the timeline. In physics, these scenarios contradict our existing knowledge, meaning either constraints exist to preserve consistency, or our understanding of time and causality is incomplete.

A M\"obius strip is a surface with only one side and only one edge. You
can make one right now: take a strip of paper, give it a half twist, and
tape the ends together. Draw a line down the middle without lifting your
pen. You will cover \emph{both} sides of the paper, traveling twice the
length of the strip, before you return to where you started. There is no
inside or outside.

Now try this: cut along the line you drew. You might expect two strips.
You get one long strip with two full twists. (Try cutting \emph{that} one
down the middle, too.)

If you are curious, the mobius strip is defined by the following parametric equations:
$$\mathbf{r}(u,v) =
    \bigl(
    (1 + v\cos \tfrac{u}{2})\cos u,\;
    (1 + v\cos \tfrac{u}{2})\sin u,\;
    v\sin \tfrac{u}{2}
    \bigr)$$
where $u$ ranges from $0$ to $2\pi$ and $v$ ranges from $-1$ to $1$. Do not worry about this yet; you will learn parametric equations in a
later volume.
\subsection{Coastline paradox}
How long is the coast of Britain? It depends on your ruler. That is the
\newterm{coastline paradox}\index{coastline paradox}. Measure with a
100 km ruler, and you skip over every bay. Use a 1 km ruler, and you follow
the bays, but skip the coves. Use a 1 m ruler, and you trace every rock.
Each smaller ruler finds more wiggles, so the measured length keeps
growing.

Lewis Fry Richardson noticed this in the 1950s while studying whether the
length of a border between two countries affects their chances of going
to war. He found that Spain and Portugal reported very different lengths
for their shared border. Neither was wrong: they had used different
rulers. For a smooth curve, shrinking the ruler makes the measurement
settle down to one answer. For a coastline, it never settles; the length
just keeps growing.


In calculus, you will learn to find the exact length of smooth curves. A
coastline is not smooth: zoom in, and it looks just as wiggly as before.
Shapes like this are called \newterm{fractals}, and mathematicians measure
them by how quickly their length grows as the ruler shrinks, a number
called the \emph{fractal dimension}.

\subsection{Moravec's Paradox}
\index{Moravec's paradox}\newterm{Hans Moravec}, a pioneer in robotics and Artificial Intelligence, wrote in 1988,
\begin{quote}
    it is comparatively easy to make computers exhibit adult level performance on intelligence tests or playing checkers, and difficult or [near] impossible to give them the skills of a one-year-old when it comes to perception and mobility
\end{quote}

This is \newterm{Moravec's paradox}: tasks that are \emph{hard} for
humans are often \emph{easy} for machines, and tasks that are \emph{easy}
for humans are often \emph{hard} for machines.

Chess is the classic example. We think of chess as a sign of great
intelligence, yet in 1997 IBM's Deep Blue beat the world champion, Garry
Kasparov. Multiplying huge numbers, searching millions of possibilities,
and solving many kinds of equations turned out to be easy for computers,
too. But for decades, no robot could fold laundry, walk across a messy room,
or recognize a friend's face in a crowd, things a young child does without
thinking.

Why? Moravec's explanation is evolution. Seeing, moving, and grasping have
been polished by hundreds of millions of years of evolution, so they feel
effortless to us; the hard work happens out of sight. Abstract reasoning
like chess and algebra is new, so we do it slowly and consciously, and it
feels hard. Computers have no such history, so ``hard'' and ``easy'' mean
something completely different to them.
\subsection{Neural Scaling Law}
Imagine you are working for a factory that is producing pencils. With a single, static, automatic assembly line, you make pencils at a rate of $100$ pencils per hour. By adding a second assembly line, you are now able to make pencils at $200$ pencils per hour, double the rate it was previously. This shows that generally, production scales \emph{linearly}.

However, not all systems scale this cleanly. Suppose instead that each additional assembly line becomes harder to integrate: workers need to coordinate more, machines interfere slightly, and space becomes constrained. Now, when you add a second line, production might increase to $180$ pencils per hour instead of $200$. Adding a third might bring you to $250$, not $300$. The total output still increases, but each new addition contributes less than the previous one. This is called \newterm{diminishing returns}.

\index{neural scaling laws}AI models like the ones behind chatbots
behave more like this second factory. Researchers track four quantities:
the number of parameters in the model ($N$), the size of the training data
($D$), the amount of computing used to train it ($C$), and the model's
error, called the \emph{loss} ($L$). As $N$, $D$, or $C$ grows, the loss
shrinks, but with diminishing returns: each tenfold increase buys a
smaller improvement than the last. The surprising part is how
\emph{predictable} it is. The loss follows a smooth power law, the kind of
curve you straightened with a log-log plot in the exponentials chapter.
So researchers can test small models and predict how well a giant one will
do before spending millions of dollars to build it. These predictable
patterns are called \newterm{neural scaling laws}.

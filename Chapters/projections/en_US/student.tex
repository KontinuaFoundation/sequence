\chapter{Projections}

At noon, with the sun straight overhead, your shadow is a small blob at your
feet. Hold a meter stick at an angle, and its shadow on the ground shows how
much of the stick points sideways. That shadow is a \emph{projection}: the
part of one thing that lies along another.

Projections are used in many fields, such as science, math, engineering, and finance. Here are a few examples: 
\begin{itemize}
\item Investors evaluate risk and return of a portfolio by projecting an asset’s return onto a reference portfolio.
\item Astronomers analyze the motion of stellar objects by projecting the object’s true motion onto the plane of the sky.
\item Robotics engineers use projections to prevent robots from running into obstacles by projecting the robot’s position onto the optimal path.
\end{itemize}

\index{vector!projection}Mathematically, a projection describes the relationship of one vector to another in terms of direction and orthogonality. Given two vectors, $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$, the projection of $\mathbf{u}$ onto $\mathbf{v}$ separates $\mathbf{u}$ into two components. The first component signifies how much $\mathbf{u}$ lies in the direction of $\mathbf{v}$. The second signifies the component of $\mathbf{u}$ that is orthogonal (perpendicular) to $\mathbf{v}$. \index{projections} 

The figure~\ref{fig:projection} depicts a projection. The perpendicular line dropped from the end of $\mathbf{u}$ is the \index{projection!orthogonal component}\newterm{orthogonal} component. The portion of $\mathbf{u}$ that lies in the direction of $\mathbf{v}$ is the blue segment.

To calculate the projection of
$\mathbf{u}$ onto $\mathbf{v}$, use this formula:
\index{projections!formula for}
\begin{mdframed}[style=important]

$$
\text{Projection of }\mathbf u\text{ onto }\mathbf v:\quad 
\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf v}(\mathbf u)=\frac{\mathbf u\cdot \mathbf v}{\lVert \mathbf v\rVert^2}\,\mathbf v = \frac{\mathbf u\cdot \mathbf v}{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v}}\,\mathbf v
$$
where $\mathbf u\cdot \mathbf v$ is the dot product of $\vec{\mathbf{u}}$ and $\vec{\mathbf{v}}$. Note that either form of the equation works, and the $\mathbf v$ being multiplied by the dot product quotient cannot be cancelled because it is a vector, not a scalar. 
\end{mdframed}
Note that the denominator is the magnitude squared of vector $\mathbf{v}$.
$$\left(\sqrt{a_1^2 + a_2^2 + ... + a_n^2} \right)^{2}$$

You learned previously that this is the same as the dot product of a vector with itself.
$${v\cdot v}$$
In the examples that follow, we will simplify to the dot product notation.

\index{projection!example}\textbf{Worked example:} Let us find the projection of $u$ onto $v$ for a specific pair of vectors:
$$u = (1,4,6)$$ 
$$v = (-2,6,2)$$ 

$$\mathbf{proj}_\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{u}) = \frac{u\cdot v}{\parallel {v}\parallel ^2}v$$
$$\mathbf{proj}_\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{u}) = \frac{(1,4,6)\cdot(-2,6,2)}{ (-2,6,2)\cdot (-2,6,2)}(-2,6,2)$$
$$\mathbf{proj}_\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{u}) = \frac{34}{44}(-2,6,2)$$
$$\mathbf{proj}_\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{u}) = (-1.545, 4.64, 1.545)$$

As you work your way through this course, you will have a chance to apply the calculations you learn in this chapter to a variety of problems. Specifically, the Gram-Schmidt chapter shows how to transform a set of linearly independent vectors into a set of orthogonal ones. Projections are essential to that transformation. 

\begin{Exercise}[title={Projections}, label=projections]
	Find the projection of $\mathbf{a}$ on $\mathbf{b}$ where:
	$$a = (1,3)$$
	$$b = (-4,6)$$
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=projections]
	Compute the dot product of $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$:
	$$1(-4) + 3(6) = -4 + 18 = 14$$
	Compute the dot product of $\mathbf{b}$ with itself:
	$$(-4)^2 + 6^2 = 16 + 36 = 52$$
	Then the projection is
	$$\frac{14}{52}(-4, 6) = \left(-\frac{14}{13}, \frac{21}{13}\right) \approx (-1.077, 1.615)$$
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Projection onto an axis}, label=projections2]
	Find the projection of $\mathbf{u} = (3, 4)$ onto $\mathbf{v} = (1, 0)$. Then find the orthogonal component, $\mathbf{u} - \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{u})$.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=projections2]
	The dot product is $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 3$, and $\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 1$. So
	$$\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{u}) = \frac{3}{1}(1, 0) = (3, 0)$$
	The orthogonal component is $(3, 4) - (3, 0) = (0, 4)$. It is perpendicular to $\mathbf{v}$, since $(0, 4) \cdot (1, 0) = 0$.
\end{Answer}
 
\begin{Exercise}[title={Sliding Down a Ramp}, label=projections3]
A skateboarder is on a ramp that drops 3 m for every 4 m forward, so the
downhill direction is $\mathbf{r} = (4, -3)$. Gravity accelerates them at
$\mathbf{g} = (0, -9.8)$ m/s$^2$. Find the part of $\mathbf{g}$ that points
along the ramp, and its size.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=projections3]
$\mathbf{g} \cdot \mathbf{r} = 0 + 29.4 = 29.4$ and $\mathbf{r} \cdot
\mathbf{r} = 16 + 9 = 25$, so
$$\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{r}}(\mathbf{g}) = \frac{29.4}{25}(4, -3) = (4.704, -3.528) \text{ m/s}^2$$
Its length is $\sqrt{4.704^2 + 3.528^2} = 5.88$ m/s$^2$, which is $0.6
\times 9.8$. The ramp's slope angle $\theta$ has $\sin\theta = \frac{3}{5}
= 0.6$, so this matches the physics formula $g\sin\theta$.
\end{Answer}

\section{Projections in Python}\index{projection!Python}

Create a file called \filename{projections.py} and enter this code:
\begin{minted}{python}
import numpy as np

# define two vectors  
a = np.array([1, 4, 6])   
b = np.array([-2, 6, 2])  
  
# use np.dot() to calculate the dot product
projection_a_on_b = (np.dot(a, b)/np.dot(b, b))*b
  
print("The projection of vector a on vector b is:", projection_a_on_b)
\end{minted}

You should see:
\begin{minted}{text}
The projection of vector a on vector b is:  [-1.54545455  4.63636364  1.54545455]
\end{minted}
This matches the worked example.

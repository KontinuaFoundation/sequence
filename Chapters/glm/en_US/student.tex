\chapter{Generalized Linear Models}

How many goals will a team score? Will a customer click ``buy''? How long
until a light bulb burns out? Linear regression could make a prediction for
each, but it might predict $-0.4$ goals or a 130\% chance of clicking. This
chapter shows how to keep the simplicity of a linear model while respecting
what the answer is allowed to be.

\section{Beyond the Normal Distribution}\index{generalized linear model}
Ordinary linear regression assumes the response is normal, with a mean that is a linear function of the predictors.
Many responses are not normal: counts, binary outcomes, and positive amounts all violate that assumption. A
\newterm{generalized linear model} (GLM) keeps the linear predictor but allows other distributions for the response.

\section{Three Components}
A GLM has three parts.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Random component}: the distribution of the response, such as normal, binomial, or Poisson.
\item \textbf{Systematic component}: the linear predictor $\eta = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_p x_p = X\boldsymbol{\beta}$.
\item \textbf{Link function} $g$: a function that connects the mean of the response to the linear predictor,
$$g(E[Y]) = \eta$$
\end{enumerate}

\section{Choosing a Model}
The distribution is chosen by the type of response. Counts often use a Poisson distribution, and yes-or-no
outcomes use a binomial distribution. The link is then chosen so the mean stays in the allowed range. The
\newterm{canonical} link for each distribution is a natural default. The chapter on link functions lists the common choices.

\section{A Worked Example}
A Poisson model for the number of customers $Y$ per hour at a smoothie shop uses the log link. Suppose $\beta_0 = 0.5$ and $\beta_1 = 0.2$,
where $x$ is the number of social media posts the shop made that day. With $x = 3$,
$$\eta = 0.5 + 0.2(3) = 1.1$$
The log link gives $\ln(E[Y]) = 1.1$, so the expected number of customers is $E[Y] = e^{1.1} \approx 3.004$.
The log link keeps the mean positive, which is required for counts. It also
makes the coefficients multiplicative: each extra post multiplies the expected
number of customers by $e^{0.2} \approx 1.22$, a 22\% increase.

\section{Fitting a GLM}
Parameters are estimated by maximum likelihood, not least squares. Software iterates until the likelihood stops increasing.
Logistic regression is the GLM with a binomial response and logit link, and ordinary linear regression is the GLM with a
normal response and identity link.

\begin{Exercise}[label=glm1, title=Compute the mean]
In the Poisson model above, what is the expected count when $x = 5$?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=glm1]
$\eta = 0.5 + 0.2(5) = 1.5$, so $E[Y] = e^{1.5} \approx 4.48$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=glm2, title=Name the model]
A survey records whether each person voted (yes or no). Which GLM is appropriate, and what is its link?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=glm2]
Logistic regression: a binomial response with the logit link.
\end{Answer}

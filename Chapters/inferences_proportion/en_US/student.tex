\chapter{Statistical Inferences for Proportions}

\section{Interval Estimation and the Confidence Interval}\index{interval estimation}

Suppose you had to guess your own weight, and then guess a classmate's
weight. You would probably feel more confident about your own guess. You
might say 140 pounds for both, but you would trust your own number a lot
more than the one for your classmate. A point estimate only gives you a
single number. It does not tell you how much to trust that number.

A \index{point estimate}point estimate is a single value that is calculated from a sample. It
serves as our best guess for a population parameter. Although it can be
useful, it is incomplete. Two point estimates can look identical on paper
while carrying very different amounts of certainty behind them, like the
way your weight guess and your classmate's guess might both be 140 pounds
while being backed by very different amounts of information.

This is where the \newterm{confidence interval}\index{confidence interval}
comes in. A confidence interval gives a range of plausible values for the
population parameter that is built around the point estimate. The
\newterm{margin of error}\index{margin of error} is the "plus or minus"
amount that sets the width of that range. It is driven by how confident we
want to be and how much the estimate varies from sample to sample.

% Think about this: if someone tells you "I am 95\% confident the true
% proportion is between 0.60 and 0.70," what do you think happens to that
% range if they instead wanted to be 99\% confident? Do you think the
% interval gets wider or narrower?

A wider interval is more likely to capture the true value, but it also
tells you less. This tradeoff between confidence and precision runs through
everything in this chapter.

Before we build an interval, we check three conditions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Random:} the sample comes from a random sample or a randomized experiment.
\item \textbf{10\% condition:} the sample is no more than 10\% of the population, so the individuals are roughly independent.
\item \textbf{Large counts:} $n\hat{p} \geq 10$ and $n(1-\hat{p}) \geq 10$, so the sampling distribution is close to normal.
\end{itemize}

\section{Constructing a Confidence Interval for a Proportion}\index{confidence interval!for a proportion}

When we discussed confidence intervals, we saw that a wider interval gives us
more certainty at the cost of being precise. That tradeoff is captured by
a single number called the \newterm{critical value}\index{critical value},
written $z^*$.

Suppose a city council wants to estimate the proportion of residents who
support building a new network of bike lanes. Before collecting any data,
they need to decide how confident they want to be in their eventual
estimate, say 95\%. That confidence level determines $z^*$.

A \index{confidence level}95\% confidence level leaves 5\% split evenly between the two tails of the
standard normal distribution, so 2.5\% in each tail. We need the value
$z^*$ such that the area to its right is 0.025, which means the area to its
left is 0.975. Using a standard normal table or a calculator's inverse
normal function, $z^* \approx 1.96$.

If the city council instead wanted 99\% confidence, would $z^*$ be larger
or smaller than 1.96? (Larger: to catch the true value 99\% of the time,
the interval must reach farther out. For 99\%, $z^* \approx 2.576$.)

Once we have $z^*$, we still need to decide how precise we want our estimate
to be. An interval can technically be true and still be worthless. If the
council's confidence interval for bike lane support turned out to be
``somewhere between 5\% and 95\%,'' that would be almost useless for making a
decision, even if it is a perfectly valid 95\% confidence interval. The
width of a useful interval, called the margin of error, depends on how large
a sample the council collects. Larger samples produce narrower, more
informative intervals for the same confidence level.

A confidence interval for a population proportion $p$ has the form
$$\hat{p} \pm z^* \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1 - \hat{p})}{n}}$$
where $\hat{p}$ is the sample proportion, $n$ is the sample size, and the square root term is the standard error. The margin of error is $z^*$ times the standard error.

\begin{mdframed}[frametitle = {Example}, style = important]
The council surveys $n = 200$ residents and finds that 120 support the bike lanes, so $\hat{p} = \frac{120}{200} = 0.60$. For 95\% confidence, $z^* = 1.96$. The standard error is $\sqrt{\frac{0.6(0.4)}{200}} \approx 0.0346$, and the margin of error is $1.96(0.0346) \approx 0.068$. The 95\% confidence interval is $0.60 \pm 0.068$, or $(0.532, 0.668)$. We are 95\% confident that between 53.2\% and 66.8\% of residents support the bike lanes.
\end{mdframed}

To plan a sample size, we solve the margin-of-error formula for $n$. Using the conservative guess $p^* = 0.5$ (which makes the standard error as large as possible), the sample size needed for a margin of error $E$ is
$$n = \left(\frac{z^*}{E}\right)^2 p^*(1 - p^*)$$

For example, to get a margin of error of 0.03 at 95\% confidence, the council needs $n = \left(\frac{1.96}{0.03}\right)^2(0.5)(0.5) \approx 1067.1$, so it should survey at least 1068 residents.

This is why \index{sample size!planning}sample size planning matters just as much as the confidence
level. A researcher who wants a useful interval has to think ahead about how
much data collecting that precision will require.

\textbf{Computing in Excel}

Put the counts in cells: $n$ in \texttt{B1} (200) and the number supporting in \texttt{B2} (120). Then use these formulas:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\hat{p}$: \texttt{=B2/B1}, which gives 0.60.
\item $z^*$: \texttt{=NORM.S.INV(0.975)}, which gives 1.96.
\item Margin of error: \texttt{=B4*SQRT(B3*(1-B3)/B1)}, where \texttt{B3} holds $\hat{p}$ and \texttt{B4} holds $z^*$. This gives about 0.068.
\item Interval: \texttt{=B3-B5} and \texttt{=B3+B5}, which give 0.532 and 0.668.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Computing in Python}

The \texttt{scipy} library finds $z^*$, and the \texttt{statsmodels} library builds the interval from the counts:
\begin{minted}{python}
from scipy.stats import norm
from statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportion_confint

z_star = norm.ppf(0.975)                  # 1.96
low, high = proportion_confint(120, 200, alpha=0.05, method="normal")
print(f"z* = {z_star:.2f}")
print(f"95% CI: ({low:.3f}, {high:.3f})")  # about (0.532, 0.668)
\end{minted}

To plan a sample size, a short function solves the formula above:
\begin{minted}{python}
import math

def sample_size(margin, z_star=1.96, p_star=0.5):
    return math.ceil((z_star / margin) ** 2 * p_star * (1 - p_star))

print(sample_size(0.03))  # 1068
\end{minted}


\section{Inference: Tests of Significance}

Building a confidence interval answers "what is the population proportion
likely to be?" But sometimes the question you actually care about is
different: has something changed? \newterm{Statistical inference}\index{Statistical inference}
is the process of using information from a sample to answer a specific
question or make a decision about a population parameter, and a
\newterm{test of significance}\index{test of significance} is the tool built
for exactly this kind of question.

\subsection{The Logic of a Significance Test}

A streaming service offers a free trial. Historically, 20\% of trial users
convert to paying subscribers. The company redesigns its sign-up page and
wants to know: did the conversion rate go up?

A significance test starts by assuming nothing changed, then asks whether
the data would be surprising under that assumption.
\begin{itemize}
\item The \newterm{null hypothesis}\index{null hypothesis}, $H_0$, is the
``nothing changed'' claim: $H_0: p = 0.20$.
\item The \newterm{alternative hypothesis}\index{alternative hypothesis},
$H_a$, is what we are looking for evidence of: $H_a: p > 0.20$. (If we
cared about a change in either direction, we would write $H_a: p \neq
0.20$, a \emph{two-sided} test.)
\end{itemize}

After the redesign, a random sample of $n = 400$ trial users shows 98
conversions, so $\hat{p} = 0.245$. That is higher than 0.20, but samples
vary. Could a jump this big happen by chance if the true rate were still
20\%?

\subsection{The Test Statistic and the p-value}\index{test statistic}\index{p-value}

If $H_0$ is true, $\hat{p}$ is approximately normal with mean $p_0 = 0.20$
and standard deviation $\sqrt{\frac{p_0(1-p_0)}{n}}$. (Notice that we use
$p_0$, the value from $H_0$, not $\hat{p}$. The conditions are the same
as before, but the large-counts check uses $p_0$: $np_0 = 80 \geq 10$ and
$n(1-p_0) = 320 \geq 10$.) The \newterm{test statistic} measures how many
standard deviations $\hat{p}$ is from $p_0$:
$$z = \frac{\hat{p} - p_0}{\sqrt{\frac{p_0(1-p_0)}{n}}} = \frac{0.245 - 0.20}{\sqrt{\frac{(0.2)(0.8)}{400}}} = \frac{0.045}{0.02} = 2.25$$

The \newterm{p-value} is the probability, assuming $H_0$ is true, of getting
a result at least as extreme as the one observed. Here that is $P(Z \geq
2.25) \approx 0.012$. If the redesign did nothing, only about 1.2\% of
samples would show a conversion rate this high.

\subsection{Making a Decision}\index{significance level}

Before collecting data, we choose a \newterm{significance level} $\alpha$,
the cutoff for ``too surprising to be chance.'' The most common choice is
$\alpha = 0.05$.
\begin{itemize}
\item If the p-value $< \alpha$, \textbf{reject $H_0$}: the data give
convincing evidence for $H_a$.
\item If the p-value $\geq \alpha$, \textbf{fail to reject $H_0$}: the data
do not give convincing evidence for $H_a$. (This does \emph{not} prove
$H_0$ is true.)
\end{itemize}
Since $0.012 < 0.05$, we reject $H_0$. There is convincing evidence that
the redesign raised the conversion rate above 20\%.

In Python:
\begin{minted}{python}
from scipy.stats import norm
import math

p0, n, successes = 0.20, 400, 98
p_hat = successes / n
z = (p_hat - p0) / math.sqrt(p0 * (1 - p0) / n)
p_value = 1 - norm.cdf(z)          # one-sided, H_a: p > p0
print(round(z, 3), round(p_value, 4))   # 2.25 0.0122
\end{minted}
In Excel, the p-value is \texttt{=1-NORM.S.DIST(2.25, TRUE)}. For a
two-sided test, double the one-sided tail area.

\subsection{Type I and Type II Errors}\index{Type I error}\index{Type II error}

Because tests rely on samples, they can be wrong in two ways:
\begin{itemize}
\item A \newterm{Type I error} is rejecting $H_0$ when it is actually true:
concluding the redesign helped when it did not. The probability of a Type I
error is $\alpha$.
\item A \newterm{Type II error} is failing to reject $H_0$ when $H_a$ is
actually true: missing a real improvement.
\end{itemize}
The \newterm{power}\index{power} of a test is the probability of correctly
rejecting $H_0$ when $H_a$ is true. Power goes up with a larger sample size,
a bigger true effect, or a larger $\alpha$. For example, if the redesign
really raised the conversion rate to 25\%, this test (with $n = 400$ and
$\alpha = 0.05$) would detect it about 79\% of the time; the other 21\% of
the time it would make a Type II error.

\begin{Exercise}[title={The Cafeteria Survey}, label=prop_test1]
The cafeteria claims that 60\% of students like the new menu. The student
council surveys a random sample of 150 students, and 81 say they like it.
At $\alpha = 0.05$, is there convincing evidence that the true proportion
differs from 60\%?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=prop_test1]
$H_0: p = 0.60$ and $H_a: p \neq 0.60$. The conditions hold: $np_0 = 90$
and $n(1-p_0) = 60$ are both at least 10. $\hat{p} = \frac{81}{150} = 0.54$, and
$$z = \frac{0.54 - 0.60}{\sqrt{\frac{(0.6)(0.4)}{150}}} = \frac{-0.06}{0.04} = -1.5$$
The two-sided p-value is $2P(Z \leq -1.5) \approx 0.134$. Since $0.134 >
0.05$, fail to reject $H_0$. The survey does not give convincing evidence
that the true proportion differs from 60\%.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Name the Error}, label=prop_test2]
In the streaming-service example, suppose the redesign actually had no
effect, but the test rejected $H_0$ anyway. What kind of error is this, and
what is its consequence for the company?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=prop_test2]
A Type I error: rejecting a true $H_0$. The company would believe the
redesign works and might spend money rolling it out, even though it does
not help.
\end{Answer}

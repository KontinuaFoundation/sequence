\chapter{Introduction to the Kontinua Sequence}\index{Kontinua Sequence}

You are starting a long trip. By the end, you will be a problem solver:
someone who uses math, computers, and science to answer questions that
nobody has answered yet.\index{problem solving}

\section{Why Keep Going?}
Problem solvers build the world. Engineers design bridges and phones.
Scientists find cures for diseases. Doctors figure out what is making a
patient sick. Programmers write the apps on your phone. Architects design
buildings, actuaries measure risk for insurance companies, and
mathematicians discover ideas no one has seen before.\index{Engineering ! careers}
Many of these jobs pay well, and the world needs more people who can do
them.\index{career}

\section{When It Feels Hard}
Sooner or later, you will meet someone who solves problems faster than
you. You might think, ``I worked hard, and that person is still better.
Maybe I should quit.'' Do not quit.\index{quitting}

Two ideas help. First, problem solving is a muscle. The more you use it,
the stronger it gets. ``I am not good at this'' is really ``I am not good
at this \emph{yet}.''

Second, you do not need to be the best in the world. You need to be
good, and good is within reach of anyone who keeps practicing. Employers,
colleges, and your future self will all be glad you did.

\section{How This Book Works}
Each chapter teaches one idea. Read the examples slowly, with a pencil in
your hand. Then try the exercises \emph{before} you peek at the answers,
which are at the back of the book. The chapters build on each other, so
take them in order.

\section{Where to Start}
The physicist Richard Feynman\index{Feynman, Richard} once asked: if
almost all scientific knowledge were destroyed, and you could pass just
one sentence to the next generation, which sentence would carry the most
information in the fewest words?

His answer: ``All things are made of atoms\index{atoms}---little particles
that move around in perpetual motion, attracting each other when they are a
little distance apart, but repelling upon being squeezed into one another.''

That is a good place to start. Atoms are so small that even a pinch of
anything holds an enormous number of them. Chemists count atoms in
\emph{moles}: one mole is about $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ particles. The molar
mass tells you how many grams one mole weighs.

\textbf{Worked example: counting atoms in a gram of carbon.} Carbon has a
molar mass of about 12 g/mol. How many atoms are in 1 g of carbon?

\textbf{Solution.} First find the number of moles: $\frac{1 \text{ g}}{12 \text{ g/mol}} \approx 0.083$ mol.
Then multiply by the number of atoms in a mole:
$$0.083 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} \approx 5.0 \times 10^{22} \text{ atoms}$$
That is fifty thousand billion billion atoms, in about the mass of a
paperclip.

\begin{Exercise}[label=intro1, title=Counting molecules in a glass of water]
A glass holds 250 g of water. Water has a molar mass of about 18 g/mol.
How many water molecules are in the glass?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=intro1]
$\frac{250}{18} \approx 13.9$ mol, so the number of molecules is about
$13.9 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} \approx 8.4 \times 10^{24}$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=intro2, title=Why such big numbers?]
One gram of carbon has about $5 \times 10^{22}$ atoms. Why do numbers this
large show up whenever we count atoms?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=intro2]
Atoms are tiny, so any amount you can see or weigh contains a huge number
of them. Moles turn a mass you can measure in a lab into a count of
particles, which lets you move from a measurement to a statement about
atoms.
\end{Answer}

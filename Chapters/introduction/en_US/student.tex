\chapter{Introduction to the Kontinua Sequence}\index{Kontinua Sequence}

This book helps you start a long journey. At the end, you will be a
problem solver. You will use math, computers, and science to answer
questions that nobody has answered before.\index{problem solving}

\section{Why Keep Going?}
Problem solving is valuable. Engineers design bridges and phones.
Scientists find cures for diseases. Doctors diagnose patients. Computer
programmers build the apps you use every day. Architects design
buildings, actuaries measure risk for insurance companies, and
mathematicians discover new ideas.\index{Engineering ! careers}
Many of these jobs are well paid, and the demand for people who can
solve problems is growing.\index{career}

\section{When It Feels Hard}
At some point, you will meet people who are better at solving problems
than you are. You may think, ``I have worked hard, but that person is
better than I am. I should quit.'' Do not quit.\index{quitting}

Two ideas can help. First, problem solving is like a muscle. The more
you practice, the stronger it gets. Saying ``I am not good at this yet''
is fine. It means you need more practice.

Second, you do not need to be the best in the world. Millions of people
your age can solve problems better than you can right now. You can still
be very good at it, and employers want people who are good at it.

\section{How This Book Works}
Each chapter teaches one topic. Read the examples slowly, and then try the
exercises before you look at the answers. The answers are in the back of
the book. Most chapters build on earlier ones, so the order matters.

\section{Where to Start}
The physicist Richard Feynman\index{Feynman, Richard} once asked: if all
scientific knowledge were destroyed, and you could pass on only one
sentence to the next generation, what sentence would hold the most
information in the fewest words?

His answer was: ``All things are made of atoms\index{atoms}---little particles
that move around in perpetual motion, attracting each other when they are a
little distance apart, but repelling upon being squeezed into one another.''

That is a good place to start.

\textbf{Worked example: counting atoms in a gram of carbon.} Carbon has a molar mass of about 12 g/mol. One mole contains about
$6.02 \times 10^{23}$ atoms. How many atoms are in 1 g of carbon?

\textbf{Solution.} First find the number of moles: $\frac{1 \text{ g}}{12 \text{ g/mol}} \approx 0.083$ mol.
Then multiply by Avogadro's number:
$$0.083 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} \approx 5.0 \times 10^{22} \text{ atoms}$$

\begin{Exercise}[label=intro1, title=Counting molecules in a glass of water]
A glass holds 250 g of water. Water has a molar mass of about 18 g/mol.
How many water molecules are in the glass?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=intro1]
$\frac{250}{18} \approx 13.9$ mol, so the number of molecules is about
$13.9 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} \approx 8.4 \times 10^{24}$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=intro2, title=Why small numbers matter]
In the example, 1 g of carbon has about $5 \times 10^{22}$ atoms. Why
does it help to think about atoms this way?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=intro2]
Atoms are too small to count one by one, so a large number always
appears. Scientists use moles to turn an amount you can weigh into a
count of particles. That link lets you move from a measurement you make
in a lab to a statement about atoms.
\end{Answer}

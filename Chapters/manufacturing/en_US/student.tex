\chapter{Manufacturing}

Look around the room. Your chair, your phone case, the hinges on the door,
the pen in your hand: every one of them was made by a machine following a
process that engineers designed. Why is a phone case plastic and molded,
while a bike frame is metal and welded? Each manufacturing process has its
strengths, and choosing the right one means balancing cost, quantity,
precision, and time. Here is a tour of the most important ones.

\section{Woods and Metals Processes}



Bridges, cars, buildings, and furniture all depend on shaping wood and
metal accurately. These first two machines work on both.

\subsection{Milling}

\index{milling}A mill carves shapes out of a solid block. A spinning
cutting tool follows a path and removes material, so milling is a
\newterm{subtractive}\index{subtractive manufacturing} process: you start
with more than you need and cut away the rest, like a sculptor. The tool
spins very fast, which lets it cut hard materials like wood and metal.

A 3-axis mill moves the tool along the $x$, $y$, and $z$ axes, which is
enough for many parts. A 5-axis (or even 6-axis) mill can also tilt and rotate, so it can
reach around curves and undercuts, like the blades of a jet engine
turbine.
\begin{center}
    
    \includegraphics[width=.75\textwidth]{sixAxisCnc.png}
\end{center}


In manufacturing settings where speed and repeatability is paramount, mills are often computer controlled. This functionality is referred to as \newterm{Computer Numerical Control}\index{computer numerical control}, or CNC. CNC mills are able to repeatedly follow a tool path, resulting in consistent and accurate parts.

\subsection{Lathes}

\index{lathing}A lathe makes round things: bolts, axles, table legs,
baseball bats. It is also subtractive, but it flips the mill's idea around.
The \emph{material} spins, and a stationary cutting tool shaves it down,
layer by layer. (Shaping on a lathe is called \newterm{turning}.) 

% graphic showing how a lathe removes material - show cylinder of raw material with rotation arrow indicating it rotates, tool bit and tool path, and lathed result
\begin{center}
    
    \includegraphics[width=.75\textwidth]{lathe.png}
\end{center}


In the manufacturing industry, lathes are also often computer controlled. Alongside CNC mills, these CNC lathes are responsible for a majority of the objects that we interact with everyday.

\section{Metal-specific Processes}

While mills and lathes are already able to cover the vast majority of manufacturing needs for woods and metals, there are certain processes that are specifically enabled by the unique properties of metals. Most of them take advantage of \index{malleability}malleability: metals
can be pressed, rolled, and bent into new shapes without cracking,
especially when warm.

\subsection{Sheet Metal Processes}

While milling allows us to process blocks of metal to great effect, sometimes the application we need does not require material of such thickness.

This is where sheet metal comes in, as well as the methods we use to process it. One of the most common techniques in manufacturing is rolling, where a raw sheet is gradually rolled into the desired shape. This method is used to create many objects you may recognize, such as metal roofing, the skin of an airplane, and soda cans.
\begin{center}
    
    \includegraphics[width=.75\textwidth]{rolling.png}
\end{center}


Another method is stamping, where a raw sheet is stamped into the desired shape. This allows us to create surfaces with complex geometries in an instant, and in large quantities. This method is often used for applications like the exterior panels of a car, where parts with compound curves are needed.
\begin{center}
    
    \includegraphics[width=.75\textwidth]{stamping.png}
\end{center}


%graphic of a flat sheet stamped into a car door shape e.g.

Finally, sheet metal parts are often stiffened by bending their edges
over into a lip, called a \newterm{flange} or a \newterm{hem}. Look at the
edge of a metal shelf or a car door: the folded edge makes the part much
stiffer, and it hides the sharp cut edge.

\subsection{Casting}

The last kind of metal-specific process we will cover is casting. \index{casting}Casting involves pouring molten metal into a mold, then letting the metal cool and set inside the mold. Casting can make complex shapes in large numbers: engine blocks, faucets,
and die-cast toy cars are all cast.

\includegraphics[width=.75\textwidth]{casting.png}


Casting also results in minimal material wastage, as it is not a subtractive manufacturing method where excess material is machined away, but rather only the specific amount of material required is poured in each time.

\section{Wood-specific Processes}

Wood is made of long fibers, so it is strong along the grain and flexible
when thin. Woodworkers use that to make curves.

\subsection{Bending}

Turning raw wood into flat, workable pieces involves a variety of tools that you probably know of already, such as saws and drills. However, there are specific processes that help us create curved shapes with wood, in addition to the mill and the lathe mentioned earlier.

This is where bent lamination comes in. \index{bent lamination}Bent lamination involves layering multiple thin veneers or strips of wood with adhesive, and clamping it to create the desired form while letting the glue dry. This method is often used for furniture production, enabling continuous curves in wood with tight radii.
\begin{center}
    
    \includegraphics[width=.75\textwidth]{woodBend.png}
\end{center}

%graphic of bent lamination - various veneers being layered, then clamped within a form, and then the final result

Steam bending works differently. Hot steam softens the natural glue
(lignin) that holds wood fibers together, so a solid piece of wood can be
bent around a form. As it cools and dries, it holds the new shape. Unlike
bent lamination, steam bending needs no adhesive. Wooden boat ribs and
classic bentwood chairs are made this way.

\section{Plastic-specific Processes}

Although plastics only came into prominence in the mid 20th century, they have changed manufacturing and, by extension, the world as we know it. Easily manufacturable, durable, and cost-effective, they have come to permeate almost everything we use on a daily basis.

It must also be noted that these exact qualities have also resulted in the proliferation of plastics in our environment, and as such, usage of plastics should be well considered and limited. Alternative biodegradable materials are currently being trialed by scientists and would look to replicate many of the same qualities we expect from plastics, including its manufacturability.

\subsection{Injection Molding}

\index{injection molding}Injection molding is responsible for the vast majority of plastic products that you interact with on a daily basis. It is extremely quick, highly accurate, and has minimal material wastage, making it a popular and cost effective method of manufacturing plastic goods.

Similar to casting, injection molding involves injecting molten plastic into a mold, then allowing the part to cool and set inside the cavity. 

You can often tell when a part was produced through injection molding, with telltale signs such as the parting line. This is where the parts of the mold meet, forming a visible line on the surface of a part.

\section{3D Printing}

Injection molding has traditionally been the go-to technique for manufacturing plastic goods. However, new technologies result in the advent of new manufacturing methods. 3D printing has become popular in the last few decades as a way to make
a part quickly without first building an expensive mold.

\index{3D printing}3D printing is an \newterm{additive}\index{additive
manufacturing} process: it builds a part one thin layer at a time. The
most common printers melt plastic filament and lay it down like a very
precise hot glue gun. Others harden liquid resin with light or fuse metal
powder with a laser. 3D printers can make shapes no mold could, like a
part with hollow channels inside. They are slower and usually less
precise than injection molding, but they are improving rapidly.

\textbf{Worked example.} A part 30 mm tall is printed with 0.2 mm layers.
How many layers does it need?

\textbf{Solution.} $\frac{30}{0.2} = 150$ layers.

\section{Laser Cutting and Water Jet}

A laser cutter focuses a powerful beam of light to a tiny spot that burns
or melts its way through sheet material as the head moves. Small laser
cutters in school makerspaces cut and engrave wood, acrylic, cardboard, and
fabric; big industrial lasers slice sheet steel. Laser cutting is
subtractive, and it works best on thin sheets.

A water jet cutter blasts water through a tiny nozzle at several times the
speed of sound. Mixed with grit, the stream cuts metal, stone, glass, and
rubber. Unlike a laser, it does not heat the material, so it will not
warp or scorch the edge. Very thick material is still hard to cut.

\begin{Exercise}[title={More Layers}, label=mfg1]
A part $45$ mm tall is printed with $0.1$ mm layers. How many layers are
needed? If each layer takes 30 seconds, how long does the print take?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=mfg1]
$\frac{45}{0.1} = 450$ layers. $450 \times 30 = 13{,}500$ seconds, or
3.75 hours. Thinner layers look smoother but take longer.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Mold or printer?}, label=mfg_breakeven]
You need plastic phone stands. An injection mold costs \$20,000 to make,
and then each stand costs \$0.50. A 3D-printing service charges \$8.00 per
stand and needs no mold. For how many stands is injection molding the
cheaper choice? Which would you use for 100 stands? For 100,000?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=mfg_breakeven]
Molding costs $20{,}000 + 0.5n$ and printing costs $8n$. They are equal
when $20{,}000 = 7.5n$, or $n \approx 2{,}667$. Above about 2,667 stands,
molding is cheaper. For 100 stands, print them (\$800 vs. \$20,050). For
100,000 stands, mold them (\$70,000 vs. \$800,000). This is why companies
3D-print prototypes and injection-mold products.
\end{Answer}

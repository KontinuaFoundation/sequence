\chapter{Momentum}

Why does a bowling ball scatter pins while a beach ball bounces off them?
Why do airbags save lives? Why does a rifle kick back into your shoulder?
All three answers come from one idea: momentum.

Start with a puzzle. A 2 kg block of putty flies through space at 5 meters
per second and smacks into a 3 kg block of putty that is sitting still. The
blocks squish and stick together. How fast does the combined blob move?
\begin{center}
  
  \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{putty1.png}
  \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{putty2.png}
\end{center}
\index{momentum}


\begin{mdframed}[style=important, frametitle={Formula for Momentum}]
Every object has \newterm{momentum}. The momentum is a vector
quantity --- it points in the direction that the object is moving and has
a magnitude equal to its mass times its speed.
\begin{equation}
  p=mv
\end{equation}
\end{mdframed}

Add up the momentum vectors of a group of objects, and you get their total
momentum. When the objects only push on each other, that total never
changes.
\begin{equation}
p = p_1 + p_2 + \dots + p_n = m_1v_1+m_2v_2 + \dots + m_nv_n
\end{equation}

In our puzzle, the moving block has momentum $(2)(5) = 10$ kg m/s and
the resting block has 0. After they merge, the 5 kg blob must still carry
10 kg m/s, so it moves at 2 m/s in the original direction.
\index{momentum!conservation of}
\begin{mdframed}[style=important, frametitle={Conservation of Momentum}]
\textbf{The total momentum of a system remains constant as long as no external forces act on it.}
In a collision or interaction, the momentum before the event must equal the momentum after the event:
\begin{equation}
p_{\text{initial}} = p_{\text{final}}
\end{equation}
This applies whether the collision is elastic, inelastic, or perfectly inelastic. Even if kinetic energy is lost (as heat, sound, or deformation), momentum is still conserved because it depends only on mass and velocity, not on energy.
\end{mdframed}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Cars on Ice}, label=cars_on_ice]
A 1000 kg car is going north at 12 m/s. A 1500 kg car is going east at
16 m/s. They both hit a patch of frictionless ice and collide. Steel bends,
and the two cars become one tangled object. What is its velocity (speed
and direction) as it slides across the ice?
\includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{icecar.png}
\includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{icecar2.png}

\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=cars_on_ice]
  The momentum of the first car is 12,000 kg m/s in the north direction.

  The momentum of the second car is 24,000 kg m/s in the east direction.

  The new object will be moving northeast. What is the angle compared with the east?

  $$\theta = \arctan{\frac{12,000}{24,000}} \approx 0.4636 \text{ radians } \approx 26.565\text{ degrees north of east}$$

  The magnitude of the momentum of the new object is $\sqrt{12,000^2 + 24,000^2} \approx 26,833\text{ kg m/s}$

  Its mass is 2,500 kg.  So the speed will be $26,833/2,500 = 10.73$ m/s.
\end{Answer}


Note that kinetic energy ($1/2 m v^2$) is \emph{not} conserved
here.  Before the collision, the moving putty block has $(1/2)(2)(5^2) = 25$
joules of kinetic energy.  Afterward, the big block has $(1/2)(5)(2^2)
= 10$ joules of kinetic energy.  What happened to the energy that was
lost? It went into squishing the putty (and a little into heat and
sound).

What if the blocks were marble instead of putty?  Then there would be
very little deforming, so kinetic energy \emph{and} momentum would be
conserved. The two blocks would end up having different velocity
vectors.

Let us assume for a moment that they strike each other straight on, so
there is motion in only one direction, both before and after the
collision.  Can we solve for the speeds of the first block ($v_1$) and
the second block ($v_2$)?

We end up with two equations. Conservation of momentum says:

$$2 v_1 + 3 v_2 = 10$$

Conservation of kinetic energy says:

$$(1/2)(2)(v_1^2) + (1/2)(3)(v_2^2) = 25$$

Using the first equation, we can solve for $v_1$ in terms of $v_2$:

$$v_1 = \frac{10 - 3 v_2}{2}$$

Substituting this into the second equation, we get:

$$\left(\frac{10 - 3 v_2}{2}\right)^2 + \frac{3 v_2^2}{2} = 25$$

Simplifying, we get:

$$v_2^2 - 4 v_2 + 0 = 0$$

This quadratic has two solutions: $v_2 = 0$ and $v_2 = 4$.  $v_2 = 0$
represents the situation before the collision.  Substituting in $v_2 = 4$:

$$v_1 = \frac{10 - 3(4)}{2} = -1$$

Thus, if the blocks are hard enough that kinetic energy is conserved,
after the collision, the smaller block will be heading in the opposite
direction at 1 m/s. The larger block will be moving at 4 m/s in the
direction of the original motion.

\begin{Exercise}[title={Billiard Balls}, label=billiards]
  
A 0.4 kg billiard ball traveling at 3 m/s hits an identical billiard ball
at rest. It strikes obliquely (neither perpendicular nor parallel), so that the ball at rest starts to
move at a 45 degree angle from the path of the ball that hit it.

Assuming all kinetic energy is conserved, what is the velocity
vector of each ball after the collision?

\includegraphics[width=.75\textwidth]{poolball.png}

\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=billiards]

  The original forward momentum was 1.2 kg m/s.  The original kinetic energy is $(1/2)(0.4)(3^2)$ = 1.8 joules. 

  Let $s$ be the post-collision speed of the ball that had been at
  rest.  Let $x$ and $y$ be the forward and sideways speeds
  (post-collision) of the other ball. Conservation of kinetic energy says

  $$(1/2)(0.4)(s^2) + (1/2)(0.4)(x^2+y^2) = 1.8$$

  Forward momentum is conserved:

  $$0.4\frac{s}{\sqrt{2}} + 0.4 x = 1.2$$

  Which can be rewritten:

  $$x = 3 - \frac{s}{\sqrt{2}}$$
  
  Sideways momentum stays zero:

  $$(0.4)\frac{s}{\sqrt{2}} - 0.4 y = 0.0$$

  Which can be rewritten:

  $$y = \frac{s}{\sqrt{2}}$$

  Substituting into to the conservation of kinetic energy equation above:

  $$(1/2)(0.4)(s^2) + (1/2)(0.4)\left(\left(3 - \frac{s}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^2+\left(\frac{s}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^2\right) = 1.8$$

  Which can be rewritten:

  $$s^2 - \frac{3}{\sqrt{2}} s + 0 = 0$$

  There are two solutions to this quadratic: $s = 0$ (before collision) and $s = \frac{3}{\sqrt{2}}$. Thus,

  $$y = \frac{3}{2}$$

  and

  $$x = 3 - \frac{3}{2} = \frac{3}{2}$$

  So both balls careen off at $45^\circ$ angles (on opposite sides of the
  original path) at the same speed, $\frac{3}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 2.12$ m/s.
  Notice that they leave at right angles to each other. Pool players know
  this: when a moving ball hits a resting ball off-center, the two always
  split at $90^\circ$. 

  
\end{Answer}
\section{Impulse}
A change in momentum is called an \newterm{impulse}\index{impulse}. Like
momentum, impulse is a vector.

\begin{mdframed}[style=important, frametitle={Formula for Impulse}]
The impulse $\textbf{J}$ is the change in momentum:
\begin{equation}
  \textbf{J} = \Delta p
\end{equation}
It also equals the force times how long the force acts:
\begin{equation}
  \textbf{J} = F\Delta t \label{eq:impulse-momentum1}
\end{equation}
If the force changes over time, the impulse is the area under the
force-versus-time graph. (In calculus, you will write that area as an
integral.) The fact that impulse equals change in momentum is called the
\index{impulse-momentum theorem}\textbf{impulse-momentum theorem}.
\end{mdframed}
So a big force for a short time \textit{or} a small force for a long time
can produce the same change in momentum.

\subsection{Airbags and Egg Tosses}
Have you ever played the egg toss? The trick to catching the egg is to let
your hands move back with it. Here is why.

\textbf{Worked example.} A 0.06 kg egg arrives at 5 m/s. To stop it, you
must change its momentum by $(0.06)(5) = 0.3$ kg m/s. That impulse is fixed.
If your stiff hands stop it in 0.01 s, the force is
$$F = \frac{J}{\Delta t} = \frac{0.3}{0.01} = 30 \text{ N}$$
That is enough to crack the shell. If you let your hands ride back and take
0.2 s to stop it, the force is only $\frac{0.3}{0.2} = 1.5$ N. Same
impulse, one twentieth of the force.

Airbags, bike helmets, crumple zones, and the bend in your knees when you
land a jump all work the same way: they stretch out the stopping time so
the force stays small.

\begin{Exercise}[title={Landing a jump}, label=impulse_jump]
A 50 kg student jumps off a wall and hits the ground at 4 m/s. What impulse
does the ground deliver? What is the average force if the student lands
stiff-legged and stops in 0.02 s? What if the student bends their knees and
stops in 0.25 s?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=impulse_jump]
The impulse is the change in momentum: $(50)(4) = 200$ kg m/s, upward.
Stiff-legged: $F = \frac{200}{0.02} = 10{,}000$ N. Knees bent:
$F = \frac{200}{0.25} = 800$ N. (On top of this, the ground must also hold
up the student's weight, about 490 N.) Bending your knees cuts the force by
a factor of more than 10.
\end{Answer}
\section{Collisions}
\index{collisions}
In every collision between isolated objects, momentum is conserved.
Kinetic energy is another story. We sort collisions into three kinds by
what happens to the kinetic energy.

\subsection{Elastic Collision}
In an \newterm{elastic collision}\index{collisions!elastic}, \emph{both
momentum and kinetic energy are conserved}. The billiard balls in
Exercise~\ref{billiards} collided elastically. When the hit is dead
center, something striking happens: the moving ball stops cold, and the
resting ball takes off with all of its velocity (figure
\ref{fig:billiardElasticCollision}).

No real collision is perfectly elastic. A little energy always goes into
sound, heat, and tiny dents. But billiard balls come close. Listen: a
billiard collision makes a quiet click, not the crunch of a car crash.

In equations, for an elastic collision:
\[\text{total }\textbf{p}_{\text{before collision}} = \text{total }\textbf{p}_{\text{after collision}} \implies m_1v_{i,1} + m_2v_{i,2} + \cdots  = m_1v_{f,1} + m_2v_{f,2} + \cdots\]
\emph{and}
\[\text{total }\textbf{KE}_{\text{before collision}} = \text{total }\textbf{KE}_{\text{after collision}}\]

\subsection{Inelastic Collisions}
\index{collisions!inelastic}In an \newterm{inelastic collision}, some
kinetic energy is lost, usually to heat, sound, or bending. Momentum is
still conserved:

\[\text{total }\textbf{p}_{\text{before collision}} = \text{total }\textbf{p}_{\text{after collision}} \implies m_1v_{i,1} + m_2v_{i,2} + \cdots  = m_1v_{f,1} + m_2v_{f,2} + \cdots\]

Drop a basketball, and it never bounces back to the height you dropped
it from. Some kinetic energy went into the thump you heard and into
squishing the ball. The missing height measures the lost energy (figure
\ref{fig:basketballInelastic}). (Where did the ball's momentum go? Into the
Earth, which recoils by an amount far too small to notice.)

\subsection{Perfectly Inelastic}
A collision is \emph{perfectly inelastic}\index{collisions!perfectly
inelastic} when the objects stick together and move off with the same
velocity, like the putty and the cars on ice. This loses as much kinetic
energy as momentum conservation allows.

\textbf{Worked example.} A $2$ kg cart moving at $3$ m/s hits a $1$ kg cart
at rest, and they stick together. Momentum is conserved:
$(2)(3) + (1)(0) = (2 + 1)v$, so $v = 2$ m/s. The kinetic energy drops from
$\frac{1}{2}(2)(3^2) = 9$ J to $\frac{1}{2}(3)(2^2) = 6$ J.


\begin{Exercise}[title={Two Carts}, label=carts_elastic]
Two carts on a frictionless track collide elastically.

Cart A has mass $2.0$ kg and is moving to the right at $3.0$ m/s.

Cart B has mass $1.0$ kg and is initially at rest.

They collide head-on elastically.

Find the final velocities of both carts. Hint: You may have to do a few equation substitutions
\end{Exercise}

\begin{Answer}[ref=carts_elastic]
Because both carts collide elastically, the Conservation of Kinetic Energy and Conservation of Momentum both apply.

We can rearrange the Conservation of Kinetic Energy in the following ways:
\begin{align*}
\frac{1}{2}m_1 v_{1i}^2 + \frac{1}{2}m_2 v_{2i}^2&=\frac{1}{2}m_1 v_{1f}^2 + \frac{1}{2}m_2 v_{2f}^2 \\ 
\frac{1}{2}m_1 v_{1i}^2 + \frac{1}{2}m_2 v_{2i}^2 - \frac{1}{2}m_1 v_{1f}^2 - \frac{1}{2}m_2 v_{2f}^2 &= 0\\ 
m_1 v_{1i}^2 + m_2 v_{2i}^2 - m_1 v_{1f}^2 - m_2 v_{2f}^2 &= 0\\ 
m_1 v_{1i}^2 - m_1 v_{1f}^2 + m_2 v_{2i}^2 - m_2 v_{2f}^2 &= 0 \\ 
m_1 (v_{1i}^2 - v_{1f}^2) + m_2 (v_{2i}^2 -  v_{2f}^2) &= 0 \\ 
m_1 (v_{1i} - v_{1f})(v_{1i} + v_{1f})+m_2 (v_{2i} - v_{2f})(v_{2i} + v_{2f}) &= 0 \tag{1}\label{eq:1}\\
\end{align*}

And rearranging Conservation of Momentum:
\begin{align*}
  m_1 v_{1i} + m_2 v_{2i} &= m_1 v_{1f} + m_2 v_{2f} \\
  m_1 v_{1i} + m_2 v_{2i} - m_1 v_{1f} - m_2 v_{2f} &= 0\\
  m_1 (v_{1i} - v_{1f}) + m_2 (v_{2i} - v_{2f}) &= 0 \\
  m_1 (v_{1i} - v_{1f}) &= -m_2 (v_{2i} - v_{2f}) \tag{2}\label{eq:2}\\
\end{align*}

Substituting Equation~\eqref{eq:2} into Equation~\eqref{eq:1}:

\begin{align*}
m_1 (v_{1i} - v_{1f})(v_{1i} + v_{1f})+m_2 (v_{2i} - v_{2f})(v_{2i} + v_{2f}) &= 0 \\
- m_2 (v_{2i} - v_{2f})(v_{1i} + v_{1f})+m_2 (v_{2i} - v_{2f})(v_{2i} + v_{2f}) &= 0 \\ 
m_2 (v_{2i} - v_{2f})\left[\, (v_{2i} + v_{2f}) - (v_{1i} + v_{1f}) \right] &= 0 \\ 
(v_{2i} + v_{2f}) - (v_{1i} + v_{1f}) &= 0 \hfill \text{ assuming } v_{2i} \ne v_{2f}\\
v_{2f} - v_{1f} &= v_{1i} - v_{2i} \tag{3}\\ 
\end{align*}
Equation 3 tells us that, in an elastic collision, the relative speed of separation is equal to relative speed of approach. 
This allows us to do the following plug-ins:
\begin{align*}
m_1 v_{1i} + m_2 v_{2i}&=m_1 v_{1f}+m_2 (v_{1f} + v_{1i} - v_{2i}) \\ 
m_1 v_{1i} + m_2 v_{2i}&=(m_1 + m_2) v_{1f} + m_2 v_{1i} - m_2 v_{2i} \\
(m_1 - m_2) v_{1i} + 2 m_2 v_{2i}&=(m_1 + m_2)\, v_{1f} \\ 
v_{1f}&=\frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2} v_{1i}+\frac{2m_2}{m_1 + m_2} v_{2i} \tag{4}\label{eq:vel1Final}\\
\end{align*}
The same process results in $v_{2f}$:

\begin{equation}
  v_{2f} =\frac{2m_1}{m_1 + m_2} v_{1i}+\frac{m_2 - m_1}{m_1 + m_2} v_{2i}\tag{5}\label{eq:vel2Final}
\end{equation}

Equations~\eqref{eq:vel1Final} and \eqref{eq:vel2Final} solve every
head-on elastic collision. Try $m_1 = m_2$ and $v_{2i} = 0$: you get
$v_{1f} = 0$ and $v_{2f} = v_{1i}$, the billiard stop shot. Now plug in our
values:
$$v_{1f} = \frac{2 - 1}{2 + 1}(3.0)
= \frac{1}{3}(3.0)
= 1.0\text{ m/s}$$
$$v_{2f} = \frac{2(2)}{2+1}(3.0)
= \frac{4}{3}(3.0)
= 4.0\text{ m/s}$$
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Car Crash}, label={car_crash}]
Sometimes, car companies will intentionally crash cars in order to test the safety of their prototypes.

Two cars collide head-on on a straight, frictionless test track, where east is considered positive.

Car A has mass $1000$ kg and is traveling \emph{east} at $20$ m/s. Car B has mass $1500$ kg and is traveling \emph{west} at $10$ m/s.

During the collision, the cars do not stick together. After the collision, Car A is observed to move east at $5$ m/s.

Find the final velocity of Car B. How much kinetic energy is lost? What
kind of collision is this?
\end{Exercise}

\begin{Answer}[ref={car_crash}]
Using the Conservation of Momentum theorem:
\begin{align*}
m_A v_{A,i} + m_B v_{B,i}&= m_A v_{A,f} + m_B v_{B,f} \\ 
(1000)(20) + (1500)(-10)&= (1000)(5) + (1500)v_{B,f} \\
20000 -15000 - 5000&= (1500)v_{B,f} \\
0&= (1500)v_{B,f} \\
v_{B,f} = 0 \text {m/s}\\
\end{align*}
Car B ends up stopped.

Before the crash, the kinetic energy is
$\frac{1}{2}(1000)(20^2) + \frac{1}{2}(1500)(10^2) = 200{,}000 + 75{,}000 = 275{,}000$ J.
Afterward, it is $\frac{1}{2}(1000)(5^2) = 12{,}500$ J. The crash
destroyed 262,500 J of kinetic energy (into bent metal, heat, and noise),
so it is inelastic. The cars did not stick together, so it is not
perfectly inelastic.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Ready, Aim, Fire!}, label=bullet_in_wood]
A $m_{bullet}=10$ g bullet is fired horizontally at $500$ m/s into a wooden block of mass $m_{block}=2.00$ kg resting on a frictionless horizontal surface. The bullet embeds itself in the block, such that a loud bang was heard and the \emph{block-bullet system} slides together after the collision.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item What is the velocity of the \emph{block-bullet system} after the collision?
  \item How much kinetic energy was lost in the collision?
  \item What percent of the bullet's kinetic energy was lost? What kind of
        collision is this?
\end{enumerate}
\end{Exercise}

\begin{Answer}[ref=bullet_in_wood]
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item Using the conservation of momentum, we can say that:
  \begin{align*}
    m_{bullet}(v_{bullet}) + m_{block}(0) &= (m_{bullet} + m_{block})v_f \\ 
    (0.01)(500) + (2)(0) &= ((0.01) + (2))v_f \\ 
    v_f &= \frac{(500)(0.01)}{2.01} \\ 
    v_f &\approx 2.49 \text{m/s}
  \end{align*}
  The \emph{block-bullet system} moves at a speed of $2.49$ m/s, in the same direction the bullet was fired.
  \item 
  To find the initial kinetic energy, we use the initial velocity:
  \[KE_i = \frac{1}{2}(0.01)(500)^2 = 1250\]
  And, to find the final kinetic energy, we use the summed masses and the calculated final velocity
  \[KE_f = \frac{1}{2}(2.01)(2.49)^2 \approx 6.23\]
  So the net energy lost is the difference:
  $$\Delta KE = KE_i - KE_f \approx 1250 - 6.23 \approx 1243.7 \,\text{J}$$
  \item 
  $$\text{Fraction lost} = \frac{\Delta KE}{KE_i}= \frac{1243.7}{1250}\approx 0.99496$$
  About $99.5\%$ of the kinetic energy is lost, almost all of it to heat
  and splintered wood. The bullet and block stick together, so the
  collision is perfectly inelastic.
\end{enumerate}
\end{Answer}

\section{Momentum, Center of Mass, and Explosions}

Picture a firework shell bursting at the top of its arc. Pieces fly in
every direction. Yet the \index{center of mass}center of mass of all those
pieces keeps sailing along the exact parabola the unexploded shell would
have followed.

\index{explosions}Why? The explosion pushes the fragments apart, but
every push on one fragment comes with an equal and opposite push on
another (Newton's third law). These internal forces cancel, so they cannot
change the total momentum. And the total momentum is the total mass times
the velocity of the center of mass:

$$M \vec v_{\text{COM}} = m_1 \vec v_1 + m_2 \vec v_2 + \dots$$
Since the explosion cannot change the left side, $\vec v_{\text{COM}}$
right after the explosion equals the velocity just before. If you know all
the fragments but one, this equation tells you the missing one.

\begin{Exercise}[title={Three-Fragment Projectile Explosion}, label=three_fragment_projectile]

A projectile of mass $6.0$ kg is launched from the ground and follows a
parabolic arc. At the very top of its trajectory, its speed is measured
to be $20$ m/s, moving horizontally.

At that instant, it explodes into three fragments:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Fragment A has mass $2.0$ kg and flies off at $40^\circ$ above the horizontal with a speed of $30$ m/s.
    \item Fragment B has mass $1.0$ kg and flies off at $60^\circ$ below the horizontal with a speed of $25$ m/s.
    \item Fragment C has mass $3.0$ kg. Its velocity is unknown.
\end{itemize}

The explosion is so quick that gravity has no time to change the momentum
during it. Everything moves in one vertical plane.

Find the \textbf{magnitude and direction} of the velocity of Fragment C.

\end{Exercise}

\begin{Answer}[ref=three_fragment_projectile]
The total momentum immediately before the explosion has two components:
\[p_{i,x} = (6)(20) = 120 \text{ kg m/s} \qquad p_{i,y} = 0\]

Let us analyze Fragment A:
$$p_{Ax} = m_A v_{Ax} = 2(30\cos 40^\circ), \qquad p_{Ay} = m_A v_{Ay} = 2(30\sin 40^\circ)$$

And Fragment B,
$$p_{Bx} = m_B v_{Bx} = 1(25\cos 60^\circ), \qquad p_{By} = m_B v_{By} = 1(-25\sin 60^\circ)$$

Since the Conservation of Momentum applies to the components:
\begin{align*}
  p_{x,\text{final}} &= p_{Ax} + p_{Bx} + p_{Cx} = 120\ \text{kg·m/s}\\
  p_{Cx} &= 120 - (p_{Ax} + p_{Bx}) \\
  &= 120 - 2(30\cos 40^\circ) - 1(25\cos 60^\circ) \\
  v_{Cx} &= \frac{120 - 2(30\cos 40^\circ) - 1(25\cos 60^\circ)}{3.0} \\ 
  &= 20.51 \text{ m/s}
\end{align*}

And same for the Y-components
\begin{align*}
  p_{y,\text{final}} &= p_{Ay} + p_{By} + p_{Cy} = 0\ \text{kg·m/s}\\
  p_{Cy} &= - (p_{Ay} + p_{By}) \\
   &= -(2(30\sin 40^\circ) + 1(-25\sin 60^\circ)) \\
  v_{Cy}&= \frac{-(38.57 - 21.65)}{3} \\ 
  &= -5.64 \text{ m/s}
\end{align*}

So Fragment C moves at $\sqrt{20.51^2 + (-5.64)^2} = 21.3 \text{ m/s}$ with a direction of $\theta=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{-5.64}{20.51}\right) = -15^\circ$ or $15^\circ$ below the horizontal.
\end{Answer}

Momentum, impulse, collisions, and explosions all rest on one fact: when
objects push only on each other, their total momentum never changes. Next,
we will use these ideas to see how boats, planes, and helicopters move.

\begin{Exercise}[title={Sticking Together}, label=mom1]
A $1$ kg cart moving at $4$ m/s hits a $3$ kg cart at rest, and they stick together. What is their final velocity?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=mom1]
$(1)(4) = (1 + 3)v$, so $v = 1$ m/s.
\end{Answer}

\chapter{Bayesian Networks}

You wake up and the lawn is wet. Did it rain, or did the sprinkler run? Your brain weighs the possibilities
without effort. A Bayesian network does the same reasoning on paper, and it scales to problems with hundreds of
variables, from medical diagnosis to deciding whether there is school today.

\section{Variables and Dependencies}\index{Bayesian network}
A \newterm{Bayesian network} is a probability model for several variables and the dependencies among them. Its
structure is a \newterm{directed acyclic graph}\index{directed acyclic graph} (DAG): each node is a variable, and
an arrow from $A$ to $B$ means $A$ directly influences $B$. ``Acyclic'' means you can never follow the arrows in a
loop back to where you started. Each node has a \newterm{conditional probability table}\index{conditional
probability table} (CPT) giving its probabilities for each combination of its parents' values.

The naive Bayes classifier from the last chapter is a small Bayesian network: one arrow from the class to each
feature.

\section{A Small Network}
Let $R$ be ``it rained'' and $W$ be ``the grass is wet,'' with the arrow $R \to W$. The network needs one number
for $R$ (since $P(\neg R) = 1 - P(R)$) and two conditional probabilities for $W$:
$$P(R) = 0.2, \qquad P(W \mid R) = 0.9, \qquad P(W \mid \neg R) = 0.1$$

\section{Inference}\index{Bayesian network!inference}
A Bayesian network answers questions about unknown variables given observed ones. Three kinds of reasoning are
common.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Prediction} (causal): given a cause, how likely is the effect? Here, $P(W \mid R) = 0.9$.
\item \textbf{Diagnosis} (evidential): given an effect, how likely is the cause? This needs Bayes' rule.
\item \textbf{Explaining away}: if two causes can produce the same effect, observing one cause lowers the
probability of the other.
\end{itemize}

For the diagnosis question, first find the total probability of wet grass:
$$P(W) = P(W \mid R)P(R) + P(W \mid \neg R)P(\neg R) = 0.9(0.2) + 0.1(0.8) = 0.26$$
Then Bayes' rule gives
$$P(R \mid W) = \frac{P(W \mid R) P(R)}{P(W)} = \frac{0.18}{0.26} \approx 0.692$$
Seeing wet grass raises the probability of rain from 0.2 to about 0.69.

\subsection{Explaining Away, with Numbers}\index{explaining away}
Now add a sprinkler $S$, so the network is $R \to W \leftarrow S$. Let $P(R) = 0.2$ and $P(S) = 0.3$,
independently, and
$$P(W \mid R, S) = 0.99, \quad P(W \mid R, \neg S) = 0.9, \quad P(W \mid \neg R, S) = 0.8, \quad
P(W \mid \neg R, \neg S) = 0.05$$
Multiplying out the four cases where the grass is wet:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|c|c}
$R$ & $S$ & $P(R, S, W)$ \\
\hline
yes & yes & $0.2 \cdot 0.3 \cdot 0.99 = 0.0594$ \\
yes & no & $0.2 \cdot 0.7 \cdot 0.9 = 0.126$ \\
no & yes & $0.8 \cdot 0.3 \cdot 0.8 = 0.192$ \\
no & no & $0.8 \cdot 0.7 \cdot 0.05 = 0.028$ \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The total is $P(W) = 0.4054$. Knowing only that the grass is wet,
$$P(R \mid W) = \frac{0.0594 + 0.126}{0.4054} \approx 0.457$$
Now you also learn that the sprinkler was on. Keep only the rows where $S$ is yes:
$$P(R \mid W, S) = \frac{0.0594}{0.0594 + 0.192} \approx 0.236$$
The sprinkler already explains the wet grass, so the probability of rain drops from 0.457 to 0.236, close to its
starting value of 0.2.

and whether your little brother announces that there is no school (which he does fairly often anyway, hoping
to make it true). The tables:

\begin{tabular}{c c}

$S$ & 0.10 \\
$I$ & 0.08 \\
\end{tabular}

$S$ & $I$ & $P(D \mid S, I)$ \\

yes & yes & 0.95 \\

\textbf{The joint distribution.} The arrows say exactly how to build the probability of any complete
combination of values: multiply each variable's probability given its parents.
$$P(s, i, d, c, b) = P(s)\,P(i)\,P(d \mid s, i)\,P(c \mid d)\,P(b \mid d)$$
Five yes-or-no variables have $2^5 = 32$ combinations. Writing out all 32 probabilities would take 31 numbers
(the last is 1 minus the rest), but the network needs only 10, the entries in the tables above. With hundreds of
variables, that saving is the difference between impossible and easy.

\textbf{Example}: Before you hear anything, what is the probability that school is closed?

\textbf{Solution}: Add up over the four combinations of the parents:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c c l}
$S$ & $I$ & $P(S)\,P(I)\,P(D \mid S, I)$ \\
\hline
yes & yes & $0.1 \cdot 0.08 \cdot 0.95 = 0.0076$ \\
yes & no & $0.1 \cdot 0.92 \cdot 0.70 = 0.0644$ \\
no & yes & $0.9 \cdot 0.08 \cdot 0.50 = 0.0360$ \\
no & no & $0.9 \cdot 0.92 \cdot 0.01 = 0.00828$ \\
\hline
 & & $P(D) = 0.11628$ \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

About a 12\% chance. Now your brother bursts in: ``No school!'' Bayes' rule, as in the rain example:
$$P(D \mid B) = \frac{0.9 \times 0.11628}{0.9 \times 0.11628 + 0.3 \times 0.88372} = \frac{0.1047}{0.3698} \approx 0.283$$
His word raises the chance from 12\% to 28\%, which is not enough to go back to bed. The robocall, which almost
never comes on a school day, is far more convincing:
$$P(D \mid C) = \frac{0.98 \times 0.11628}{0.98 \times 0.11628 + 0.02 \times 0.88372} \approx 0.866$$

\textbf{Explaining away again.} Suppose school is closed. Was there heavy snow? Use the table above: the rows
where $S$ is yes account for $0.0076 + 0.0644 = 0.072$ of the $0.11628$, so
$$P(S \mid D) = \frac{0.072}{0.11628} \approx 0.619$$
Now you look outside and see that everything is coated in ice. Keep only the rows where $I$ is yes:
$$P(S \mid D, I) = \frac{0.0076}{0.0076 + 0.0360} \approx 0.174$$
The ice storm explains the closing, so snow drops from 62\% to 17\%, which is not much above its starting value
of 10\%. Snow and ice are independent, but once you know school is closed, learning about one changes what you
believe about the other.

\section{Inference in NumPy}
For a network this small, the simplest method is the one we used by hand, called \newterm{inference by
enumeration}\index{inference by enumeration}: build the whole joint table, keep the rows that match the
evidence, and add. Here is \filename{snow\_day.py}:

\begin{minted}{python}
import numpy as np

# Each table gives P(variable = True) for every combination of its parents.
# Axis order everywhere: S (snow), I (ice), D (school closed), C (robocall), B (brother)
p_snow = 0.1
p_ice = 0.08
p_closed = np.array([[0.01, 0.5],                # index: S, I
                     [0.7, 0.95]])
p_call = np.array([0.02, 0.98])                  # index: D
p_brother = np.array([0.3, 0.9])                 # index: D

def table(p_true):
    """Stack P(False) and P(True) along a new last axis."""
    return np.stack([1 - p_true, p_true], axis=-1)

S = table(np.array(p_snow))         # shape (2,)
I = table(np.array(p_ice))          # shape (2,)
D = table(p_closed)                 # shape (2, 2, 2):    S, I, D
C = table(p_call)                   # shape (2, 2):       D, C
B = table(p_brother)                # shape (2, 2):       D, B

# The joint distribution: P(s, i, d, c, b) = P(s) P(i) P(d|s,i) P(c|d) P(b|d)
joint = np.einsum("s,i,sid,dc,db->sidcb", S, I, D, C, B)
print("Joint table shape:", joint.shape, " total:", round(joint.sum(), 10))

AXES = "SIDCB"

def probability(query, given={}):
    """P(query variable is True | given), by adding up rows of the joint table."""
    keep = joint.copy()
    for name, value in given.items():
        index = [slice(None)] * 5
        index[AXES.index(name)] = 1 - int(value)    # zero out rows that disagree
        keep[tuple(index)] = 0
    other = tuple(k for k in range(5) if k != AXES.index(query))
    marginal = keep.sum(axis=other)
    return marginal[1] / marginal.sum()

print(f"P(closed)                     = {probability('D'):.3f}")
print(f"P(closed | brother says so)   = {probability('D', {'B': True}):.3f}")
print(f"P(closed | robocall)          = {probability('D', {'C': True}):.3f}")
print(f"P(snow | closed)              = {probability('S', {'D': True}):.3f}")
print(f"P(snow | closed, icy)         = {probability('S', {'D': True, 'I': True}):.3f}")
print(f"P(snow | closed, not icy)     = {probability('S', {'D': True, 'I': False}):.3f}")
\end{minted}

\begin{verbatim}
Joint table shape: (2, 2, 2, 2, 2)  total: 1.0
P(closed)                     = 0.116
P(closed | brother says so)   = 0.283
P(closed | robocall)          = 0.866
P(snow | closed)              = 0.619
P(snow | closed, icy)         = 0.174
P(snow | closed, not icy)     = 0.886
\end{verbatim}

Each variable gets its own axis, with index 0 for false and 1 for true. For example,
\verb|joint[1, 0, 1, 1, 0]| is $P(S, \neg I, D, C, \neg B)$. The star of the program is \verb|np.einsum|\index{einsum}, short for ``Einstein
summation.'' Its first argument names the axes of each input and of the output:
\verb|"s,i,sid,dc,db->sidcb"| says ``for every combination of the five variables, multiply \verb|S[s]|,
\verb|I[i]|, \verb|D[s,i,d]|, \verb|C[d,c]|, and \verb|B[d,b]|.'' That is the factorization formula, typed almost
directly. The total is 1, as a probability distribution's must be.

To condition on evidence, \verb|probability| zeros out every entry that disagrees with it, adds over all the
other variables, and divides by the total that remains: exactly the ``keep the matching rows'' step from the
hand calculations.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l c}
\textbf{What you know} & \textbf{$P(\text{heavy snow})$} \\
\hline
nothing & 0.100 \\
school is closed & 0.619 \\
school is closed, and there is no ice & 0.886 \\
school is closed, and it is icy & 0.174 \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Enumeration doubles in cost with every variable you add: 50 yes-or-no variables would need a table with
$2^{50} \approx 10^{15}$ entries. Real systems use smarter exact methods, which add up one variable at a time, or
approximate methods based on random sampling.

\section{Learning a Network}
Learning has two parts. \newterm{Parameter learning} estimates the CPT entries from data, often by counting:
if school was closed on 14 of the 20 heavy-snow, no-ice days in the district's records, estimate
$P(D \mid S, \neg I) = 0.7$. \newterm{Structure learning} chooses the arrows, and it is harder, because many
structures fit the same data. Expert knowledge is often used to choose the structure.

\begin{Exercise}[label=bn1, title=Compute a total probability]
In the rain network, find $P(W)$ if $P(R)$ is changed to $0.5$, with the same conditional probabilities.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=bn1]
$P(W) = 0.9(0.5) + 0.1(0.5) = 0.5$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=bn2, title=Read a table]
A node $C$ has two parents, $A$ and $B$, each either true or false. How many rows does its CPT have?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=bn2]
There are $2 \times 2 = 4$ parent combinations, so the CPT has 4 rows, each giving $P(C \mid A, B)$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=bn3, title={Both messages}, space=3cm]
In the snow-day network, the robocall comes \emph{and} your brother says there is no school. Find
$P(D \mid C, B)$ by hand. (Given $D$, the call and your brother are independent.)
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=bn3]
$$P(D \mid C, B) = \frac{0.98 \times 0.9 \times 0.11628}{0.98 \times 0.9 \times 0.11628 + 0.02 \times 0.3 \times
0.88372} = \frac{0.10256}{0.10256 + 0.00530} \approx 0.951$$
Two pieces of evidence together are stronger than either alone.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=bn4, title={Count the numbers}]
A network has 6 yes-or-no variables. One has no parents, two have one parent each, and three have two parents
each. How many numbers do its tables need? How many would the full joint table need?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=bn4]
A node needs one number for each combination of its parents' values: $1 + 2(2) + 3(4) = 17$. The full joint
table has $2^6 = 64$ entries, so it needs 63 numbers (the last one is 1 minus the others).
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=bn5, title={Check the code}]
Use \verb|probability| in \filename{snow\_day.py} to check your answer to the ``Both messages'' exercise. Then
find $P(\text{ice} \mid \text{robocall})$ and $P(\text{ice} \mid \text{robocall}, \text{no heavy snow})$.
Explain the difference.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=bn5]
\begin{minted}{python}
print(probability('D', {'C': True, 'B': True}))   # 0.951
print(probability('I', {'C': True}))              # 0.330
print(probability('I', {'C': True, 'S': False}))  # 0.595
\end{minted}
Without snow, something else has
to explain the call, and the most likely explanation is ice: explaining away in reverse.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=bn6, title={Learn by simulating}]
Generate 10,000 random mornings from the network. Draw snow and ice by comparing random numbers with their
probabilities, then draw school closings using the probabilities in \verb|p_closed|. Estimate $P(S \mid D)$ by counting. How close is it to 0.619?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=bn6]
\begin{minted}{python}
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 10_000
s = rng.random(n) < p_snow
i = rng.random(n) < p_ice
d = rng.random(n) < p_closed[s.astype(int), i.astype(int)]
print(np.mean(s[d]))
\end{minted}
With this seed, 1,173 of the mornings are closings, and the fraction of them with snow is 0.618. (Different
seeds give answers that typically differ from 0.619 by about 0.014.) This is the idea behind
approximate inference by sampling.
\end{Answer}

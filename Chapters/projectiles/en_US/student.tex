\chapter{Projectile Motion}
\index{projectile motion}
A basketball arcing toward the hoop, a soccer ball off a goal kick, a diver
leaving the high board: each is a \newterm{projectile}, an object that,
once launched, moves under the influence of gravity alone. NASA uses the
same physics to aim rovers at Mars.

In the kinematics chapter, you handled objects that fall straight down. Now
we add sideways motion. The key idea is surprisingly simple: the sideways
motion and the up-and-down motion ignore each other. Once you see that, you
can predict where a ball will land, how high it will go, and what angle
sends it farthest. At the end, you will write a simulation that handles any
projectile, even one slowed by air. 

\section{Comparing Projectiles}
Hold one coin on your fingertip and another on the edge of a table. Flick
the table coin off sideways at the same moment you drop the other one.
Listen: they hit the floor at the same time. This video shows the same
experiment with balls: \url{https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zMF4CD7i3hg}.

Adding sideways speed does not change how fast an object falls. This is
called \emph{independence of motion}.


\section{Independence of Motion}
\index{independence of motion}
For a projectile, the horizontal and vertical parts of the motion are
independent. Gravity pulls only downward, so it changes only the vertical
velocity. Nothing pushes sideways (if we ignore air resistance), so the
horizontal velocity never changes.

Vertically, the projectile behaves exactly like the falling objects in the
kinematics chapter: constant acceleration of $9.8 \frac{m}{s^2}$ downward.
(We often round to $10 \frac{m}{s^2}$ to make the arithmetic easier.)

Horizontally, it coasts at constant speed.

The two motions are tied together by just one thing: time. The ball lands
when its vertical motion says it hits the ground, and that same moment ends
its horizontal travel. So we use separate equations for each direction
(figure \ref{independenceOfMotionEqs}) and connect them through $t$.
\begin{figure}
    \centering

        \hline
        \underline{Horizontal Motion} & \underline{Vertical Motion} \\
        \hline
        $\Delta x = v_{0x} t$ & $\Delta y = v_{0y} t + \dfrac{1}{2}(-g)t^2$ \\
        $v_x = v_{0x}$ (constant!) & $v_y = v_{0y} + (-g)t$ \\
        $a_x = 0$ & $a_y = -g$ \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Projectile motion equations from kinematics equations. }\label{independenceOfMotionEqs}
\end{figure}
\index{projectile motion!equations of}
Note that the velocity in the horizontal direction is \emph{\textbf{constant}}, while the velocity in the vertical direction is \emph{\textbf{not constant}} due to the acceleration of gravity ($9.8 \frac{m}{s^2}$ downward).

\section{Horizontally-launched Projectiles}
Imagine a ball is thrown horizontally off of a cliff. The ball has an initial horizontal velocity of $20$ m/s, but no initial vertical velocity. The ball will continue to move horizontally at a constant velocity, while simultaneously accelerating downward due to gravity. The ball falls for 5 seconds before hitting the ground. We will use $-10 \frac{m}{s^2}$ for the acceleration due to gravity to make calculations easier. See figure \ref{fig:horiz_launch}.

Let us calculate the change in $y$. 

The vertical motion can be described by the equation:

\[
\Delta y = v_{0y} t + \frac{1}{2} (-g) t^2
\]

Since the initial vertical velocity $v_{0y} = 0$, this simplifies to:

\[
\Delta y = \frac{1}{2} (-g) t^2
\]

Substituting in the values for $g$ and $t$:

\[
\Delta y = \frac{1}{2} (-10 \frac{m}{s^2}) (5 s)^2
\]

Calculating this gives:

\[
\Delta y = -125 m
\]

So the ball falls 125 m before hitting the ground. The cliff is 125 m
tall.

Now let us calculate the change in $x$.
The horizontal motion can be described by the equation:

\[
\Delta x = v_{0x} t
\]

Substituting in the values for $v_{0x}$ and $t$:
\[
\Delta x = (20 \frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}) (5 \text{s}) = 100 \text{m}
\]

\begin{Exercise}[title=Horizontally-launched Projectile, label=projectiles_horiz1]
A rock is thrown horizontally off of the edge of a cliff with an initial velocity of $8$ m/s. If the rock falls for $3$ seconds before hitting the ground, (a) how far from the base of the cliff does the rock land? (b) How high is the cliff? 

Use $-10 \frac{m}{s^2}$ for the acceleration due to gravity.
\end{Exercise}

\begin{Answer}[ref=projectiles_horiz1]
(a) To find how far from the base of the cliff the rock lands, we can use the horizontal motion equation:
\begin{align*}
    \Delta x &= v_{0x} t\\
    \Delta x &= (8 \tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}})(3 \text{s}) = 24 \text{m}
\end{align*}
(b) To find the height of the cliff, we can use the vertical motion equation. Since $v_{0y} = 0$, and the initial vertical position is at the top of the cliff (we can call this $y_0 = 0$), we have:

\begin{align*}
    y_f &= v_{0y} t + \frac{1}{2} (-g) t^2 \\
    y_f &= 0 + \frac{1}{2} (-10) (3)^{2} \\
    y_f &= -45 m
\end{align*}

We get a negative value for $y_f$ because the rock is falling downward from its initial position, the top of the cliff which we selected as the origin. Therefore, the height of the cliff is $45$ meters.

So the rock lands $24$ meters from the base of the cliff, and the cliff is $45$ meters high.

\end{Answer}

\subsection{Newton's Cannon and Escape Velocity}
Isaac Newton imagined a cannon on top of a mountain so tall that it pokes
above the atmosphere. This thought experiment is called
\index{Newton's cannon}Newton's cannon. Fire the cannonball horizontally,
and one of three things happens:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The cannonball would not have enough horizontal velocity, and fall back to the Earth. 
    \item The cannonball would get shot at enough velocity to be in continuous orbit around the Earth. (This is how satellites stay in orbit around the Earth!).
    \item The cannonball would get shot at such a high velocity that it would escape the Earth's gravitational pull, and fly off into space. This is known as \emph{escape velocity}. \index{escape velocity}
\end{itemize}

Take a look at this simulation of Newton's cannon: \url{https://physics.weber.edu/schroeder/software/NewtonsCannon.html}. Then, watch this video: \url{https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ALRdYPMpqQs}.

Here is the beautiful part. The cannonball is always falling. But Earth
is round, so the ground curves away beneath it. Fire it fast enough (about
7,900 m/s near Earth's surface), and the ground curves away exactly as fast
as the ball falls. The ball falls forever without landing. That is an
orbit. The International Space Station is doing this right now. For Earth,
the escape velocity is about 11,200 m/s.

For any given planet, the escape velocity can be calculated using the formula:
$${\displaystyle v_{\text{e}}={\sqrt {\frac {2GM}{d}}}}$$
where $G$ is the gravitational constant, $M$ is the mass of the planet, and $d$ is the distance from the center of the planet to the object. We will cover this in more detail in the orbits chapter.
\index{Escape Velocity}
\section{Projectiles Launched at an Angle}

\index{projectile motion!at an angle}
\subsection{From the Ground}

Now, let us imagine a ball is launched from the ground at an angle.
The angle of launch influences both horizontal and vertical components of the initial velocity. 
We can use trigonometry to separate the \index{components of velocity}initial velocity into its horizontal and vertical components.

\[            
v_{0x} = v_0 \cos(\theta) \qquad \qquad
v_{0y} = v_0 \sin(\theta) 
\]

A horizontal throw is the special case $\theta = 0^\circ$: then
$v_{0x} = v_0$ and $v_{0y} = 0$. At any other angle, the launch speed is
shared between the two directions, so each component is smaller than $v_0$.

The horizontal velocity stays $v_{0x}$ the whole flight. The vertical
velocity $v_y$ starts at $v_{0y}$ and drops by $9.8 \frac{m}{s}$ every
second. At the peak, $v_y = 0$: the ball has stopped rising and has not
started falling.

For example, take a watermelon launched at $20 \frac{m}{s}$ at an angle of $30^\circ$ above the horizontal. What would the horizontal and vertical components of the initial velocity be?

Well, we can use the equations above to find out:
$$
v_{0x} = (20) \cos(30^\circ) \approx 17.32 \text{ m/s}, \qquad v_{0y} = (20) \sin(30^\circ) = 10 \text{ m/s}
$$

Projectile paths are \index{symmetry}symmetric. At any given height, the
ball is rising on the way up exactly as fast as it is falling on the way
down. The way down is a mirror image of the way up.

When the ball lands at the same height it was launched from (level
ground), its horizontal distance, or \newterm{range}, has a neat formula.
You will derive it in Exercise \ref{projectiles_angle2}.
\begin{mdframed}[frametitle={Range Formula},style=important]
    \begin{equation}
        R = \frac{v_0^2 \sin(2\theta)}{g}
    \end{equation}
    where the object starts and lands at the same height.
\end{mdframed}

\textbf{Worked example.} A ball is launched at $20$ m/s at $30^\circ$
above horizontal on level ground. Take $g = 9.8$ m/s$^2$. How high does it
go, and how far?

\textbf{Solution.} $v_{0y} = 20 \sin 30^\circ = 10$ m/s. At the peak,
$v_y = 0$, so the rise time is $\frac{10}{9.8} \approx 1.02$ s and the
height is $\frac{(10)^2}{2(9.8)} \approx 5.1$ m. The range is
$R = \frac{20^2 \sin 60^\circ}{9.8} = \frac{400 \cdot 0.866}{9.8} \approx 35.3$ m.
\index{Range}

\begin{Exercise}[title=Projectile Motion at an angle, label=projectiles_angle1]
A pumpkin is launched at $25$ m/s at an angle of $40^\circ$ above the horizontal. Find (a) the peak height of the pumpkin, and (b) the horizontal distance the pumpkin travels before hitting the ground. Use $-10 \frac{m}{s^2}$ for the acceleration due to gravity. You may assume the ground is level the entire time. 
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=projectiles_angle1]
Let us first find the horizontal and vertical components of the initial velocity:
\begin{align*}
v_{0x} &= (25) \cos(40^\circ) \approx 19.15 \text{ m/s}, \\
v_{0y} &= (25) \sin(40^\circ) \approx 16.07 \text{ m/s}.
\end{align*}

(a) To find the peak height, we can use the vertical motion equations. Taking it in two steps, let us first find the time to reach the peak height, where the vertical velocity $v_y = 0$:
\begin{align*}
    v_y = v_{0y} + (-g) t \\
    0 = 16.07 - 10t \\
    t_{peak} = \frac{16.07}{10} \approx 1.607 \text{ s}
\end{align*}
Now, we can use this time to find the peak height using the vertical displacement equation:
\begin{align*}
    \Delta y &= v_{0y} t + \frac{1}{2} (-g) t^2 \\
    \Delta y &= (16.07)(1.607) + \frac{1}{2} (-10) (1.607)^2 \\
    \Delta y &\approx 12.91 \text{ m}
\end{align*}

(b) To find the total horizontal distance traveled, we first need the total \index{time of flight}time of flight. Since the motion is symmetric, the total time will be twice the time to reach the peak height:
\[
t_{total} = 2t_{peak} \approx 2(1.607) \approx 3.214 \text{ s} = 3.21 \text{ s}
\]
Since there is no horizontal acceleration, we can use the horizontal motion equation to find the horizontal distance:
\[
\Delta x = v_{0x} t_{total} \approx (19.15)(3.21) \approx 61.5 \text{ m}
\]

So, the peak height of the pumpkin is approximately \(12.91\) m, and the horizontal distance it travels before hitting the ground is approximately \(61.5\) m.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title=Launch Angle for Maximum Range, label=projectiles_angle2]
(a) At what \index{launch angle}angle should you launch for an object to go the furthest given a maximum launch velocity? 
(b) How can you get the same horizontal distance with two different launch angles?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=projectiles_angle2]
\index{maximum range}There is a trade-off. Aim low, and the ball moves
fast sideways but lands quickly. Aim high, and it stays up a long time but
barely moves sideways. The best compromise is $45^\circ$. Here is the
proof.

Let the initial launch speed be $v$ and the launch angle be $\theta$. The horizontal and vertical velocity components are
\[
v_x = v \cos\theta, \qquad v_y = v \sin\theta.
\]

The ball lands when its height returns to zero: $v \sin\theta \, t -
\frac{1}{2} g t^2 = 0$, or
\[
0 = t\Big(v \sin\theta - \tfrac{1}{2} g t\Big)
\]

This has two solutions: $t = 0$ (the time at launch) and
\[
t = \frac{2 v \sin\theta}{g}.
\]

Thus, the horizontal range is
\[
R_x = v_x \cdot t = v \cos\theta \cdot \frac{2 v \sin\theta}{g}
    = \frac{v^2}{g} \sin(2\theta).
\]

The function $\sin(2\theta)$ reaches its maximum value of 1 when $2\theta = 90^\circ$, or $\theta = 45^\circ$. Therefore, the optimal launch angle for maximum horizontal distance is $45^\circ$.

(b) Since $\sin(2\theta) = \sin(180^\circ - 2\theta) = \sin(2(90^\circ -
\theta))$, the angles $\theta$ and $90^\circ - \theta$ give the same range. There are two ways to get a certain horizontal launch distance, one at an angle less than $45^\circ$ and one at an angle greater than $45^\circ$. For example, a projectile launched at $30^\circ$ will travel the same horizontal distance as one launched at $60^\circ$, assuming the same initial velocity. However, the projectile launched at $30^\circ$ will spend less time in the air and have a lower peak height than the one launched at $60^\circ$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title=Projectile Motion at an angle, label=projectiles_angle3]
A target is 100 meters away. Your bow launches arrows at 14 meters per
second. At what angle should you aim? If you cannot reach the target,
what is the slowest launch speed that could? Use $-10 \frac{m}{s^2}$ for the acceleration due to gravity. You may assume the ground is level the entire time.
\end{Exercise}

\begin{Answer}[ref=projectiles_angle3]

Using the horizontal distance equation, $R$, with $v_0 = 14$ m/s and $R = 100$ m, we have:
\begin{align*}
R&=\frac{v^2\sin(2\theta)}{g} \\ 
\sin(2\theta) &= \frac{Rg}{v^2} \\
\sin(2\theta) &= \frac{(100)(10)}{14^2} \approx 5.10
\end{align*}
Since the sine of an angle cannot be greater than 1, it is impossible to hit the target with a launch speed of 14 m/s.

Instead, we can calculate the minimum launch speed needed to hit the target at a $45^\circ$ angle, while satisfying the $100$ m requirement. Assuming the $\sin(2\theta) = 1$ for a $45^\circ$ launch angle, we can rearrange the range equation to solve for $v$:
\begin{align*}
v &= \sqrt{\frac{Rg}{\sin(2\theta)}} \\
v &= \sqrt{\frac{(100)(10)}{1}} = \sqrt{1000} \approx 31.62 \text{ m/s}
\end{align*}

So at an assumed launch angle of $45^\circ$, a minimum launch speed of approximately $31.62$ m/s is needed to hit the target 100 meters away.
\end{Answer}




\subsection{From a Height Above the Ground}
Now launch at an angle from a height, like throwing a ball from the top of
the bleachers.

\textbf{Worked example.} A ball is launched from a height of $50$ m at
$30^\circ$ above the horizontal at $20$ m/s. Use $g = 10 \frac{m}{s^2}$.
Find its peak height, how long it flies, and where it lands.

\textbf{Solution.} The components of the initial velocity are

$$v_{0x} = (20) \cos(30^\circ) \approx 17.32 \text{ m/s}, \qquad v_{0y} = (20) \sin(30^\circ) = 10 \text{ m/s}.$$
Measure height from the ground, so the ball starts at $y_0 = 50$ m:
\[y = y_0 + v_{0y} t + \frac{1}{2} (-g) t^2 = 50 + 10t - 5t^2\]

Horizontally:
\[x = v_{0x} t = 17.32t\]

To find the peak of the projectile, we can set $v_y = 0$ and solve for $t$:
\begin{align*}
    v_y &= v_{0y} + (-g) t \\
    0 &= 10 - 10t \\
    t_{peak} &= 1 \text{ s}
\end{align*}

Substituting this time into the vertical and horizontal equations, we can find the peak height and horizontal distance at the peak:
\[
y = 50 + 10(1) - 5(1)^2 = 55 \text{ m}
\]
\[
x = 17.32(1) = 17.32 \text{ m}
\]

The ball lands when $y = 0$:
\begin{align*}
    0 &= 50 + 10t - 5t^2 \\
    0 &= -5t^2 + 10t + 50 \\
    0 &= t^2 - 2t - 10
\end{align*}

Using the quadratic formula from the Solving Quadratics chapter, we find:
\begin{align*}
    t &= \frac{2 \pm \sqrt{(-2)^2 - 4(1)(-10)}}{2(1)} \\
    &= \frac{2 \pm \sqrt{4 + 40}}{2} \\
    &= \frac{2 \pm \sqrt{44}}{2} \\
    &= 1 \pm \sqrt{11} \approx 4.32 \text{ s (taking the positive root)}
\end{align*}

(The negative root, about $-2.32$ s, is when a ball on this same path would
have left the ground before the launch. It is not part of our flight.)

In 4.32 s, the ball travels $x = 17.32(4.32) \approx 74.8$ m sideways.

\section{Simulating Projectile Motion}

Formulas are great for clean problems. Real projectiles feel air
resistance, and then the formulas stop working. A simulation does not
care. It chops time into tiny steps and, in each one, nudges the position by
the velocity and the velocity by the acceleration.

Create \filename{projectile.py}:

\begin{minted}{python}
import math
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

g = 9.8          # m/s^2
dt = 0.001       # s, the size of each time step
speed = 20.0     # m/s
angle = math.radians(45)

x, y = 0.0, 0.0
vx = speed * math.cos(angle)
vy = speed * math.sin(angle)
xs, ys = [x], [y]

while y >= 0:
    x += vx * dt     # move
    y += vy * dt
    vy -= g * dt     # gravity changes only the vertical velocity
    xs.append(x)
    ys.append(y)

print(f"Range: {x:.1f} m")
plt.plot(xs, ys)
plt.xlabel("x (m)")
plt.ylabel("y (m)")
plt.gca().set_aspect("equal")
plt.show()
\end{minted}

Run it. It prints \texttt{Range: 40.8 m} and draws the arc. The range
formula agrees: $\frac{20^2 \sin 90^\circ}{9.8} \approx 40.8$ m. Notice
that \mintinline{python}{vx} never changes inside the loop. That is
independence of motion, written in Python.

\begin{Exercise}[title={Adding air}, label=proj_drag]
Air resistance pushes against the velocity, and it grows with the square of
the speed. Replace the line \mintinline{python}{vy -= g * dt} with
\begin{minted}{python}
    k = 0.01                       # drag strength, per meter
    s = math.hypot(vx, vy)         # current speed
    vx -= k * s * vx * dt
    vy -= (g + k * s * vy) * dt
\end{minted}
What is the range now? Try several angles between $30^\circ$ and
$50^\circ$. Is $45^\circ$ still the best?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=proj_drag]
At $45^\circ$, the range drops from 40.8 m to about 31.36 m. Trying angles
shows the best angle is now a little lower: $43^\circ$ gives about 31.43 m. Drag steals speed
the whole time the ball is in the air, so a slightly flatter, shorter
flight wins. That is why the best launch angle for a real soccer ball or
golf ball is below $45^\circ$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Range at 45 Degrees}, label=proj1]
A ball is launched at $20$ m/s at $45^\circ$. Take $g = 9.8$ m/s$^2$. What is its range?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=proj1]
$R = \frac{v^2 \sin 90^\circ}{g} = \frac{400}{9.8} \approx 40.8$ m.
\end{Answer}

\chapter{Linear Regression and Gradient Descent}

You are hiking down a mountain when the fog rolls in. You cannot see the parking lot, or even the trail. But
you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet, so you take a step downhill. Then another. As long as
the mountain has one lowest point, you will eventually get there.

That is \newterm{gradient descent}\index{gradient descent}, and it is how nearly every model after this chapter
is trained, including the neural networks at the end of the unit. Here we use it on a problem we have already
solved, linear regression, so you can check every answer against the least squares formula.

\section{Why Not Just Use the Formula?}
The Least Squares chapter gave an exact answer: solve $X^T X \mathbf{b} = X^T \mathbf{y}$. So why walk
downhill in the fog when you have a map?
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Most models have no formula.} Logistic regression, in the next chapter, and neural networks have
no equation you can solve for the best parameters. You can only search for them.
\item \textbf{Big problems.} With a million features, $X^T X$ has a trillion entries. Gradient descent
never forms it.
\item \textbf{Big data.} Gradient descent can learn from a small batch of examples at a time, so the data does
not have to fit in memory at once.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Cost Function and Its Gradient}
We call the parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ (theta), which is the usual name in machine learning; for a line,
$\boldsymbol{\theta} = (\theta_0, \theta_1)$ is the intercept and the slope. The ``height of the mountain'' is the
\newterm{cost function}\index{cost function}, here the mean squared error:
$$J(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (\mathbf{x}_i^T\boldsymbol{\theta} - y_i)^2
= \frac{1}{n}\|X\boldsymbol{\theta} - \mathbf{y}\|^2$$
where $\mathbf{x}_i$ is row $i$ of the design matrix (a 1 followed by the features). The gradient, the
direction of steepest uphill, is
$$\nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{2}{n} X^T(X\boldsymbol{\theta} - \mathbf{y})$$
Read it from the inside out: $X\boldsymbol{\theta} - \mathbf{y}$ is the vector of prediction errors, and
multiplying by $X^T$ asks, for each parameter, ``if I nudged this one, how would the errors change?''

\section{The Update Rule}\index{learning rate}
Start with a guess $\boldsymbol{\theta}_0$, usually all zeros. Then repeat:
\begin{mdframed}[style=important]
$$\boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} - \alpha \nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta})$$
\end{mdframed}
The minus sign means ``go downhill.'' The \newterm{learning rate} $\alpha$ sets the step size. Too small and
you creep; too large and you overshoot the bottom of the valley and land higher up the other side.

\textbf{Example}: Take one step on the quiz data from the Least Squares chapter, $x = 1, \ldots, 5$ and
$y = 2.1, 3.9, 6.2, 7.8, 10.1$, starting at $\boldsymbol{\theta}_0 = (0, 0)$ with $\alpha = 0.01$.

\textbf{Solution}: With $\boldsymbol{\theta}_0 = \mathbf{0}$, every prediction is 0, so
$X\boldsymbol{\theta}_0 - \mathbf{y} = -\mathbf{y}$. The first entry of $X^T(-\mathbf{y})$ is $-\sum y_i = -30.1$,
and the second is $-\sum x_i y_i = -110.2$. With $n = 5$,
$$\nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0) = \frac{2}{5}(-30.1, -110.2) = (-12.04, -44.08)$$
$$\boldsymbol{\theta}_1 = (0, 0) - 0.01(-12.04, -44.08) = (0.1204, 0.4408)$$
The cost drops from $J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0) = 44.18$ to $J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_1) = 25.77$. The answer
is $(0.05, 1.99)$, so we are heading the right way, with a long way to go.

\section{Letting the Computer Walk}
By hand, two steps is plenty. A computer can take thousands. Here is \filename{quiz\_descent.py}:
\begin{minted}{python}
import numpy as np

x = np.array([1, 2, 3, 4, 5])
y = np.array([2.1, 3.9, 6.2, 7.8, 10.1])
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(x)), x])

def descend(alpha, steps):
    theta = np.zeros(2)
    for _ in range(steps):
        gradient = 2 / len(y) * X.T @ (X @ theta - y)
        theta = theta - alpha * gradient
    return theta

print(descend(0.05, 50))
print(descend(0.05, 5000))
print(descend(0.1, 50))
\end{minted}
\begin{verbatim}
[0.24846555 1.93502823]
[0.05 1.99]
[ -3074524.42217419 -11100007.21998943]
\end{verbatim}
With $\alpha = 0.05$, 50 steps get close and 5000 steps land on the least squares answer. Doubling the learning
rate to $0.1$ makes every step overshoot by more than the last, and after 50 steps the parameters are in the
millions.

\section{Valleys and Learning Rates}
Figure~\ref{fig:contour-paths} draws the cost as a contour map, like a hiking map, with the minimum marked
by a star. The contours are long, thin ovals: the cost changes quickly if you change the slope $\theta_1$, but
slowly if you change the intercept $\theta_0$. That shape causes most of the trouble with gradient descent.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{contour_paths.png}
\caption{Contours of the cost for the quiz data, and two gradient descent paths from $(0,0)$.}
\label{fig:contour-paths}
\end{figure}

With $\alpha = 0.01$ (blue), the path heads quickly down to the floor of the valley, then crawls along it. With
$\alpha = 0.08$ (red), each step is so big that it crosses the valley, and the path zigzags from wall to wall
while slowly working its way toward the star. A little bigger and the zigzags grow instead of shrinking.
Figure~\ref{fig:learning-rates} shows the cost after each step for four learning rates.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{learning_rates.png}
\caption{Cost against step on the quiz data. The vertical axis is logarithmic.}
\label{fig:learning-rates}
\end{figure}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{8cm}}
\textbf{Learning rate} & \textbf{What happens on the quiz data} \\
\hline
$0.001$ & steady but slow; still far from the bottom after 100 steps \\
$0.01$ & drops fast, then crawls along the valley floor \\
$0.08$ & zigzags, but ends lowest after 100 steps \\
$0.09$ & each zigzag is bigger than the last: the cost explodes \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The cutoff between 0.08 and 0.09 is not luck. For a squared-error cost, gradient descent converges exactly
when $\alpha$ is less than 2 divided by the largest eigenvalue of the matrix $\frac{2}{n}X^TX$. For the quiz
data, that is about $0.0845$. In practice, people just try a few values, as in the table, and keep the one
whose cost goes down fastest without blowing up.

\section{Standardize First}\index{standardization!gradient descent}
Now the penguins. In the Preparing Data for Models chapter, standardizing features mattered because of
distances. For gradient descent, it matters even more.

With raw flipper lengths around 200 mm, the cost valley is stretched more than 8 million times longer in one
direction than the other. The largest learning rate that does not blow up is about $0.0000246$, so steps along
the valley are microscopic. Standardize the flipper lengths and the valley becomes a perfectly round bowl.
Here is \filename{penguin\_descent.py}:

\begin{minted}{python}
import numpy as np
from penguins import load_penguins

data = load_penguins()
flipper = data["flipper_length_mm"]
y = data["body_mass_g"]
n = len(y)

def descend(X, alpha, steps):
    theta = np.zeros(X.shape[1])
    for _ in range(steps):
        gradient = 2 / n * X.T @ (X @ theta - y)
        theta = theta - alpha * gradient
    rmse = np.sqrt(np.mean((X @ theta - y)**2))
    return theta, rmse

# Raw flipper lengths: anything much above 2e-5 blows up
X_raw = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), flipper])
for steps in [100, 10_000, 1_000_000]:
    theta, rmse = descend(X_raw, 2e-5, steps)
    print(f"raw, {steps:>9,} steps:", np.round(theta, 1) + 0.0, f"RMSE {rmse:.0f}")

# Standardized flipper lengths
mean, sd = np.mean(flipper), np.std(flipper)
X_std = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), (flipper - mean) / sd])
for steps in [10, 30, 100]:
    theta, rmse = descend(X_std, 0.1, steps)
    print(f"standardized, {steps:>3} steps:", np.round(theta, 1),
          f"RMSE {rmse:.0f}")

# Convert back to grams per millimeter
b1 = theta[1] / sd
b0 = theta[0] - b1 * mean
print(f"mass = {b0:.1f} + {b1:.2f} * flipper")
\end{minted}

\begin{verbatim}
raw,       100 steps: [ 0.  21.1] RMSE 566
raw,    10,000 steps: [-11.2  21.1] RMSE 565
raw, 1,000,000 steps: [-1030.7    26.2] RMSE 517
standardized,  10 steps: [3755.3  626.5] RMSE 603
standardized,  30 steps: [4201.8  701. ] RMSE 392
standardized, 100 steps: [4207.1  701.9] RMSE 392
mass = -5872.1 + 50.15 * flipper
\end{verbatim}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l r r}
\textbf{Flipper length} & \textbf{Steps} & \textbf{RMSE (g)} \\
\hline
raw & 1,000,000 & 517 \\
standardized & 30 & 392 \\
least squares formula & --- & 392 \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A million steps on raw data still have not found the line, because the intercept needs to reach $-5872$ and
it is moving a few thousandths of a gram per step. Thirty steps on standardized data match the formula.
Converting back gives the same line as the Least Squares chapter. On the standardized scale, the
coefficients are easy to read, too: $\theta_0 = 4207$ g is the average mass, and $\theta_1 = 702$ g is the
change in mass for a flipper one standard deviation (14 mm) longer.

\section{Mini-Batches}\index{stochastic gradient descent}\index{mini-batch}
Each step above used all 333 penguins to compute the gradient. With a billion examples, one step would take a
very long time. \newterm{Mini-batch gradient descent} estimates the gradient from a small random batch,
say 32 examples, and steps right away. The estimate is noisy, but you get many more steps for the same
amount of work. (With a batch of a single example, the method is called \newterm{stochastic gradient descent},
or SGD; people often use that name for mini-batches, too.)

One full pass through the data is called an \newterm{epoch}\index{epoch}. Here is \filename{minibatch.py}:
\begin{minted}{python}
import numpy as np
from penguins import load_penguins

data = load_penguins()
flipper = data["flipper_length_mm"]
y = data["body_mass_g"]
n = len(y)
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), (flipper - np.mean(flipper)) / np.std(flipper)])

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
theta = np.zeros(2)
alpha, batch_size = 0.1, 32
for epoch in range(1, 6):
    order = rng.permutation(n)                  # shuffle once per epoch
    for start in range(0, n, batch_size):
        rows = order[start:start + batch_size]
        Xb, yb = X[rows], y[rows]
        gradient = 2 / len(rows) * Xb.T @ (Xb @ theta - yb)
        theta = theta - alpha * gradient
    rmse = np.sqrt(np.mean((X @ theta - y)**2))
    print(f"epoch {epoch}: theta = {np.round(theta, 1)}, RMSE = {rmse:.1f}")
\end{minted}

\begin{verbatim}
epoch 1: theta = [3822.4  648.1], RMSE = 551.9
epoch 2: theta = [4179.6  685.6], RMSE = 393.5
epoch 3: theta = [4219.4  685.9], RMSE = 392.7
epoch 4: theta = [4181.5  712. ], RMSE = 393.1
epoch 5: theta = [4207.4  692.1], RMSE = 392.3
\end{verbatim}

After two epochs, the mini-batch version is within 2 g of the best possible RMSE. The full-batch version, with
one step per epoch, would still be above 2700 g (Figure~\ref{fig:minibatch}). Notice that the mini-batch
$\boldsymbol{\theta}$ never settles down exactly; it jitters around the answer, because each batch pulls in a
slightly different direction. In practice, people often shrink $\alpha$ over time to calm the jitter.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{minibatch.png}
\caption{RMSE on the penguins against the number of passes through the data. The vertical axis is
logarithmic.}
\label{fig:minibatch}
\end{figure}

You will see this exact loop (shuffle, slice a batch, compute the gradient, step) again when you train neural
networks with PyTorch.

\begin{Exercise}[label=gd1, title=One more step, space=4cm]
Starting from $\boldsymbol{\theta}_1 = (0.1204, 0.4408)$, take one gradient step with $\alpha = 0.01$ on the quiz
data. Find $\boldsymbol{\theta}_2$, rounding to four decimal places.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=gd1]
The predictions are $X\boldsymbol{\theta}_1 = (0.5612, 1.0020, 1.4428, 1.8836, 2.3244)$. The errors are
$X\boldsymbol{\theta}_1 - \mathbf{y} = (-1.5388, -2.8980, -4.7572, -5.9164, -7.7756)$. Then
$X^T(X\boldsymbol{\theta}_1 - \mathbf{y}) = (-22.886, -84.150)$, so
$$\nabla J = \tfrac{2}{5}(-22.886, -84.150) = (-9.1544, -33.660)$$
$$\boldsymbol{\theta}_2 = (0.1204, 0.4408) - 0.01(-9.1544, -33.660) = (0.2119, 0.7774)$$
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=gd2, title=Learning rate]
What happens to gradient descent if $\alpha$ is made very large? What if it is made very small?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=gd2]
Very large: the steps overshoot the minimum. The parameters oscillate or diverge, and the cost can increase
instead of decrease. Very small: the cost goes down every step, but so slowly that you may run out of patience
(or computer time) long before reaching the minimum.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=gd3, title={A one-parameter mountain}, space=4cm]
Fit a line through the origin, $\hat{y} = \theta x$, to the points $(1, 2)$ and $(2, 3)$, so
$$J(\theta) = \tfrac{1}{2}\left[(\theta - 2)^2 + (2\theta - 3)^2\right]$$
Find $J'(\theta)$. Then, starting at $\theta = 0$ with $\alpha = 0.2$, take two steps. What value is gradient descent heading for?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=gd3]
$J'(\theta) = (\theta - 2) + 2(2\theta - 3) = 5\theta - 8$. Step 1: $\theta = 0 - 0.2(-8) = 1.6$. Step 2:
$J'(1.6) = 0$, so $\theta$ stays at $1.6$. The minimum is where $5\theta - 8 = 0$, at $\theta = 1.6$. (This
learning rate happens to land on it in one step.)
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=gd4, title={Find the cliff}]
On the quiz data, try learning rates from 0.080 to 0.090 in steps of 0.002, with 2000 steps each. Which
ones find the answer? Where is the cutoff?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=gd4]
\begin{minted}{python}
for alpha in np.arange(0.080, 0.0901, 0.002):
    print(f"{alpha:.3f}", np.round(descend(alpha, 2000), 3))
\end{minted}
0.080, 0.082, and 0.084 converge; 0.086, 0.088, and 0.090 blow up. The cutoff is about 0.0845, matching the
eigenvalue rule in the chapter.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=gd5, title={Two features}]
Extend \filename{penguin\_descent.py} to predict mass from standardized flipper length \emph{and} standardized
bill depth (three parameters). Run 200 steps with $\alpha = 0.1$, and compare your answer with the
one from \verb|lstsq|.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=gd5]
\begin{minted}{python}
depth = data["bill_depth_mm"]
Z = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), (flipper - mean) / sd,
                     (depth - np.mean(depth)) / np.std(depth)])
theta, rmse = descend(Z, 0.1, 200)
print(np.round(theta, 1), round(rmse, 1))
print(np.round(np.linalg.lstsq(Z, y)[0], 1))
\end{minted}
Both give about $(4207.1, 724.5, 39.1)$, and the RMSE is 391 g. With two standardized features that are
correlated, the bowl is no longer perfectly round, but it is round enough that 200 steps are plenty.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=gd6, title={Batch size}]
In \filename{minibatch.py}, try batch sizes of 1, 8, 32, and 333 for five epochs each. Which gets close
in the fewest epochs? Which ends with the steadiest $\boldsymbol{\theta}$? Explain the trade-off.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=gd6]
A batch size of 8 is close after one epoch (RMSE about 402 g), because it takes 42 steps per epoch. A batch
size of 1 takes the most steps, but each one follows a single penguin, so $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ jumps by
more than 100 g at a time, and the RMSE after five epochs (about 450 g) is worse than after four. A batch of 333
is ordinary gradient descent: perfectly smooth, but only five steps in five epochs, so the RMSE is still about
1450 g. Small batches buy speed with noise; batches of a few dozen get most of the speed with little noise,
which is why they are the usual choice.
\end{Answer}

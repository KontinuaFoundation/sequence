\chapter{How Cameras Work}

It is a sunny day, and you are standing in a field a few meters from a
cow. You pull out your phone and snap a picture. In a few thousandths of a
second, light that left the Sun eight minutes ago bounces off the cow,
squeezes through a lens smaller than a pea, and lands on a chip with 24
million tiny light meters. Let us follow that light the whole way.

\section{The Light That Shines On the Cow}

The sun is a sphere of hot gas. About 70\% of the gas is
hydrogen. About 28\% is helium. There is also a little carbon, nitrogen,
and oxygen.

Gradually, the sun is converting hydrogen into helium through a
process known as ``nuclear fusion'' (which we will be discussing more in a future chapter). A large amount of heat is created in this
process. This heat makes the gases glow.

How does heat make things glow? The heat pushes the electrons into
higher orbitals. When they come back down to a lower orbital, they release a photon, which
travels away at the speed of light. (Recall from the photons chapter that
light is a stream of these little packets of energy.)
\begin{figure}[H]
    \includegraphics[width=.5\textwidth]{cow2.png}
    \includegraphics[width=.5\textwidth]{cow3.png}
    \caption{Photons from the Sun travel to the cow.}
    \label{fig:photonreleases}
\end{figure}

Heat is not the only way to push the electrons into a higher
orbital. For example, a fluorescent lightbulb is filled with gas.
When we pass electricity through the gas, its electrons are moved to a
higher orbital. When they fall back to a lower orbital, light is created.

What is the frequency of the wave that the photon travels on?
Depending on what orbital it falls from and how far it falls, the
photon created has different amounts of energy. The amount of energy
determines the frequency of the electromagnetic wave, and ultimately, its color!

The Sun's hot gas contains many kinds of atoms, each with many different
orbitals, and the atoms are jostling constantly. Thus, the light
coming from the sun is made up of electromagnetic waves of many
different frequencies.

We can see some of these frequencies as different colors, but some are
invisible to humans, such as ultraviolet and infrared.

\section{Light Hits the Cow}


When these photons from the sun hit the cow, the hide and hairs of the
cow will absorb some of the photons. These photons will become heat
and make the cow feel warm. Some of the photons will not be absorbed
-- they will leave the cow. When you say ``I see the cow,'' what you are
really saying is ``I see some photons that were not absorbed by the cow.''

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{cow4.png}
    \caption{Photons hitting the cow create a heated spot, where some are absorbed.}
    \label{fig:example-camera}
\end{figure}
\index{photon!colors of}
\index{photon!wavelength}
Different materials absorb different amounts of each wavelength. A
plant, for example, absorbs a large percentage of all blue and red
photons that hit it, but it absorbs only a small percentage of the
green photons that hit it. Thus, we say ``That plant is green.''

White things absorb very small percentages of photons of any visible
wavelength. Black things absorb very \emph{large} percentages of
photons of any visible wavelength. That is why, on a hot summer day, a black car with black
seats and interior will heat up on the inside much hotter than a white car.

Before we go on, let us review: The sun creates photons that travel as
electromagnetic waves of assorted wavelengths to the cow. Many of
those photons are absorbed, but some are not. Some of those photons
that are not absorbed go into the lens of our camera.

\section{Pinhole camera}


\index{camera ! pinhole}
The simplest cameras have no lenses. They are just a box. The box has
a tiny hole that allows photons to enter. The side of the box
opposite the hole is flat and covered with film or some other
photo-sensitive material.

The photons entering the box continue in the same direction they were
going when they passed through the hole. Thus, the photons that
entered from high hit the back wall at a low point. The photons that came from
the left hit the back wall on the right. This is how the image is projected
onto the back wall, rotated 180 degrees; what was up is down, what was
on the left is on the right.

You can make one with a shoebox, a pin, and a sheet of wax paper for the
back wall. Point it at a bright window, and you will see a dim, upside-down
picture of the outdoors.

\textbf{Worked example.} A 2 m tall object stands 10 m from a pinhole
camera whose back wall is 0.05 m behind the pinhole. How tall is the
image?

\textbf{Solution.} The rays through the pinhole form two similar
triangles, so the image is $\frac{0.05}{10}$ as tall as the object:
$\frac{0.05}{10}(2) = 0.01$ m, or 1 cm.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{pinholeCamera.png}
    \caption{A pinhole camera flips the image it ``sees'' by 180$^\circ$.}
    \label{fig:example-camera-2}
\end{figure}


\begin{Exercise}[title={Height of the image}, label=image_height]
    Let us say that the pinhole is exactly the same height as the
    shoulder of the cow, and that the shoulder is directly above one hoof.
    This means the pinhole, the shoulder, and the hoof form a right triangle.

    Now, let us say that the camera is being held perpendicular to the
    ground. The pinhole, the image of the shoulder, and the image of
    the hoof on the back wall of the camera now also form a right triangle.

    These two triangles are similar.

    The shoulder is 2 meters from the hoof. The cow is standing 3 meters
    from the camera. The distance from the pinhole to the back wall of
    the camera is 3 cm. How tall is the image of the cow on the back wall
    of the camera?

\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=image_height]

    The two triangles are similar, so $\frac{x}{3 \text{ cm}} = \frac{2 \text{ m}}{3 \text{ m}}$,
    and $x = 2$ cm. The image of the cow is 2 cm tall (and upside down).

\end{Answer}

\section{Lenses}

Now, a quick review: A photon leaves the sun in some random direction. It
travels 150 million km from the sun and hits a cow. It is not
absorbed by the cow, and heads off in a new direction. It passes
through the pinhole and hits the back wall of the camera. That seems
incredibly improbable, right?

It is improbable. A pinhole lets in very little light, so pinhole images
are dim, especially at dusk. You could make the hole bigger, but then light
from each point of the cow lands on a whole blob of the back wall, and the
picture blurs. The solution is a \newterm{lens}: a big opening that still
makes a sharp image.
\index{camera ! lens}
Focus a lens on a wall with a dot drawn on it. All the photons from that
dot that hit the lens, no matter which part of the lens they pass
through, get bent to the same spot on the back wall of the camera. A lens
gathers light like a big hole but makes a sharp picture like a pinhole.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{pinholePoints.png}
    \caption{A lens bends all the light from one point of the object to one point of the image.}
    \label{fig:pinholePoints}
\end{figure}



Note that the image still gets flipped. Light rays that enter the lens
parallel to its axis all pass through a single point, the \newterm{focal
point}.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{lensPoints.png}
    \caption{A lens still flips the image, but introduces a focal point.}
    \label{fig:lensPoints}
\end{figure}

The distance from the lens to its focal point is called the lens's
\newterm{focal length}.\index{camera ! lens ! focal length} \index{telephoto lens}Telephoto lenses, that let you take big
pictures of things that are far away, have long focal lengths.
\index{wide-angle lens}Wide-angle lenses have short focal lengths.

\section{Sensors}

The camera on your phone has a \index{image sensor}sensor on the back wall of the
camera. \index{pixel}The sensor is broken up into tiny rectangular regions called
pixels. When you say a sensor is 6000 by 4000 pixels (most common ratio for photography), we are saying
the sensor is a grid of 24,000,000 pixels: 6000 pixels wide and
4000 pixels tall.


Each pixel is a tiny patch of silicon, a cousin of the solar cell from the
diodes chapter. When a photon with enough energy (recall $E = hf$) strikes
it, the photon frees an electron, and the pixel collects it. The minimum
energy for silicon, called its \newterm{band gap}, is about 1.1 eV. A
photon with less energy passes right through without freeing anything.
During the exposure, each pixel counts up its electrons: more photons
mean more electrons, which mean a brighter pixel. Then the camera measures
the charge in each pixel as a voltage and turns it into a number.

But a pixel cannot tell what color a photon was. So a mosaic of tiny color
filters sits on top of the sensor, one filter per pixel, in a checkerboard
called a \newterm{Bayer pattern}. Half the pixels are under green filters,
a quarter under red, and a quarter under blue. (There are twice as many
green ones because our eyes are most sensitive to green.) The camera then
estimates all three colors at every pixel by looking at its neighbors.
\index{rgb}
So if your camera has a \index{resolution}\newterm{resolution} of
$6000 \times 4000$, the finished image has 24,000,000 pixels, each with
three numbers: the intensity of red, green, and blue light, for a total of
72,000,000 numbers. We call these numbers ``RGB'' for Red, Green, and
Blue. The RGB values range from $0-255$ for each channel. We will talk more about this when hexadecimal is introduced.

\begin{Exercise}[title={Camera Pixels}, label={ex:photon_camera}]
A digital camera sensor is made of silicon, with a band gap of $1.1$ electronvolts. If the sensor is exposed to monochromatic light with a wavelength of $\lambda = 1200\text{ nm}$, what does the resulting image look like? Why?

Hint: $E = \frac{1240}{\lambda}$ where $\lambda$ is in nanometers and $E$ is in electronvolts ($\textrm{eV}$).
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref={ex:photon_camera}]

The pixels will remain dark, or black, because the energy of each incoming photon is less than the band gap of silicon. Calculating $E = \frac{1240}{1200} = 1.033 \, \textrm{eV}$, which is less than the work function of $1.1 \, \textrm{eV}$.

\end{Answer}
\section{Aspect Ratio}
\index{aspect ratio}
When you are buying a monitor, TV, or even smartphone, you may hear the term \textbf{aspect ratio}. The aspect ratio is a set of two numbers representing the ratio between the width and the height. Conventionally, the width comes first, separated by a colon, and then the height:

$$\mathrm{width} : \mathrm{height}$$

Some of the most common aspect ratios are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $16:9$ (known as Widescreen)
    \item $17:9$
    \item $9:16$
    \item $4:3$ (known as Fullscreen)
    \item $4:5$
    \item $3:2$
    \item $1:1$ (perfect square, like on a smartwatch)
\end{itemize}

As we talked about above, the aspect ratio does not describe the actual size of the screen or image. Instead, it describes the shape. A movie theatre screen can have the same aspect ratio as your monitor, even if the screen is 360 inches and the monitor is 24 inches.

Aspect ratio describes the proportional relationship between width and height.
Resolution describes the total number of pixels used to represent an image.

For example, both of the following resolutions have a $16:9$ aspect ratio:

\[
    1280 \times 720
\]
\[
    1920 \times 1080
\]


Mathematically, the aspect ratio is a dimensionless quantity. Since both width and height are measured in the same units (such as centimeters, inches, or pixels), the units cancel when forming the ratio. This means aspect ratio describes proportion rather than physical measurement. Most often, we determine the aspect ratio of a digital display by comparing the number of horizontal pixels to vertical pixels.

For example:
\[
    1920 \times 1080 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \frac{1920}{1080} = \frac{16}{9} = 16:9
\]

Digital cameras and smartphone cameras capture images using a rectangular sensor. The shape of this sensor determines the default aspect ratio of the image. For example:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Many digital cameras use a $3:2$ sensor.
    \item Micro Four Thirds cameras use $4:3$.
    \item Most video recording uses $16:9$.
\end{itemize}

Important: when you change the aspect ratio setting on a camera, the device does not change the sensor itself. Instead, it crops part of the image to match the selected ratio. Cropping reduces the number of recorded pixels, which may reduce file size. However, actual file size also depends on compression methods used by the device.

Human vision covers a wider angle side to side than up and down, so wide
shapes like $16:9$ feel natural and immersive. That is why TVs and movie
screens are wide. But phones are usually held upright, so short videos made
for phones use the tall $9:16$ shape, which is just $16:9$ turned on its
side.

\subsection{Printing}\index{printing photos}
You can imagine this may cause a problem for printing out photos. Most printing paper is $8.5 \, \mathrm{ inches} \times 11 \, \mathrm{ inches}$ or $8 \, \mathrm{inches} \times 10 \, \mathrm{inches}$. This causes an issue, as that fits a $8.5 : 11 \iff 17 : 22$  or $4:5$ aspect ratio, respectively.
We combat this by using very standardized printing sizes (in inches):
\begin{itemize}
    \item $6 \times 4 \rightarrow 3:2$,
    \item $7 \times 5 \rightarrow 7:5$,
    \item $10\times8 \rightarrow 5:4$,
    \item and, rarely, $8.5 \times 11 \rightarrow 1.29:1$
\end{itemize}
Going to any framing or printing store will give you a very standardized set of sizes, or some multiple of these.

Most smartphones take photos at $4:3$ or $3:2$. A $3:2$ photo fits a
$6 \times 4$ inch print exactly; a $4:3$ photo fits an $8 \times 6$ inch
print.

\begin{Exercise}[title={Will it fit?}, label=cam_print]
A phone photo is $4032 \times 3024$ pixels. What is its aspect ratio? If
you print it as a $6 \times 4$ inch photo, how much of the image must be
cropped?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=cam_print]
$\frac{4032}{3024} = \frac{4}{3}$, so it is $4:3$. To fit $3:2$, keep the full
width of 4032 pixels and cut the height to $\frac{2}{3}(4032) = 2688$
pixels. That crops $3024 - 2688 = 336$ pixel rows, about 11\% of the
image.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Taller Person}, label=cam3]
A $1.8$ m person stands $20$ m from the same camera. How tall is the image on the sensor?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=cam3]
$\frac{0.05}{20}(1.8) = 0.0045$ m, or $4.5$ mm.
\end{Answer}

\chapter{Neural Networks for Classification}

\section{The Output Layer}\index{neural network!classification}
Classification uses the same network as regression, but the output layer and the loss change. The output is a probability, not a number.
For two classes, a single output neuron uses the sigmoid activation, $\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$, which lies between 0 and 1.
For more than two classes, the output layer has one neuron per class, and the \newterm{softmax} activation turns the outputs into probabilities
that sum to 1.

\section{Cross-Entropy Loss}\index{cross-entropy}
The usual loss for classification is cross-entropy. For a binary label $y \in \{0, 1\}$ and predicted probability $p$,
$$L = -\left[y \ln p + (1 - y)\ln(1 - p)\right]$$
If the true label is 1, the loss is $-\ln p$. It is small when the network assigns high probability to the correct class, and large when $p$ is small. Think of a weather forecast that gives a 10 percent chance of rain on a day that turns out rainy. The forecast was confidently wrong, and cross-entropy punishes it heavily.

\section{A Worked Example}
A single neuron computes $z = wx + b$ with $w = 2$, $b = -1$, and input $x = 1$. Then $z = 1$, and the predicted probability is
$$p = \sigma(1) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-1}} \approx 0.731$$
If the true label is $y = 1$, the cross-entropy loss is $-\ln(0.731) \approx 0.313$.

\subsection{Softmax with Three Classes}
A network that sorts photos into cat, dog, or bird produces three raw scores, say
$z = (2, 1, 0)$. Softmax exponentiates each score and divides by the total:
$$e^2 \approx 7.389, \quad e^1 \approx 2.718, \quad e^0 = 1, \qquad \text{total} \approx 11.107$$
$$P(\text{cat}) \approx \frac{7.389}{11.107} \approx 0.665, \quad P(\text{dog}) \approx 0.245, \quad P(\text{bird}) \approx 0.090$$
The probabilities sum to 1, and the largest score gets the largest probability. If
the photo really is a cat, the cross-entropy loss is $-\ln(0.665) \approx 0.408$.

\section{Training and Prediction}
Training repeats the same steps as in regression: feedforward, loss, backpropagation, and a weight update. Once trained, a new instance is classified by
computing $p$ and predicting the class with the larger probability. A threshold of 0.5 is common for binary outputs.

\begin{Exercise}[label=nnc1, title=Compute a probability]
A neuron has $z = 0$. What is the predicted probability of class 1?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=nnc1]
$\sigma(0) = \frac{1}{1+1} = 0.5$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=nnc2, title=Loss of a wrong prediction]
A network predicts $p = 0.1$ for an example whose true label is 1. What is the cross-entropy loss?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=nnc2]
$-\ln(0.1) \approx 2.303$. The loss is large because the network gave the correct class a low probability.
\end{Answer}

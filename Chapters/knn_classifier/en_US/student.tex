\chapter{The k-Nearest Neighbor Classifier}

\section{The Idea}\index{k-nearest neighbor}
The \newterm{k-nearest neighbor} (k-NN) classifier predicts the class of a new point
from the points closest to it. It needs no model to be fit. Think of asking the three friends who sit closest to you what movie to watch: you go with what most of them say. It stores the training data,
and does all its work when a prediction is requested. This is called \emph{instance-based} learning.

\section{The Algorithm}
To classify a new point $\mathbf{x}$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Compute the distance from $\mathbf{x}$ to every training point.
\item Find the $k$ training points with the smallest distances.
\item Predict the most common class among those $k$ points.
\end{enumerate}
For regression, step 3 is replaced by the average of the $k$ values.

\section{Distance}\index{Euclidean distance}
The usual distance is the Euclidean distance. For two points with $n$ features,
$$d(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - y_i)^2}$$

\section{A Worked Example}
Six labeled points are given in the plane:
$$A: (1,1), \quad A: (2,1), \quad A: (4,1), \quad B: (2,3), \quad B: (5,4), \quad B: (6,5)$$
Classify the new point $\mathbf{x} = (1, 2.5)$. The distances to the nearest points are:
\begin{itemize}
\item $(2,3)$, labeled B: $\sqrt{1^2 + 0.5^2} \approx 1.118$
\item $(1,1)$, labeled A: $\sqrt{0^2 + 1.5^2} = 1.5$
\item $(2,1)$, labeled A: $\sqrt{1^2 + 1.5^2} \approx 1.803$
\end{itemize}
With $k = 1$, the nearest point is labeled B, so the prediction is B. With $k = 3$, the three
nearest points are B, A, and A, so the prediction is A. The choice of $k$ changed the answer.

\section{Choosing $k$}
A small $k$, such as 1, follows the training data closely. It can capture noise and overfit.
A large $k$ averages over many points and can blur the boundary between classes, which is
underfitting. The value of $k$ is chosen on a validation set, as described in the chapter on
training, validation, and testing. An odd $k$ avoids ties in two-class problems.

\section{Feature Scaling}\index{standardization!k-NN}
Distance depends on the units of each feature. A feature measured in thousands dominates a
feature measured in fractions. Standardize the features first, so that each has mean 0 and
standard deviation 1, and compute the standardization from the training set alone.

\section{Cost}
k-NN is simple to understand, but prediction is slow for large data sets. Every prediction
computes the distance to every training point.

\begin{Exercise}[label=knn1, title=One neighbor]
Using the six points in the worked example, classify $\mathbf{x} = (3, 1.5)$ with $k = 1$.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=knn1]
The points $(2,1)$ and $(4,1)$ are tied for nearest, both at distance $\sqrt{1^2 + 0.5^2} \approx 1.118$.
Both are labeled A, so the prediction is A either way.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=knn2, title=Three neighbors]
Using the same point, $(3, 1.5)$, and $k = 3$, what is the prediction?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=knn2]
The two A points at distance $1.118$ come first. The third nearest is $(2,3)$, labeled B, at
$\sqrt{1^2 + 1.5^2} \approx 1.803$. The vote is A, A, B, so the prediction is A.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=knn3, title=Why scale?]
A data set has height in centimeters and income in dollars. Explain why the distances are dominated by income.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=knn3]
Income values are in the tens of thousands, while heights are around 170. Differences in
income are far larger in size, so they control the distance. Standardizing both features gives
each the same influence.
\end{Answer}

\chapter{Mass and Weight}
Mass\index{mass} is how much stuff is in an object. Weight\index{weight} is how hard gravity pulls on it. Your mass is the same everywhere in the universe. Your weight is not: the Moon's gravity is about one-sixth as strong as Earth's, so on the Moon you would weigh one-sixth as much, even though you are exactly as massive.

\section{Kilograms versus Pounds}
You probably learned that 1 kilogram is about 2.2 pounds. That is only true \textit{on Earth}. In everyday talk, people swap kilograms and pounds, or mass and weight, as if they were the same thing. Scientists do not.

\begin{tabular}{|c|c|}\hline
  \textbf{Mass}                              & \textbf{Weight}                      \\\hline
  measure of \textit{amount}                 & measure of \textit{force}            \\\hline
  units include grams, kilograms, milligrams & units include pounds, newtons, dynes \\\hline
  a \textit{scalar} measurement              & a \textit{vector} measurement        \\\hline
  does \textit{not} depend on location       & does depend on location              \\\hline
\end{tabular}

On Earth, a 1 kg mass weighs 2.2 pounds. On the Moon, that same mass weighs about 0.36 pounds. The ``conversion'' you learned works everywhere on Earth, because gravity is nearly the same everywhere on Earth. Leave the planet, and it falls apart.

\subsection{Scalars and Vectors}
Mass and weight are different kinds of measurement. A \textit{scalar}\index{scalar} is just a size: mass, energy, temperature, and density are all scalars. A \textit{vector}\index{vector} has a size \emph{and} a direction. Force is a vector, so your weight is not just 185 pounds; it is 185 pounds \textit{downward}. Velocity is a vector too: 35 mph east is a different trip than 35 mph west. You will see much more of vectors in the vectors chapter.

\section{Weight Near Earth's Surface}

Gravity pulls on each object in proportion to its mass. That is why we can usually get away with treating mass and weight as the same thing, as long as we stay on Earth.

The weight\index{weight!of an object} of an object is the force of gravity on it. When we say, ``This potato weighs 1 pound,'' we really mean ``on Earth.'' The same potato weighs about one-sixth of a pound on the Moon (see figure \ref{fig:massvweight}).

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{massvweight.png}
  \caption{Mass is a measure of all the matter in an object. Weight is a measure of
    the force of gravity on that object. Mass is not location-dependent, while weight
    is.}
  \label{fig:massvweight}
\end{figure}

Its mass, though, is 0.45 kg anywhere in the universe.

\textbf{Worked example: an astronaut on the Moon.} An astronaut has a mass of 60 kg. On Earth, her weight is $W = mg = (60)(9.8) = 588$ N. On the Moon, where $g = 1.62$ m/s$^2$, her weight is $W = (60)(1.62) = 97.2$ N. Her mass is 60 kg in both places.

Where does $g = 9.8$ m/s$^2$ come from? Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation says that two masses $M$ and $m$, a distance $r$ apart, pull on each other with a force
\begin{equation}
  F = G \frac{Mm}{r^2}
\end{equation}
where $G$ is the gravitational constant. (The next section looks at this law more closely.)

For an object near Earth's surface, $M$ is Earth's mass and $r$ is basically Earth's radius $R_E$, no matter whether the object is on the floor or on a mountaintop:
\begin{equation}
  F = G \frac{Mm}{R_E^2}
\end{equation}

The only thing that changes from object to object is $m$, so pull it out front:
\begin{equation}
  F = m \left( G \frac{M}{R_E^2} \right)
\end{equation}

Everything in the parentheses is a constant: $G$, Earth's mass $M$, and Earth's radius $R_E$. We call it the gravitational field strength\index{gravitational field strength}:
\begin{equation}
  g = G \frac{M}{R_E^2}
\end{equation}

Substituting gives the famous short version:
\begin{equation}
  F=mg \label{eq:gravity}
\end{equation}
where $m$ is the object's mass and $g=9.8 \text{ m/s}^2$. Do not mix up little $g$ with big $G$: $g$ is built from $G$, but they are different numbers.

Here are the steps in one place:
\begin{align*}
  F & = G \frac{Mm}{r^2}                   \\
    & = G \frac{Mm}{R_E^2}                 \\
    & = m \left( G \frac{M}{R_E^2} \right) \\
  g & = G \frac{M}{R_E^2}                  \\
  F & = mg
\end{align*}

Equation~\ref{eq:gravity} gives the force of gravity, $F_g$, on nearly everything you will meet on Earth: a thrown ball, a book on a table, a lamp hanging from a cord.\footnote{Some problems let you use 10 for $g$ to keep the arithmetic simple. Use 9.8 unless the problem says otherwise.}

\begin{Exercise}[title=May the Force be with You 1, label=grav1]
  A hammer is falling along the side of a building, close to the surface of the Earth. What is its acceleration?

  We will not ask it yet, but think about this: why does the Earth not move as much toward the hammer as the hammer moves toward the Earth?
\end{Exercise}

\begin{Answer}[ref=grav1]
  The gravitational acceleration is $9.8 \text{ m/s}^2$, no matter the mass of the object.
\end{Answer}
\begin{Exercise}[title=May the Force be with You 2, label=grav2]
  A dropped coin feels a gravitational force of $0.0198 \text{ N}$. What is the mass of the coin?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=grav2]
  The mass of the coin is $\frac{0.0198 \text{ N}}{9.8 \text{ m/s}^2} \approx 0.00202$ kg, or about $2.0$ grams.
\end{Answer}
\section{Universal Gravitation}
When we discuss weight, we usually mean the gravitational force between an object and the largest nearby object. My weight on Earth, a spacecraft's weight on the Moon, or a rover's weight on Mars are all examples. But planets and moons do not have some special property that makes them emit gravity. Rather, \textit{all objects with mass are gravitationally attracted to each other}. But, compared to the mass of the Earth, the mass of all the other objects around you (a table, your family, your house or apartment complex) is very, very small.

The
force of the gravitational attraction between two objects is proportional to the product
of their masses, and inversely proportional to their distance squared.
This means that as objects get farther away, the force decreases. If you double the distance, the force quarters. Quadruple the distance, the force is $1/16^{th}$ as much.
This is why you are more attracted to the Earth than you are to
distant stars, even though they have much more mass than the Earth.

\index{Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation}
\begin{mdframed}[style=important, frametitle={Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation}]

  Two masses ($m_1$ and $m_2$) that are a distance of
  $r$ from each other are attracted toward each other with a force of
  magnitude:

  \begin{equation}
    F = G\frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2} \label{eq:univGrav}
  \end{equation}

  where $G$ is the universal gravitational constant\index{gravitational constant}. If you measure the
  mass in kilograms and the distance in meters, $G$ is about $6.674
    \times 10^{-11}$. That will get you the force of the attraction in
  newtons.

\end{mdframed}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Gravitation between two planets}, label=moon_and_planet]
  A small moon orbits a planet at a distance of $4.0 \times 10^6 \text{ m}$ from the planet's center. The mass of the planet is $6.0 \times 10^{24} \text{ kg}$, and the mass of the moon is $8.0 \times 10^{22} \text{ kg}$.

  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Using Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation, calculate the gravitational force between the planet and the moon.
    \item If the moon were moved to a distance of $8.0 \times 10^6 \text{ m}$ from the planet's center (twice the original distance), determine the new gravitational force.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{Exercise}

\begin{Answer}[ref=moon_and_planet]
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item
          \begin{align*}
            F & = G\frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}                                                                         \\
              & = (6.674 \times 10^{-11})\frac{(6.0 \times 10^{24}) (8.0 \times 10^{22})}{(4.0 \times 10^6)^2} \\
              & = 2.0022 \times 10^{24}
          \end{align*}

    \item Now we have a new distance, $r' = 8.0 \times 10^{6} \, \text{m}$, double the original. Notice that gravitational force is inversely proportional to the square of the distance:
          $$F \propto \frac{1}{r^2}$$
          So if distance doubles,
          $$r' = 2r \quad \Rightarrow \qquad F' = \frac{F}{4}$$
          Which results in $5.0055 \times 10^{23} \text{ N}$
  \end{enumerate}
\end{Answer}


\begin{Exercise}[title={Gravity}, label=gravity_earth]

  The Earth's mass is about $6 \times 10^{24}$ kilograms.

  Your spacecraft's mass is 6,800 kilograms.

  Your spacecraft is also about 100,000 km from the center of the Earth. (For
  reference, the moon is about 400,000 km from the center of the Earth.)

  What is the magnitude of the force of gravity that is pulling your spacecraft and the Earth
  toward each other?

\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=gravity_earth]

  First convert the distance to meters: $100{,}000$ km $= 1.0 \times 10^8$ m. Then
  $$F = G\frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2} = (6.674 \times 10^{-11})\frac{(6.8 \times 10^3)(6
      \times 10^{24})}{(10^8)^2} \approx 270 \text{ N}$$

  About 270 newtons, roughly the weight of a 28-kg backpack on Earth.

\end{Answer}


\subsection{Gravitational Field}
\index{gravitational field}
In Newton's law of gravity (Equation~\ref{eq:univGrav}), the force grows with each mass. Double either mass, and the force doubles. That is why gravity is obvious for planets and moons, which keep each other in orbit, and invisible between you and your desk.

Now remember the hammer from Exercise~\ref{grav1}. The Earth pulls the hammer, and the hammer pulls the Earth just as hard. So why does only the hammer seem to move? Because acceleration is force divided by mass (Newton's second law, which you will meet in the Forces and Motion chapter). The hammer's mass is about $1 \, \mathrm{kg}$, so $a_\text{hammer} = \frac{F}{m_\text{hammer}}$ is large. The Earth's mass is about $6 \times 10^{24}$ kg, so $a_\text{Earth} = \frac{F}{m_\text{Earth}}$ is far too small to notice. Both actually fall toward their shared \newterm{center of mass}, which sits almost exactly at the center of the Earth (see the next section).

Figure~\ref{fig:grav_field} shows the direction of the pull around a planet:

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1]

  \draw[thick] (0,0) circle (2);
  \node at (0,0) {$m$};

  \foreach \a in {0,22.5,...,337.5} {
      \draw[->] ({3*cos(\a)},{3*sin(\a)}) -- ({2.2*cos(\a)},{2.2*sin(\a)});
    }
  \foreach \a in {0,22.5,...,337.5} {
      \draw[->] ({4*cos(\a)},{4*sin(\a)}) -- ({3.2*cos(\a)},{3.2*sin(\a)});
    }
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \caption{A figure of the direction of force vectors for a gravitational field.}
  \label{fig:grav_field}
\end{figure}

Every arrow points toward the planet's center of mass, and the arrows are longer closer in, where the pull is stronger. Attaching an arrow to every point in space like this makes a \newterm{vector field}\index{vector field}, an idea you will see again in vector calculus. The length of each arrow depends on the distance $r$ from the center.

  The gravitational field at a point in space is defined as the force per unit mass experienced by a small test mass placed at that point:
  $$\vec g = \frac{\vec F}{m}$$

Notice that $\vec g$ has units of acceleration. That is no accident: the gravitational field at a point is exactly the acceleration a small object would have there if you let it go. The field gets weaker as you move away from the planet, and for a round planet it is the same at every point the same distance from the center.

\section{Center of Mass}\label{sec:CoM}
\index{center of mass}
The \emph{center of mass} of an object is its balance point: the spot where it acts as if all its mass were squeezed into one point. Balance a ruler on your finger, and your finger is under its center of mass. When we draw free body diagrams in a later chapter, we will treat each object as a single point at its center of mass.

If an object is heavier in some places than others, the center of mass shifts toward the heavy part. A hammer balances near its metal head, not halfway down the wooden handle. You will see the term \emph{center of mass} all through this sequence, though we will rarely ask you to calculate it in full.

For objects along a line, the center of mass is a weighted average of their positions:

\begin{mdframed}[style=important, frametitle={1-Dimensional Center of Mass}]
  $$x_{CM} = \frac{m_1x_1 + m_2x_2 + \cdots + m_nx_n}{m_1 + m_2 + \cdots + m_n}$$

  where $x_1, \dots, x_n$ and $x_{CM}$ are measured from an origin you choose. It works just like an average, except heavier masses count more.

  In summation notation:
  $$x_{CM} = \frac{\Sigma m_i x_i}{\Sigma m_i}$$
\end{mdframed}

\begin{Answer}[ref=center_of_mass1]
  The seesaw balances when its center of mass is right over the pivot. Put the origin at the pivot, take right as positive, and call the unknown position $d$:
  \begin{align*}
    x_{CM} & = \frac{\Sigma m_i x_i}{\Sigma m_i}                                                                    \\
    0      & = \frac{\left(30\right)\left(40\right)\ +\ \left(50\right)\left(-20\right)+\left(15\right)d}{30+50+15} \\
           & = \frac{1200\ -1000 +\left(15\right)d}{85}                                                             \\
           & = \frac{200 +\left(15\right)d}{85}                                                                     \\
    d      & = -13\tfrac{1}{3} \text{ cm}
  \end{align*}
  The answer is negative, so $M_3$ goes $13\tfrac{1}{3}$ cm to the \emph{left} of the pivot.
\end{Answer}

For a flat or solid object whose mass is spread out unevenly, you add up tiny pieces with integrals instead of a sum. You have not learned integrals yet, so just take a peek at where this is headed:
\begin{mdframed}[style=important, frametitle={3D Center of Mass}]
  For a solid that fills a region $V$, with density $\rho(x,y,z)$ at each point, each tiny piece of volume $dV$ has mass $dm = \rho(x,y,z)\,dV$. The total mass is
  $$M=\iiint_{V} \rho(x,y,z)\,dV$$
  and the center of mass is at
  \begin{align*}
    x_{CM} & =\frac{1}{M}\iiint_V x\,\rho(x,y,z)\,dV \\
    y_{CM} & =\frac{1}{M}\iiint_V y\,\rho(x,y,z)\,dV \\
    z_{CM} & =\frac{1}{M}\iiint_V z\,\rho(x,y,z)\,dV
  \end{align*}
  It is the same weighted average as before, with the sum replaced by an integral.
\end{mdframed}

\section{Exercises}

\begin{Exercise}[label=mw1, title={Weight on Earth and the Moon}]
A person has a mass of 70 kg. Find the weight in newtons on Earth ($g = 9.8$ m/s$^2$) and on the Moon ($g = 1.62$ m/s$^2$).
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=mw1]
On Earth, $W = mg = 70(9.8) = 686$ N. On the Moon, $W = 70(1.62) = 113.4$ N. The mass is the same in both places; only the weight changes.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=mw2, title={Center of mass}]
A 2 kg mass sits at $x = 0$ and a 6 kg mass sits at $x = 4$ m. Find the center of mass.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=mw2]
$\bar{x} = \frac{2(0) + 6(4)}{2 + 6} = 3$ m.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=mw3, title={Surface gravity}]
Using $G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}$ N m$^2$/kg$^2$, $M = 5.972 \times 10^{24}$ kg, and $R = 6.371 \times 10^6$ m, find the acceleration due to gravity at Earth's surface.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=mw3]
$g = \frac{GM}{R^2} = \frac{(6.674 \times 10^{-11})(5.972 \times 10^{24})}{(6.371 \times 10^6)^2} \approx 9.82$ m/s$^2$.
\end{Answer}

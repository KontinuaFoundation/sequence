\chapter{Flight}

A loaded Boeing 747 weighs about 400,000 kg, as much as 300 cars. Yet it
lifts off the runway and cruises at 900 km per hour. A hummingbird-sized
drone hovers perfectly still. A rocket climbs where there is no air at all
to push against. In this chapter, you will see how each of them does it.
Good news: if you understood how a sail works, you already understand
most of an airplane.

\section{Airplanes}

\subsection{Drag and Thrust}

As an airplane flies, the air pushes back on it, trying to slow it down.
Pilots call this force \newterm{drag}\index{drag}. You can think of drag as
air friction.

Drag grows with the square of the speed. Double your speed, and the drag
becomes four times as large. (You have felt this: stick your hand out of a
car window at 30 km/h, then at 60 km/h.)

To overcome drag, an airplane needs \newterm{thrust}\index{thrust}, a
forward push. Airplanes get thrust by throwing air toward the back of the
plane. By Newton's third law, the air pushes the plane forward.

All that pushing takes a lot of fuel. A Boeing 747 burns about 4 liters of
fuel every second, and a flight from London to New York needs about
70,000 kg of it. At takeoff, the fuel can weigh nearly twice as much as all
the passengers.

\subsection{Lift}

A hot air balloon floats because it is lighter than the air it displaces.
An airplane is far heavier than the air it displaces, so gravity would pull
it from the sky without another upward force. That force is
\newterm{lift}\index{lift}.

Lift works just like sailing on a close reach. The wing meets the air at a
slight tilt, its \index{angle of attack}\newterm{angle of attack}. Air
piles up under the wing, creating high pressure. Air flowing smoothly over
the curved top of the wing leaves low pressure there. The difference in
pressure pushes the wing up. A shape designed to do this is called an
\newterm{airfoil}\index{airfoil}. Wings, propeller blades, kites, and sails
are all airfoils.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{airfoil.png}
    \caption{Air flowing around a wing. The wing deflects air downward,
    leaving high pressure below and low pressure above.}\label{fig:airfoil}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{pitchChange.png}
    \caption{A symmetric airfoil meeting the air head-on (top) creates no
    pressure difference. Tilted to an angle of attack (bottom), it does.}
    \label{fig:example-flight}
\end{figure}

You may have heard that lift comes mostly from the rounded top of the
wing. That explanation oversells the curve. Stunt planes fly upside down
for minutes at a time, which would be impossible if the curved top did all
the work. The angle of attack matters most; the curve helps the air stay
smoothly attached to the wing.

Engineers capture all of this in the \newterm{lift equation}:
$$L = \frac{1}{2}\rho v^2 S C_L$$
where $\rho$ is the density of the air, $v$ is the airspeed, $S$ is the
wing area, and $C_L$ is the \newterm{lift coefficient}, a number that
depends on the wing's shape and angle of attack. Notice the
$\frac{1}{2}\rho v^2$: it is exactly the wind pressure from the sailing
chapter, which the Fluids in Motion chapter called the dynamic pressure. That
chapter also explains how Bernoulli's equation and Newton's third law describe
the same lift.

\textbf{Worked example.} A small plane has a wing area of 20 m$^2$ and
flies at 50 m/s through air with $\rho = 1.2$ kg/m$^3$. Its lift
coefficient is 0.5. How much lift do the wings produce?

\textbf{Solution.}
$L = \frac{1}{2}(1.2)(50)^2(20)(0.5) = 15{,}000$ N. That is enough to hold
up about $\frac{15{,}000}{9.8} \approx 1{,}500$ kg, the mass of a small
plane with its passengers.

\begin{Exercise}[title={Doubling the Speed}, label=fl1]
Using the same wing and angle of attack, what is the lift at $100$ m/s?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=fl1]
Lift depends on $v^2$, so doubling the speed multiplies the lift by four:
$L = 60{,}000$ N.
\end{Answer}

High in the sky, the air is thinner. If two identical planes fly at the same
speed at different altitudes:
\begin{itemize}
\item The higher plane feels less drag.
\item The higher plane's wings make less lift.
\item The higher plane's engines get less oxygen per liter of air, which
      changes how the fuel burns.
\end{itemize}
Airliners cruise at about 11 km because the thin air means less drag and
better fuel economy; they make up for the weaker lift by flying fast.

\begin{Exercise}[title={Thin air}, label=fl_altitude]
At 11 km, the air density is about 0.36 kg/m$^3$. How fast must the plane
from the worked example fly to make the same 15,000 N of lift (with the
same lift coefficient)?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=fl_altitude]
Solve $15{,}000 = \frac{1}{2}(0.36)v^2(20)(0.5)$: $v^2 = \frac{15{,}000}{1.8}
\approx 8{,}333$, so $v \approx 91$ m/s. The air is one third as dense, so
the plane must fly $\sqrt{3} \approx 1.7$ times as fast.
\end{Answer}

\subsection{Control: Roll, Pitch, Yaw}
\index{roll}
\index{pitch}
\index{yaw}
A plane can rotate three ways. Nose up and down is \newterm{pitch}. One wing
up and the other down is \newterm{roll}. Nose left and right is
\newterm{yaw}. The pilot has a control for each.

The first control is the throttle, which sets the thrust.

The pilot also has a stick. Pulling back on the stick tilts the
\newterm{elevator}\index{elevator} on the tail up. Air hitting the elevator
pushes the tail down and the nose up. Pushing the stick forward does the
opposite. The elevator controls pitch.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{elevator.png}
  \caption{Top and side views of the elevator and the pitch it produces.}\label{fig:elevator}
\end{figure}

The stick also moves left and right. Pushing it left raises the
\newterm{aileron}\index{aileron} on the left wing and lowers the aileron on
the right wing. The left wing drops and the right wing rises. The ailerons
control roll.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{ailerons.png}
  \caption{Aileron locations on the plane and their effect on roll.}
  \label{fig:ailerons}
\end{figure}

The pilot works the \newterm{rudder}\index{rudder} on the tail with foot
pedals. Pushing the right pedal swings the rudder to the right. Air pushes
the tail to the left, and the nose swings to the right. The rudder controls
yaw. It works exactly like a boat's rudder.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{rudder.png}
  \caption{The rudder controls yaw, the left-right swing of the nose.}\label{fig:rudderYaw}
\end{figure}

\index{flaps}\newterm{Flaps} are panels on the back edges of the wings. For
takeoff and landing, the pilot extends them down, which gives the wings
more lift (and more drag) at low speeds.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{flaps.png}
  \caption{The location and effect of the flaps.}\label{fig:flaps}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Thrust}

There are three common ways to make thrust.

A \newterm{propeller} is a spinning set of small wings. Each blade is an
airfoil turned on its side, so its ``lift'' points forward. Small planes
spin their propellers with piston engines much like car engines.
\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{propeller.png}
  \caption{Each propeller blade is a small spinning wing whose lift points forward.}\label{fig:propeller}
\end{figure}

\index{jet engine}A \newterm{jet engine} sucks in air, squeezes it with a
spinning compressor, sprays in fuel, and burns the mixture. The hot gas
roars out the back much faster than the air came in, and that pushes the
plane forward. On its way out, the gas spins a turbine, which drives the
compressor at the front.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{jet.png}
  \caption{Cross section of a jet engine.}\label{fig:jet}
\end{figure}

A \newterm{turboprop} uses a jet engine's turbine to spin a propeller.
Turboprops are efficient at the lower speeds and shorter hops flown by
regional airlines.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{turboProp.png}
  \caption{Cross section of a turboprop.}\label{fig:turboProp}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Gliders}
\index{gliders}

A glider has no engine at all. It starts high in the air with lots of
potential energy and trades height for speed, sliding gently downhill
through the air. A good glider can travel 40 meters forward for every meter
it descends; this is its \newterm{glide ratio}. To stay up for hours, glider
pilots hunt for rising air, such as warm air rising from a sunny parking
lot, and circle in it to regain height.

\begin{Exercise}[title={How far can it glide?}, label=fl_glide]
A glider with a glide ratio of 40 is released at an altitude of 1,500 m.
In still air, how far can it glide before it reaches the ground?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=fl_glide]
$40 \times 1{,}500 = 60{,}000$ m, or 60 km.
\end{Answer}

\section{Rotorcraft}

\subsection{Quadcopters}
Now we move from airplanes to \index{quadcopter}\newterm{quadcopters}, the
four-rotor aircraft most people call drones. (You may also hear rotorcraft,
or UAVs, for unmanned aerial vehicles.)
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.75\textwidth]{quad.png}
    \caption{A quadcopter: four rotors on a central body, with one end
    chosen as the front.}
    \label{fig:quad}
\end{figure}
A few terms will come up again and again:
\begin{description}
    \item[Rotor] The \index{rotor}rotor is the spinning part that makes
    lift. It has a central \emph{hub} with two or more \newterm{blades}
    attached. People often say propeller, but rotor is the better word for
    a part that lifts the aircraft.
    \item[UAS / UAV] Unmanned Aircraft System or Unmanned Aerial Vehicle:
    a drone, plus the software and controllers that fly it.
    \item[Fuselage] The main body, or frame.
    \item[Torque] A twisting force. Push on a door near its hinges and it
    barely moves; push near the handle and it swings easily. The second
    push has more torque. You will study torque in detail later. For now,
    know that every spinning rotor twists the drone the opposite way, and
    those twists must be balanced.
\end{description}

\subsection{VTOL}
An airplane needs a runway to build up enough speed for its wings to lift
it. A quadcopter's rotors make lift while the drone sits still. Each rotor
throws air downward, and by Newton's third law the air pushes the rotor
up. So a quadcopter can take off and land straight up and down: this is
called \newterm{Vertical Take-Off and Landing}, or VTOL\index{VTOL}.

Two forces act vertically: the total thrust of the rotors, upward, and the
weight $mg$, downward. Newton's second law says
$$F_{\text{rotors}} - mg = ma$$
To climb, the rotors push harder than the weight. To hover, they push
exactly as hard as the weight:
\begin{equation}
    F_{\text{rotors}} = mg
\end{equation}
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.75\textwidth]{verticalIso.png}
    \caption{A quadcopter climbing straight up. All four rotors push
    equally (red arrows).}
    \label{fig:quadcopterVtol}
\end{figure}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Hover and climb}, label=fl_hover]
A 1.2 kg drone hovers. How much total thrust do its rotors make? How much
thrust does each rotor make? How much total thrust would it need to
accelerate upward at 2 m/s$^2$?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=fl_hover]
Hovering: $F = mg = (1.2)(9.8) \approx 11.8$ N in total, or about 2.9 N per
rotor. Climbing: $F = mg + ma = 1.2(9.8 + 2) \approx 14.2$ N.
\end{Answer}

\subsection{Rotor Structure}
How does a rotor make lift? Each blade is a small wing. As it spins, it
meets the air at an angle of attack and pushes the air down.

Each blade has a \newterm{root}, which connects to the hub, and a
\newterm{tip} at its outer end. Most blades are \newterm{twisted}: steeply
angled near the root and flatter near the tip, like the thread of a screw.
Why? The tip travels around a much bigger circle than the root, so it moves
much faster through the air. A flatter angle at the tip keeps every part of
the blade pushing about equally.

Spin a rotor backward, and it pushes air the other way.

Rotors are described\index{quadcopter!propellers!numbers} by their
\newterm{diameter}, their \newterm{pitch}, and their \newterm{number of
blades}. A ``10x5'' rotor has a 10 inch diameter and a 5 inch pitch.

The \textbf{diameter} is the width of the circle the tips sweep. A bigger
rotor pushes a bigger column of air more slowly. For the same thrust, that
takes less power. This is why a helicopter uses one huge, slow rotor, while
a small drone, which must stay compact, uses several small, fast ones.

The \textbf{pitch} is how far the rotor would screw itself forward in one
full turn, if the air were solid like wood. A higher pitch takes a bigger
bite of air with each turn: more thrust per turn, but the motor must work
harder, so it cannot spin as fast. A lower pitch spins faster but moves
less air per turn.

Most rotors have \textbf{two blades}. They balance easily and are cheap to
make. More blades give more thrust at the same size and speed, but each
blade partly flies through the churned-up air left by the blade ahead of
it, so each extra blade adds less than the one before.

\subsection{Movement, Spin Direction, and Torque}
Two rotors spin clockwise (CW) and two spin counterclockwise (CCW). Rotors
diagonally across from each other spin the same way; neighbors spin
opposite ways. Each spinning rotor twists the body the other way. If all
four spun the same direction, the drone would whirl around like a top.
With two of each, the twists cancel, and the drone can hover perfectly
still.

Many drones offer \newterm{headless mode}\index{headless flight}, where
``forward'' always means ``away from the pilot,'' no matter which way the
drone faces. It is easier for beginners. Normally, though, one end of the
drone is its front, or head, and forward means the direction the head
points. In the rest of this section, we ignore drag and assume the center
of mass is in the middle of the body.

To fly forward, the drone \newterm{pitches} its nose down. The two back
rotors spin faster than the two front rotors, so the back rises. Now the
rotors' thrust tilts forward. Part of it still holds the drone up, and part
of it pushes the drone forward. To fly backward, the front rotors spin
faster instead.

\subsection{Helicopters}

A \newterm{helicopter}\index{helicopter} lifts itself with one big
\newterm{main rotor}. The physics is the same as a quadcopter's: the blades
throw air down, and the air pushes the helicopter up. But one big rotor
creates two new problems.

First, the main rotor's twist has nothing to cancel it. Second, a
helicopter cannot steer by speeding up some rotors and slowing others,
because it has only one. Let us take these one at a time.

\subsubsection{Tail Rotor}

On most American helicopters, the main rotor turns counterclockwise when
seen from above. By Newton's third law, the rotor twists the body the other
way, clockwise. With nothing to stop it, the body would spin faster and
faster until the helicopter crashed.

\subsubsection{Collective and Cyclic}

The main rotor spins at a nearly constant speed. To control it, the pilot
changes the pitch (angle of attack) of the blades as they spin. The clever
device that does this is the \newterm{swash plate}\index{swash plate}: a
pair of plates around the rotor shaft. The bottom plate does not spin. The
top plate spins with the rotor, and links connect it to each blade. Push the
plates up or tilt them, and you change the blades' pitch.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.75\textwidth]{mechanicalControl.png}
    \caption{The swash plate. Actuators move the non-rotating plate; the
    rotating plate above it passes the motion to the blades through the
    pitch change links.}
    \label{fig:mechanicalControl}
\end{figure}

The \newterm{collective}\index{collective pitch} raises or lowers the whole
swash plate. That changes the pitch of all the blades together, so the
whole rotor makes more lift or less. Raise the collective, and the
helicopter climbs.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.75\textwidth]{collective.png}
    \caption{Collective: the swash plate moves straight up or down,
    changing every blade's pitch by the same amount.}
    \label{fig:collective}
\end{figure}

The \newterm{cyclic}\index{cyclic pitch} tilts the swash plate. Now each
blade's pitch changes as it goes around: steep on one side of the circle,
flat on the other. One side of the rotor makes more lift than the other,
so the spinning disk tips, and its thrust tilts, just like a quadcopter
tipping forward. Push the cyclic forward, and the helicopter flies forward.
(One surprise: a spinning rotor acts like a gyroscope, so it tips about a
quarter turn after the point where the extra lift is added. The linkage is
built to account for that.)

\section{Rocketry}
\index{rockets}

Out in space, there is no air to push against. So how does a rocket move?

Imagine floating in space holding a bowling ball. Throw the ball away from
you, and you drift the other way. That is Newton's third law, and momentum
conservation: the ball's momentum one way equals your momentum the other
way.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{thirdLaw.png}
    \caption{Throwing mass backward pushes the spacecraft forward.}
    \label{fig:example_2-flight}
\end{figure}

A rocket does the same thing, but instead of bowling balls, it throws
enormous numbers of hot gas molecules out the back at very high speed.
There is no air in space to supply oxygen for burning, so rockets carry
their own \newterm{oxidizer} along with their fuel.

\subsection{Types of Rocket Fuels}

There are two main kinds of chemical rockets.

A \index{solid fuel rocket}\newterm{solid fuel rocket} burns a solid mix of
fuel and oxidizer, like a giant firework. Once lit, it cannot be turned off
until the fuel is gone.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{solid.png}
    \caption{A solid fuel rocket.}
    \label{fig:solidfuel}
\end{figure}

A \index{liquid fuel rocket}\newterm{liquid fuel rocket} keeps liquid fuel
and liquid oxidizer in separate tanks. Pumps feed them into a combustion
chamber, where they burn and blast out the nozzle. Most liquid fuel
engines can be throttled up and down, and some can be shut off and
restarted.
\index{rockets ! liquid fuel based}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{liquid.png}
    \caption{A liquid fuel rocket: separate tanks of oxidizer and fuel
    feed the combustion chamber.}
    \label{fig:liquidfuel}
\end{figure}

\subsection{The Tyranny of the Rocket Equation}
A rocket must carry all its fuel. But fuel is heavy, so the rocket needs
more fuel to lift that fuel, which is heavy, so it needs more fuel\ldots

The \newterm{rocket equation}\index{rocket equation} tells you how bad this
gets:
$$\Delta v = v_e \ln\left(\frac{m_0}{m_f}\right)$$
Here $\Delta v$ is the change in the rocket's speed, $v_e$ is the speed of
the exhaust, $m_0$ is the starting mass (with fuel), and $m_f$ is the final
mass (fuel used up). The $\ln$ is the natural logarithm. You will study it
in a later volume; for now, use the $\ln$ button on your calculator, or
undo it with $e^x$.

\textbf{Worked example.} Reaching low Earth orbit takes a $\Delta v$ of
about 9.4 km/s. A good kerosene engine has $v_e \approx 3$ km/s. What
fraction of the rocket must be fuel?

\textbf{Solution.} $\frac{m_0}{m_f} = e^{9.4/3} \approx 23$. The rocket
must start 23 times heavier than it ends. So about 96\% of the rocket at
launch is fuel and oxidizer, and only 4\% is everything else: tanks,
engines, and the satellite. That is the tyranny.

One way to fight back is \newterm{staging}\index{rockets ! staging}. A
staged rocket is several rockets stacked together. When the bottom stage
runs out of fuel, it drops away, and the rocket no longer has to haul its
empty tanks and heavy engines.

\subsection{Control in the Atmosphere}
\index{rockets ! in atmosphere}
Sensors on board track which way the rocket points, and a computer adjusts
the controls many times a second to keep it on course. There are three
common controls.

\newterm{Movable fins} work like an airplane's control surfaces.

A \newterm{gimbaled engine} can pivot. Swinging the nozzle changes the
direction of the thrust, which steers the rocket. This is called
\index{thrust vectoring}\newterm{thrust vectoring}. Most modern rockets
steer this way.

\newterm{Vernier engines} are small extra engines that fine-tune the
direction. They add weight, so they are rarely used today.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Movable fins}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Pros:} Simple, reliable, light, and effective at
        high speed in the atmosphere.
        \item \textbf{Cons:} Useless in a vacuum; weak at low speed or in
        thin air.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gimbaled engine (thrust vectoring)}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Pros:} Precise; works in air and in vacuum; responds
        quickly.
        \item \textbf{Cons:} Mechanically complex, heavier, more expensive,
        more things that can fail.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vernier engines}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Pros:} Fine control; a backup if something fails.
        \item \textbf{Cons:} Heavy and less efficient.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

    \centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{control.png}
    \caption{Three ways to steer a rocket in the atmosphere.}
    \label{fig:atmoscontrol}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Control in Space}
\index{rockets ! in space}
Once in space, a spacecraft still needs to turn and adjust its position.

A \newterm{reaction control system} (RCS) is a set of small thrusters, often
mounted in clusters of 3 or 4, that can nudge the spacecraft in any
direction. RCS thrusters are used for docking, turning, and small orbit
changes.

\index{reaction wheels}\newterm{Reaction wheels} are heavy wheels inside the
spacecraft. Spin a wheel one way, and the spacecraft turns the other way.
(Try it on a spinning office chair: hold a bicycle wheel and spin it, and
the chair turns the other way.) With three wheels on three axes, the
spacecraft can point anywhere without using any fuel.

A \index{magnetorquer}\newterm{magnetorquer} is an electromagnet that
pushes against Earth's magnetic field, the way a compass needle turns to
line up with it. Magnetorquers are popular on small satellites in low
orbit. For large spacecraft or deep space, Earth's magnetic field is too
weak to help.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RCS thrusters}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Pros:} Precise; can both move and turn the
        spacecraft; reliable.
        \item \textbf{Cons:} Use up propellant; add weight and complexity.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reaction wheels}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Pros:} No propellant; precise; long-lasting; quiet.
        \item \textbf{Cons:} Limited turning force. A wheel can only spin
        so fast; once it maxes out, thrusters must unload it.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Magnetorquers}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Pros:} No propellant; light; simple.
        \item \textbf{Cons:} Only work near a planet with a magnetic field;
        weak.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Alternative Propulsion}
\index{rockets ! alternate propulsion methods of}

A \index{solar sail}\newterm{solar sail} is a huge, thin, shiny sheet.
Light carries momentum, so each photon that bounces off the sail gives it
a tiny push. The push is feeble, but it never stops, and the spacecraft
needs no fuel.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{solarSail.png}
    \caption{A spacecraft with a solar sail.}
    \label{fig:solarSail}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{solarSailDiagram.png}
    \caption{Sunlight reflecting off a tilted sail pushes it at a right
    angle to its surface.}
    \label{fig:solarSail_2}
\end{figure}

A perfectly reflective sail would be pushed straight out from its surface,
at a right angle to the sail. Real sails absorb a little of the light, and
absorbed photons push along the direction the light was traveling. So the
real thrust is tilted slightly away from the right angle, toward the
direction of the sunlight.

\index{ion propulsion}\newterm{Ion propulsion} uses electricity instead of
burning. An ion thruster strips electrons from atoms of a gas (usually
xenon) and uses electric fields, or electric and magnetic fields together,
to fling the charged atoms out the back at tens of kilometers per second.
That exhaust is ten times faster than a chemical rocket's, so an ion
thruster gets far more push out of each kilogram of propellant. The catch:
the thrust is tiny, about the weight of a sheet of paper. Ion thrusters
must run for months, which is fine for a probe on a long trip through the
solar system.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{ionThruster.png}
    \caption{An ion thruster.}
    \label{fig:ionThruster}
\end{figure}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Faster exhaust}, label=fl_ion]
An ion thruster has an exhaust speed of 30 km/s. Using the rocket equation,
what fraction of a spacecraft's starting mass must be propellant to gain
9.4 km/s? Compare with the chemical rocket in the worked example.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=fl_ion]
$\frac{m_0}{m_f} = e^{9.4/30} \approx 1.37$, so the final mass is
$\frac{1}{1.37} \approx 73\%$ of the starting mass. Only about 27\% of the
spacecraft needs to be propellant, compared with 96\% for the chemical
rocket. (An ion thruster could never lift off from Earth, though; its
thrust is far too weak.)
\end{Answer}

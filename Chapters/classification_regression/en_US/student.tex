\chapter{Classification and Regression}

``How much will this sneaker resell for?'' and ``Is this sneaker fake?'' sound
similar, but they are different kinds of questions. The first wants a number;
the second wants a category. Knowing which kind you are asking decides which
tools you use and how you grade them.

This chapter opens a unit on \newterm{machine learning}\index{machine learning}: writing programs that
learn how to make predictions from examples instead of following rules that a person typed in. Every
chapter in the unit uses the same data, so you can compare the methods side by side.

\section{Meet the Penguins}\index{Palmer penguins}
From 2007 to 2009, Dr.\ Kristen Gorman and the Palmer Station Long Term Ecological Research program
measured penguins on three islands near the Antarctic Peninsula. The data, released for anyone to use
as the Palmer Penguins dataset, describes 344 penguins of three species: Adelie, Chinstrap, and Gentoo.
Eleven are missing a measurement, so we will use the other 333.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l l l}
\textbf{Column} & \textbf{Kind} & \textbf{Example} \\
\hline
\texttt{species} & category & Adelie, Chinstrap, or Gentoo \\
\texttt{island} & category & Biscoe, Dream, or Torgersen \\
\texttt{bill\_length\_mm} & number & 39.1 \\
\texttt{bill\_depth\_mm} & number & 18.7 \\
\texttt{flipper\_length\_mm} & number & 181 \\
\texttt{body\_mass\_g} & number & 3750 \\
\texttt{sex} & category & male or female \\
\texttt{year} & number & 2007 \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Each row is one penguin. The data comes in two files that you will use in every chapter of this unit:
\filename{penguins.csv}, the data itself, and \filename{penguins.py}, which contains one function,
\pyfunction{load\_penguins}. It reads the file, skips the penguins with a missing measurement, and returns a
dictionary of NumPy arrays, one per column. So \verb|data["body_mass_g"]| is an array of 333 masses.

\section{Supervised Learning}\index{supervised learning}
In \newterm{supervised learning}, you are given labeled examples: for each one, some inputs, called
\newterm{features}\index{feature}, and the correct answer, called the \newterm{label}\index{label}. The goal
is a \newterm{model}\index{model}, a function that takes the features of a new example and predicts its
label.

We usually stack the features into a matrix $X$, with one row per example and one column per feature, and
the labels into a vector $\mathbf{y}$:
$$X = \begin{bmatrix} 181 & 39.1 \\ 186 & 39.5 \\ 195 & 40.3 \\ \vdots & \vdots \end{bmatrix}
\qquad
\mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 3750 \\ 3800 \\ 3250 \\ \vdots \end{bmatrix}$$
Here the features are flipper length and bill length, and the label is body mass. With $n$ examples and $p$
features, $X$ is $n \times p$ and $\mathbf{y}$ has $n$ entries.

Everything in this unit is supervised learning except clustering, near the end, where there are no labels
at all and the computer has to find the groups on its own. That is called
\newterm{unsupervised learning}\index{unsupervised learning}.

\section{Regression}\index{regression!supervised}
In \newterm{regression}, the label is a number. Can you predict a penguin's body mass from the length of
its flippers? Figure~\ref{fig:penguin-regression} suggests you can, roughly: longer flippers go with
heavier penguins.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{penguin_regression.png}
\caption{Body mass against flipper length for 333 penguins. A regression model predicts the mass (a number)
from the flipper length.}
\label{fig:penguin-regression}
\end{figure}

Other regression problems: predicting the price of a used car from its age and mileage, tomorrow's high
temperature from today's weather, or how many minutes a student will spend on homework from the number of
assignments.

\section{Classification}\index{classification}
In \newterm{classification}, the label is a category. Can you tell a penguin's species from its bill?
Figure~\ref{fig:penguin-species} shows that the three species form three fairly separate clouds, so a
model has a good chance.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{penguin_species.png}
\caption{Bill depth against bill length, colored by species. A classification model predicts the species
(a category) from the two measurements.}
\label{fig:penguin-species}
\end{figure}

With two categories, the problem is \newterm{binary}\index{binary classification}: spam or not spam, pass or
fail, male or female penguin. With more than two, it is \newterm{multi-class}\index{multi-class
classification}: one of three penguin species, or which of 26 letters a handwritten mark is.

The same features can support either kind of problem. With each student's hours of study, ``What score will
this student get?'' is regression, and ``Will this student pass?'' is classification. What decides it is the
kind of label you predict.

\section{Measuring Errors}
To compare models, we need to grade them.

\textbf{For regression}, look at the \newterm{errors}, also called \newterm{residuals}\index{residual}: the
true value minus the prediction, $y_i - \hat{y}_i$. (The hat means ``predicted.'') Two common summaries:
\begin{align*}
\text{RMSE} &= \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2} & \text{(root mean squared error)}\\
\text{MAE} &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left|y_i - \hat{y}_i\right| & \text{(mean absolute error)}
\end{align*}
Both are in the same units as the label. RMSE punishes big misses more, because it squares them first.
\index{root mean squared error}\index{mean absolute error}

\textbf{Example}: A model predicts masses of 3550, 3900, and 3650 g for three penguins that actually weigh
3750, 3800, and 3250 g. Find the RMSE and MAE.

\textbf{Solution}: The errors are $3750 - 3550 = 200$, $3800 - 3900 = -100$, and $3250 - 3650 = -400$.
$$\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{200^2 + 100^2 + 400^2}{3}} = \sqrt{70{,}000} \approx 265 \text{ g}
\qquad
\text{MAE} = \frac{200 + 100 + 400}{3} \approx 233 \text{ g}$$
The 400 g miss pulls RMSE above MAE.

\textbf{For classification}, the simplest grade is \newterm{accuracy}\index{accuracy}, the fraction of
predictions that are correct. Accuracy hides a lot, though. A medical test that always says ``healthy'' is
99\% accurate if 99\% of people are healthy, and completely useless. The Evaluating Classification Systems
chapter gives better measures.

\section{Baselines: The Dumbest Models}\index{baseline model}
Before building a clever model, always ask: what would a model with no brains at all score? For regression,
the dumbest sensible model ignores the features and always predicts the average label. For classification, it
always predicts the most common class. These are called \newterm{baselines}. A model that cannot beat its
baseline has learned nothing.

Put \filename{penguins.csv} and \filename{penguins.py} in a folder, and create \filename{baselines.py} next to
them:

\begin{minted}{python}
import numpy as np
from penguins import load_penguins

data = load_penguins()
mass = data["body_mass_g"]
species = data["species"]
print("Penguins:", len(mass))

# Regression baseline: always predict the average mass
guess = np.mean(mass)
errors = mass - guess
rmse = np.sqrt(np.mean(errors**2))
print(f"Always guess {guess:.0f} g: RMSE = {rmse:.0f} g")

# Classification baseline: always predict the most common species
names, counts = np.unique(species, return_counts=True)
most_common = names[np.argmax(counts)]
accuracy = np.mean(species == most_common)
print(f"Always guess {most_common}: accuracy = {accuracy:.1%}")
\end{minted}

\begin{verbatim}
Penguins: 333
Always guess 4207 g: RMSE = 804 g
Always guess Adelie: accuracy = 43.8%
\end{verbatim}

\verb|np.unique| with \verb|return_counts=True| returns each distinct value and how often it appears, and
\verb|species == most_common| makes an array of \verb|True| and \verb|False|, whose mean is the fraction that
are \verb|True|.

So any mass model needs an RMSE well under 804 g, and any species model needs to beat 43.8\%. (Spoiler: a
straight line through Figure~\ref{fig:penguin-regression} gets the RMSE down to about 392 g, and several
methods in this unit identify the species correctly more than 95\% of the time.)

\begin{Exercise}[label=cr1, title=Name the task]
For each task, say whether it is classification or regression, and whether it is binary or multi-class:
(a) predicting tomorrow's temperature; (b) sorting a photo into cat, dog, or bird; (c) deciding whether a
loan will default.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=cr1]
(a) Regression, since temperature is a number. (b) Classification, multi-class, with three categories.
(c) Classification, binary, since default is yes or no.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=cr2, title=Choose a measure]
A model predicts house prices. Which error measure is appropriate, and why is accuracy not?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=cr2]
RMSE or MAE. Accuracy requires an exact match, and a predicted price is almost never exactly equal to the
true price, so accuracy is not meaningful for a number.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=cr3, title={Same data, two questions}]
A school has each student's hours of study and final exam score. Describe one regression question and one
classification question you could ask with this data.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=cr3]
Regression: predict a student's exam score (a number) from hours of study. Classification: predict whether
a student passes (yes or no) from hours of study. The inputs are the same; only the kind of label changes.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=cr4, title={RMSE by hand}, space=3cm]
A model predicts 10, 12, and 20 for three examples whose true labels are 11, 12, and 17. Find the RMSE and the
MAE.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=cr4]
The errors are $1$, $0$, and $-3$. $\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{(1 + 0 + 9)/3} = \sqrt{10/3} \approx 1.83$ and
$\text{MAE} = (1 + 0 + 3)/3 \approx 1.33$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=cr5, title={A smarter baseline}]
Instead of predicting the overall average mass for every penguin, predict the average mass of its own
species. Add this to \filename{baselines.py}. What is the RMSE now? Why is this not a fair comparison with
the first baseline?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=cr5]
\begin{minted}{python}
prediction = np.zeros(len(mass))
for name in names:
    same = species == name
    prediction[same] = np.mean(mass[same])
rmse = np.sqrt(np.mean((mass - prediction)**2))
print(f"Species average: RMSE = {rmse:.0f} g")
\end{minted}
The RMSE drops to about 459 g. It is not a fair baseline, because it uses a feature (the species); it is
really a simple model. It shows how much the species alone tells you about mass.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=cr6, title={Baseline for sex}]
What accuracy does the majority-class baseline get for predicting a penguin's sex? What does that tell you
about any sex classifier that scores 52\%?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=cr6]
The data has 168 males and 165 females, so always guessing ``male'' is right $168/333 \approx 50.5\%$ of the
time. A classifier that scores 52\% is barely better than not looking at the penguin at all.
\end{Answer}

\chapter{Poisson and Exponential Distributions}

\section{Counting Events}\index{Poisson distribution}
The \newterm{Poisson distribution} models the number of events that occur in a fixed interval of time or space,
when events happen independently at a constant average rate $\lambda$. Customer arrivals in an hour, calls to a
help desk in a day, and defects on a production line are typical examples. If $X$ counts the events, then
$$P(X = k) = \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!}, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$$
The probabilities sum to 1, because $\sum_k \frac{\lambda^k}{k!} = e^{\lambda}$.

\subsection{A Worked Example}
A store averages $\lambda = 3$ customers per hour. The probability of no customers in an hour is
$$P(X = 0) = e^{-3} \approx 0.0498$$
The probability of exactly two customers is
$$P(X = 2) = \frac{e^{-3} \cdot 3^2}{2!} \approx 0.224$$
The probability of at most two customers is $P(X \le 2) \approx 0.423$, and the probability of at least one customer is
$1 - 0.0498 \approx 0.950$.

\section{Mean and Variance}
A Poisson random variable has the same mean and variance:
$$E[X] = \lambda, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \lambda$$
So the store with $\lambda = 3$ expects 3 customers per hour, with variance 3 and standard deviation about 1.73.

\section{Waiting Times}\index{exponential distribution}
The \newterm{exponential distribution} models the time $T$ until the next event, when events occur at a constant rate $\lambda$.
Its density and tail probability are
$$f(t) = \lambda e^{-\lambda t}, \qquad P(T > t) = e^{-\lambda t}, \qquad t \ge 0$$
Its mean is $E[T] = \frac{1}{\lambda}$, and its variance is $\operatorname{Var}(T) = \frac{1}{\lambda^2}$.

\subsection{A Worked Example}
Calls arrive at a rate of $\lambda = 0.5$ per minute, so the average time between calls is 2 minutes. The probability that the next
call takes more than 3 minutes is
$$P(T > 3) = e^{-0.5 \cdot 3} = e^{-1.5} \approx 0.223$$
The probability that a call arrives within one minute is $P(T \le 1) = 1 - e^{-0.5} \approx 0.393$.

\section{The Connection}
If events form a Poisson process with rate $\lambda$, then the number of events in an interval of length $t$ is Poisson with mean
$\lambda t$, and the waiting time to the first event is exponential with rate $\lambda$. These are two views of the same process.
For example, the probability of no calls in 2 minutes is
$P(X = 0) = e^{-0.5 \cdot 2} = e^{-1}$, which equals $P(T > 2) = e^{-1}$.

\section{The Memoryless Property}\index{memoryless property}
The exponential distribution is the only continuous distribution with the \newterm{memoryless} property:
$$P(T > s + t \mid T > s) = P(T > t)$$
Knowing that 3 minutes have passed without a call says nothing about the remaining wait. For the example above,
$P(T > 5 \mid T > 3) = \frac{e^{-2.5}}{e^{-1.5}} = e^{-1} \approx 0.368$, which equals $P(T > 2)$.

\section{Assumptions}
These models are reasonable only when the events are roughly independent, the rate is roughly constant, and two events
rarely happen at exactly the same moment. A rush hour that doubles the arrival rate, or a machine that wears out, violates the
constant-rate assumption, and the models must then use a rate that changes over time.

\begin{Exercise}[label=pe1, title=Poisson probability]
A call center averages 3 calls per hour. What is the probability of exactly 5 calls in an hour?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=pe1]
$P(X = 5) = \frac{e^{-3} \cdot 3^5}{5!} \approx 0.101$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=pe2, title=Exponential tail]
Using the rate of 0.5 calls per minute from the example, find the probability that the next call takes more than 4 minutes.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=pe2]
$P(T > 4) = e^{-0.5 \cdot 4} = e^{-2} \approx 0.135$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=pe3, title=Mean and variance]
The time between arrivals at a counter is exponential with mean 4 minutes. Find the rate $\lambda$, the variance, and the probability that the next arrival takes more than 4 minutes.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=pe3]
$\lambda = \frac{1}{4} = 0.25$ per minute. The variance is $\frac{1}{\lambda^2} = 16$. The probability is $P(T > 4) = e^{-0.25 \cdot 4} = e^{-1} \approx 0.368$.
\end{Answer}

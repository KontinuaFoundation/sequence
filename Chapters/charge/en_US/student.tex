Rub a balloon on your hair and hold it against a wall. It sticks! Your hair
stands on end, too. Why? This chapter on \textbf{charge} and
\textbf{electrostatics} explains it.

The balloon has stripped some electrons from your hair, so now the balloon
has slightly more electrons than protons. We say it has a \textit{net
electrical charge}\index{electrical charge}: an imbalance between protons
and electrons. While any \textbf{friction} or contact between two objects can cause a difference in electric charge, \textit{net charge cannot be created or destroyed}.\index{net charge}
In this case, the balloon has a negative electrical charge. Objects with
slightly more protons than electrons have a positive charge. Consequently, your
hair now has a positive charge. Note that the differences are equal but opposite.

An object with a negative charge and an object with a positive charge will be \emph{attracted}\index{attraction}
to each other. Two objects with the same charge will be \emph{repelled}\index{repel}
by each other. All charge comes from subatomic particles: protons are positive and
electrons are negative. An object is charged when its numbers of protons
and electrons are unequal.\index{protons}\index{electrons}\index{atoms}

The charge of one proton is called the \textit{elementary
charge}\index{elementary charge|seealso{coulombs}}, written $e$ (not to be
confused with the number $e \approx 2.718$). An electron's charge is $-e$.
Just as you cannot pay with a fraction of a penny, an object's net charge
is always a whole number of elementary charges: $Q = ne$, where $n$ is an
integer. We say charge is \emph{quantized}\index{quantized}.

Robert Millikan measured $e$ in 1909 by watching tiny charged oil drops
float between charged plates. We write the charge of a particle as $q$.
In SI units, charge is measured in \emph{coulombs}\index{coulombs}, and
$e \approx 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ coulombs. So one coulomb is about
$6 \times 10^{18}$ protons' worth of charge.

Two objects with charges are bound to either attract (pull) or repel (push) on
each other. This is a type of force referred to as \emph{electric force}\index{electric
	force}.

\begin{mdframed}
	[style=important, frametitle={Coulomb's Law}]\index{coulombs!Coulomb's law}

	If two objects with charge $q_{1}$ and $q_{2}$ (in coulombs) are $r$ meters
	from each other, the force of attraction or repulsion (electric force) is given
	by

	\begin{equation}
		F = k\frac{ q_{1}q_{2}}{r^{2}}
		\label{eq:coulomb_law}
	\end{equation}

	% removed abs bc of alignment to ap standards (understanding sign of force)

	where $F$ is in newtons and $k$ is Coulomb's constant: about
	$8.988 \times 10^{9}$. You may use $9 \times 10^{9}\, \mathrm{N}\cdot \mathrm{m}
		^{2}/ \mathrm{C}$ in your calculations.

	The constant is also written $k = \dfrac{1}{4\pi \epsilon_0}$, where
	$\epsilon_{0}$ is the \textit{permittivity of free space}.\footnotemark
	The electric force is often written $F_{e}$.
\end{mdframed}
\footnotetext{A note on the permittivity of free space: $\epsilon_0$ (or the electric constant) represents the capability of a vacuum to permit electric field lines. Its exact value in SI units is $\epsilon_0 \approx 8.854 \times 10^{-12} \text{ C}^2/(\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2)$. Remarkably, $\epsilon_0$ together with its magnetic partner sets the speed of light, as you will see in the electromagnetic waves chapter.}
Note how similar this looks to Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation:
\[
	F_{g}= G \frac{m_{1}m_{2}}{r^{2}}
\]

Both laws follow an inverse-square relationship, meaning the force decreases with
the square of the distance. However, gravity is always attractive, while
electric force can be either attractive or repulsive depending on the signs of
the charges ($q_1$ and $q_2$).

If you keep the signs of the charges, a negative $F$ means attraction (the
charges have opposite signs) and a positive $F$ means repulsion (same
signs). If either charge is zero, there is no electric force.

\textbf{Worked example.} Two charges of $1\ \mu$C (a microcoulomb,
$10^{-6}$ C) are $0.1$ m apart. What is the force between them?

\textbf{Solution.} $F = \frac{kq_1q_2}{r^2} = \frac{(8.99 \times 10^9)(10^{-6})(10^{-6})}{(0.1)^2} \approx 0.90$ N.
That is about the weight of a large apple, from two specks of charge. The
electric force is enormously stronger than gravity.

\begin{Exercise}
	[title={Coulomb's Law}, label=charged_balloons]

	Two balloons are charged with an identical quantity and type of charge:
	$-5 \times 10^{-9}$ coulombs. They are held apart at a separation distance of 12
	cm. Determine the magnitude of the electrical force of repulsion between them.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}
	[ref=charged_balloons]
	\[
		F = k\frac{\lvert q_{1}q_{2}\rvert}{r^{2}}= (8.988 \times 10^{9}) \frac{(-5
			\times 10^{-9})(-5 \times 10^{-9})}{0.12^{2}}= \frac{224.7 \times 10^{-9}}{0.0144}
		= 15.6 \times 10^{-6} \,\text{N or} 15.6 \, \text{ micronewtons}
	\]
\end{Answer}


At this point, you might ask ``If the wall has zero charge, why is the balloon attracted
to it?'' The answer: the electrons in the wall move away from the balloon,
polarizing the atoms. The negative charge on the balloon pushes electrons away from
itself, so the surface of the wall gets a mild positive charge. The negative charge
on the balloon is attracted more to the positive charge on the surface of the wall
than the negative charge on the inside, thus the balloon sticks. Even though the wall has no net charge, its charges shift, and that is
enough. The details depend on whether a material is a \emph{conductor} or
an \emph{insulator}.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{balloon2.png}
	\caption{The negative balloon repels the wall's electrons, leaving a net positive surface. The balloon is then attracted to this induced positive charge.}
	\label{fig:example-charge}
\end{figure}
Recall that a \index{conductor}\textbf{conductor} is a type of material through which valence electrons (electrons in the outer shell) easily flows, and an \index{insulator}\textbf{insulator} is a type of material through which charge does not easily flow, if at all. Metals, such as copper or zinc, are the best conductors because the electrons within the atoms are not tied to a nucleus, but rather form a sparse collection or sea in which the charge can flow through. Insulators like plastic, rubber, and glass block the flow of charge
because their electrons are tightly bound to their atoms. (In an insulating
wall, the electrons cannot leave their atoms, but each atom's electron
cloud shifts slightly away from the balloon. The effect is the same.)

\subsection{Point Charge}
The wall and the balloon all contain a bunch of tiny particles which we can refer to as \textbf{point charges}\index{point charge}. A point charge is an electric charge that is treated as a concentrated single point in space, with no physical size or volume. Similar to point masses, we use point charges to simulate effects of charge on other objects or points.

The force between two point charges always points along the straight line
joining them.

\subsection{Superposition} %requires vector addition
What if several charges act on one point charge? Coulomb's law handles
one pair at a time, so we use the \textbf{principle of
superposition}\index{principle of superposition}.

The principle of superposition states that the total electric force on a charge
is the vector sum of the individual forces exerted on it by all other charges.\footnote{This
	is similar how we calculate tensions when there are multiple tensions (or just forces
	in general) on an object. The net force is the sum of all the forces after vector
	addition is applied.}

\section{Electric Field}


\index{electric field}
A gravitational field assigns a force-per-kilogram vector to every point
in space around a mass. An electric field does the same thing for charge.

Similar to how the gravitational field extends out from the center of a given point mass, an \emph{electric field} of a point charge has (imaginary) vectors pointing out of it.\index{electric field lines} However, the direction of the vectors\footnote{You may hear the terms electric field lines and electric field vectors used interchangeably} (and electric force) depends on the charge of the point charge. Around a negative charge, the field lines point \emph{toward} it; around
a positive charge, they point \emph{away} from it (figure
\ref{fig:ef_combined}). In short, they always originate from positive charges and approach negative charges. %https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_field

To measure the field of a charge $Q$ at some point, imagine placing a
tiny positive \emph{test charge} $q_t$ there and measuring the force on it.
The electric field is the force per unit of test charge:
\begin{equation}
	\vec{\mathbf{E}} = \frac{\vec{\mathbf{F}}_e}{q_t}
\end{equation}

What are the \textit{units} of an electric field? Well, by this equation, $\vec{\mathbf{F}}_e$ is a force which we know is in \textit{Newtons} ($N$), and the test particle $q$ is in Coulombs ($C$). The resulting units are N/C, newtons per coulomb\index{electric field!units}.
This turns out to be the same as volts per meter (V/m), which you will see
again when we discuss electric potential. (For example, 1 kV/cm is
$\frac{1000 \text{ V}}{0.01 \text{ m}} = 10^5$ V/m $= 10^5$ N/C.)

It is important to note that we can simplify this equation by substituting Coulomb's law:
\begin{equation}
	\vec{\mathbf{E}} = \frac{\vec{\mathbf{F}}_e}{q_t} = \frac{\frac{kq_1q_t}{r^2}}{q_t} = \frac{kQ}{r^2}
\end{equation}
where $Q$ is the electric field's source charge.

The field gets weaker the farther you are from the charge. In fact, it decreases by a factor of $\frac{1}{r^2}$. Think to yourself where this factor comes from. (Hint: refer back to Coulomb's law (\ref{eq:coulomb_law}))

\begin{mdframed}[frametitle = {Electric Field Force from a singular charge to a second charge}, style = important]

	A charge $q_1$ creates an electric field at a distance $r$ from it with strength:
	\begin{equation}
		E = \frac{kq_1}{r^2}
	\end{equation}

	Introducing a new charge $q_2$, the force on it due to the electric field is:
	\begin{equation}
		F = q_2 \cdot \frac{kq_1}{r^2} \iff F = q_2 E
	\end{equation}
	where $E$ is the electric field strength at the location of $q_2$. This is often referred to as the charge within a known field.
\end{mdframed}

	Two isolated point charges, $X$ of charge $+Q$ and $Y$ of charge $+2Q$, are separated by distance $3d$. Point $P$ is distance $d$ from $X$ and distance $2d$ from $Y$.

	\begin{tikzpicture}

		\node[circle, draw, fill=blue!40] (Y) at (6,0) {Y};
	\end{tikzpicture}


	What is the net electric field strength at $P$?

	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $0$
		\item $\frac{2kQ}{d^2}$
		\item $\frac{kQ}{2d^2}$
		\item $\frac{2kQ}{1.5d^2}$
	\end{enumerate}
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref={ex:ef}]
	We noted above that the electric field caused by a point charge is defined as $E = \frac{kq}{r^2}$. We need to find the electric fields for each charge and then subtract their strengths.

	Inputting known variables as constants for $X$ from point $P$:
	$$E = \frac{kQ}{d^2}$$

	And doing the same for $Y$:
	$$E = \frac{2kQ}{(2d)^2} = \frac{kQ}{2d^2}$$

	Both charges are positive, so each field points away from its charge:

The density of the electric force lines at a given point determines the strength of the force at the point. Locations where field lines are more dense are much stronger than areas where the lines are sparse. This goes back to the idea of the force strength of an electric field.

If you recall the properties of conductors: \textit{there can be no electrostatic field within the body of a conductor}. If an electric field were to exist inside the conductor, it would exert a force $F = qE$ on these free charges, causing them to accelerate. This internal motion continues until the charges redistribute themselves along the conductor's outer surface, creating an induced internal field that perfectly opposes and cancels the original field.

Electric field lines always
\begin{itemize}
	\item Start at positive charges and terminate at negative ones. If it is a single charge, this is indicated by the direction of arrows off to infinity.
	\item The number of lines is proportional to the magnitude of charge.
	\item The line approaches a conductive surface perpendicularly.
	\item No two field lines ever cross in a charge-free region.
\end{itemize}

Keep these in mind for any sketches you may do.

\section{Electric Flux and Gauss's law}
\textbf{Electric flux}\index{electric flux}, written $\Phi$, measures how
much electric field passes through a surface. Picture it as the number of
field lines poking through.

The electric flux depends on 3 factors:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Electric Field Strength ($\mathbf E$)
	\item Surface area ($A$)
	\item The angle $\theta$ between the field and the line perpendicular to the surface
\end{itemize}

If a uniform field passes straight through a flat surface, the flux is
\begin{equation}
	\Phi = EA
	\label{eq:elec_flux_perp}
\end{equation}
Think of a sheet of paper held facing a fan: it catches the most wind.

If the paper is at an angle to the fan, less flow from the fan affects the paper. If you tilt it until it is parallel to the wind, zero wind affects the paper. Tilting the surface reduces the flux in the same way:
\begin{equation}
	\Phi = EA\cos(\theta)
	\label{eq:elec_flux_angle}
\end{equation}
For curved surfaces or changing fields, you chop the surface into tiny
pieces, find $E\,dA\cos\theta$ for each, and add them all up. In calculus,
that sum becomes an integral, written $\Phi = \oint \vec{\mathbf{E}} \cdot d\vec{\mathbf{A}}$
(the dot product supplies the $\cos\theta$).
\subsection{Gauss's law}
Now imagine a closed surface, like a balloon, around some charges. Count
the field lines coming out of it. Every line starts on a positive charge
and ends on a negative one, so the net number of lines poking out depends
only on the charge inside. That is \textit{Gauss's law}:
\begin{mdframed}[style=important, frametitle={Gauss's Law}]\index{Gauss's Law}
The total electric flux out of any closed surface equals the charge
enclosed divided by $\epsilon_0$:
\begin{equation}
		\Phi = \oiint \vec{\textbf{E}} \cdot d\vec{\textbf{A}} = \frac{Q_{enc}}{\epsilon_0}
\end{equation}
\end{mdframed}

For a point charge $q$, choose a sphere of radius $r$ centered on it. By
symmetry, the field has the same strength everywhere on the sphere and
points straight out, so the flux is just $E$ times the sphere's area,
$4\pi r^2$. Gauss's law then gives
\begin{equation}
	E = \frac{q}{4\pi r^2\epsilon_0}
\end{equation}
where $q$ is the source charge, $r$ is the distance from that source charge, and $4\pi r^2$ is the surface area of the electric field. 
That is exactly Coulomb's law again, since $\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} = k$.
Gauss's law explains \emph{why} the field falls off as $\frac{1}{r^2}$: the
same field lines spread out over a sphere whose area grows as $r^2$.
\section{Continuous Charge Distributions}
In the previous sections, we treated charges as discrete ``dots'' in space. However, in applications of charges such as a long charged wire, a flat metal plate, or a solid plastic sphere, charge is spread out over a macroscopic object. When we have a collection of so many point charges that they appear to form a smooth, ``fluid-like'' surface or volume, we call it a \textbf{continuous charge distribution}\index{continuous charge distributions}. This means that the charges, while not truly continuous, can be considered continuous as a long single stream.

To analyze these, we stop summing individual particles ($\sum$) and instead use calculus to integrate ($\int$) over the entire object. While we have not covered calculus yet, keep in mind that this is just a way of calculating the area or volume of a continuous solid.
\subsection{Charge Densities}
The first step in any continuous distribution problem is defining how the charge is spread. \index{charge density}We use three Greek letters to represent the "concentration" of charge based on the object's geometry:

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{llll}
		\hline
        \textbf{Type} & \textbf{Symbol} & \textbf{Definition} & \textbf{SI Units} \\ 
		\hline
        Linear & $\lambda$ (lambda) & Charge per unit length & $\text{C/m}$ \\
        Surface & $\sigma$ (sigma) & Charge per unit area & $\text{C/m}^2$ \\
        Volume & $\rho$ (rho) & Charge per unit volume & $\text{C/m}^3$ \\ 
		\hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{The three types of charge density used in electrostatics.}
    \label{tab:densities}
\end{table}
If the charge is distributed \textbf{uniformly}, these values stay constant. For example, for a rod of length $L$ and total charge $Q$, the linear density is simply $\lambda = Q/L$.
However, if these the charge is not constant per the unit of length, we must use calculus to find the total charge density. To find the total electric field $\vec{\mathbf{E}}$ at a point $P$ near a charged object, we imagine breaking the object into many infinitesimal "pieces" of charge, $dq$. Each chunk acts like a tiny point charge and contributes a tiny bit of electric field, $d\vec{\mathbf{E}}$.

Recall our point charge formula:
\begin{equation}
    d\vec{\mathbf{E}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{dq}{r^2}
\end{equation}

To find the total field, we integrate over the entire distribution:
\begin{equation}
    \vec{\mathbf{E}} = \int d\vec{\mathbf{E}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \int \frac{dq}{r^2}
\end{equation}
The key to solving these integrals is replacing $dq$ with the appropriate density:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Lines: $dq = \lambda \, dl$
    \item Surfaces: $dq = \sigma \, dA$
    \item Volumes: $dq = \rho \, dV$
\end{itemize}

You will learn to evaluate integrals like these in the calculus volumes.
For now, the key idea is that a big charged object acts like a huge number
of tiny point charges, each adding its own small piece of field.

A continuous conveyor belt of width $0.60 \, \mathrm m$ travels at a constant speed of $2.5\, \mathrm{m}\mathrm{s}^{-1}$. The belt has a uniform distribution of charge of $5.0 \mathrm{mC}\mathrm{m}^{-2}$ on the surface. As the belt passes a point $A$, all of its charge is transferred to a

    \end{align*}
\end{Answer}
\section{Electric Potential}
A ball held above the ground has gravitational potential energy; let it
go, and gravity turns that into kinetic energy. Charges work the same way.
A charge in an electric field has \textbf{electric potential
energy}\index{electric potential energy} $U_E$. When the field does work
$W_E$ on the charge, its potential energy drops by the same amount:
\begin{equation}
	\Delta U_E = -W_E \label{eq:change_in_epe}
\end{equation}

Notice how this is similar to an object of mass $m$ undergoing work done by a gravitational field $\Delta U_G = -W_G$.

\begin{Exercise}[title={Change in Electric Potential Energy}, label={eq:electric_potential_energy}]

A positive charge $+q$ moves a distance $r$ from position $X$ to position $Y$ in a \textbf{uniform} electric field $E$.

\end{Exercise}

\begin{Answer}[ref={eq:electric_potential_energy}]

Since the field is uniform, the charge has a constant electric force felt on it: $\vec{\mathbf{F}}_E=q \vec{\mathbf{E}}$. Since $q$ is positive, $\vec{\mathbf{F}}_E$ points in the same direction as $\vec{\mathbf{E}}$. Because the force and the displacement $r$ are in the same direction, the work $W_E$ done by the electric field is positive:

$$W_E = F_E \cdot r = qEr$$

Thus the change in electric potential energy is
$$\Delta U_E = -qEr$$
It is \emph{negative} because when the field does positive work, the
potential energy goes down.
\end{Answer}

\index{electric potential}\textbf{Electric potential} $V$ is the
electric potential energy per unit charge: $V = \frac{U_E}{q}$. It is
measured in volts (1 volt = 1 joule per coulomb). Potential belongs to a
\emph{place}, whether or not a charge is there, the same way height
belongs to a spot on a hill whether or not a ball is sitting there.

Just as moving a mass up in height against gravity increases its gravitational potential, moving a positive charge "up" against an electric field increases its electric potential. In both cases, the field \textit{tries} to push the object toward a state of lower potential energy. A positive charge naturally wants to move with the electric field toward lower potential; in this case, its electric potential energy is converted into kinetic energy. Conversely, to move a positive charge against an electric field, work must be done on the charge, causing its electric potential to increase.

The \index{potential difference}electric potential difference between two points $A$ and $B$ is defined by the line integral of the electric field:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta V = V_B - V_A = -\int_{A}^{B} \vec{\mathbf{E}} \cdot d\vec{\mathbf{l}}
\end{equation}
The negative sign indicates that the potential decreases in the direction of the electric field.

We can easily define two point charges $q_1$ and $q_2$ separated by distance $r$ to have potential energy
\begin{equation}
	U=k\frac{q_1q_2}{r}=\frac{1}{4\pi \epsilon_0}\frac{q_1q_2}{r}
\end{equation}
\begin{Exercise}[title={Accelerating an Electron}, label={ex:electron_accel_epd}]
	\textit{This is a sample problem from an IB Physics Exam}.

An electron is accelerated from rest by an electric potential difference $V$. The electron reaches a speed of $9.4 \times 10^6 \, \textrm{m/s}$. Using the charge of an electron as $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \, \textrm{C}$ and the mass of an electron as $m_e = 9.11 \times 10^{-31} \, \textrm{kg}$, find the potential difference $V$ that accelerated the electron to this speed. You may use a calculator to simplify computations.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref={ex:electron_accel_epd}]

Using our equations, we can derive an equation for the potential difference $V$:
\begin{align*}
	\Delta U_E &= q\Delta V \\
	W_E &= \Delta U_E \\
	W_E &= qV \\
	\text{gain in kinetic energy} &= \text{work done by field} \\
	\frac{1}{2}mv^2 &= qV \\
	V &= \frac{1}{2}\frac{mv^2}{q}
\end{align*}

Since our we are given the speed, and the charge and mass of the electron, we can plug in our values:
\begin{align*}
	V&=\frac{\frac12(9.11\times10^{-31})(9.4\times10^6)^2}{1.60\times10^{-19}}\\
	&=\frac{\frac12(9.11\times10^{-31})(8.836\times10^{13})}{1.60\times10^{-19}}\\
	&=\frac{\frac12 (8.05\times10^{-17})}{1.60\times10^{-19}}\\
	&=\frac{4.03\times10^{-17}}{1.60\times10^{-19}}\\
	&=252 \, \textrm{V}
\end{align*}
A potential difference of about 252 volts does the job. (Since an electron
is negative, it speeds up when it moves toward \emph{higher} potential.)
\end{Answer}
\subsection{Point Charges, Uniform Fields, and Spherical Shells}
In a \textbf{uniform electric field} (such as the space between two large parallel plates), the field strength $E$ is constant. The potential difference between two points separated by a distance $d$ along the field lines is:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta V = -Ed
\end{equation}
For a \textbf{point charge} $Q$, we typically set the reference point ($V=0$) at infinity. The potential at a distance $r$ from the charge is:
\begin{equation}
    V = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r}
	\label{eq:point_charge}
\end{equation}
Unlike the electric field, which is a vector and follows an $1/r^2$ factor, electric potential is a \textbf{scalar} and follows an $1/r$ factor.
\subsection*{Surface Spherical Charge}
For all spherical objects of radius $R$ (surface spheres - assume hollow interior), it has potential inside and outside of the shell. For all points outside the shell, it is the same for any point charge $Q$, as seen in equation~\ref{eq:point_charge}. For all points \emph{inside} the shell, the potential is
$$V = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Q}{R}$$
This means that moving a test charge $q$ from the surface of the shell to the center of the shell, the potential does not change. The potential is constant within the shell.

If the charge is spread evenly through a \emph{solid} sphere instead, the
potential inside at distance $r$ from the center is
$$V = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{Q}{2R} \left(3 - \frac{r^2}{R^2}\right)$$

\subsection{Potential due to Configurations of Charge}
Because potential is a scalar, finding the total potential from multiple charges is often simpler than finding the total electric field. We do not need to worry about components, instead, we simply sum the potentials:
\begin{equation}
    V = \sum_i \frac{k q_i}{r_i} \quad \text{or for continuous distributions} \quad V = k \int \frac{dq}{r}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Conservation of Energy}
When a charge $q$ moves through a potential difference $\Delta V$, the change in its electric potential energy is $\Delta U_E = q\Delta V$. If no other forces are acting on the charge, the total mechanical energy is conserved:
\begin{equation}
    K_a + U_{E,a} = K_b + U_{E,b} \implies \frac{1}{2}mv_a^2 + qV_a = \frac{1}{2}mv_b^2 + qV_b
\end{equation}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Electron between Field Plates}, label={ex:electron_field_plates}]
	\textit{This is a sample problem from an IB Physics Exam}.
	An electron that is travelling at a horizontal speed $v_x = 9.4\times10^6 \textrm{ms}^{-1}$ now enters a region of a \textit{uniform electric field} between two parallel charged plates, separated by a distance of $4.0 \, \textrm{cm}$. The electron is initially halfway between the plates and its initial velocity is parallel to the plates. The upper plate is positively charged and the lower plate is negatively charged. There is a potential difference between the plates of $V = 30 \, \textrm{V}$.

	Find the horizontal distance travelled before hitting a plate. Which plate does the electron hit?

\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref={ex:electron_field_plates}]
First off, why do we know that the electron will hit a plate? Because the electric field between the plate exerts a force on the electron. The electron is attracted to the \textbf{positively} charged upper plate, so it will accelerate upwards and eventually hit the upper plate. Let us first find the acceleration caused by the electric field. The electric field strength $E$ between two parallel plates is given by:	
\begin{equation}
	E = \frac{V}{d} = \frac{30}{0.040} = 750 \, \textrm{V m}^{-1}
\end{equation}
Converting this to solve for the electron's acceleration:
\begin{equation}
	a = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{qE}{m} = \frac{(1.6\times10^{-19})(750)}{9.11\times10^{-31}} \approx 1.32\times10^{14} \, \textrm{m s}^{-2}
\end{equation}


To find the horizontal distance travelled, we use the vertical motion to find time, and then use that time to find the horizontal distance travelled. The vertical motion is a simple kinematics problem with constant acceleration:
\begin{align*}
	y &= v_{y0}t + \frac{1}{2}a_yt^2 \\
	0.02 &= 0 + \frac{1}{2}(1.32\times10^{14})t^2 \\
	t^2 &= \frac{0.04}{1.32\times10^{14}} \\
	t &\approx 1.74\times10^{-8} \, \textrm{s}	
\end{align*}	
Then, putting this into a horizontal motion equation:
\begin{align*}
	x &= v_{x0}t \\
	&= (9.4\times10^6)(1.74\times10^{-8}) \\
	&\approx 0.16 \, \textrm{m}
\end{align*}
So the electron travels a horizontal distance of about \textbf{0.16 meters} before hitting the upper plate.
\end{Answer}
\section{Lightning}\index{lightning}
A thundercloud is a tower of water droplets, ice crystals, and soft hail
(called graupel), churned by powerful updrafts. When small ice crystals
collide with heavier graupel, electrons are rubbed off, just like the
balloon on your hair. The heavy, negatively charged graupel sinks toward
the bottom of the cloud, while the light, positively charged ice crystals
are carried to the top.

Air is a good insulator, so at first the charge has nowhere to go. But it
keeps building until the electric field is strong enough to rip electrons
off air molecules, turning a path of air into a conductor. Then the charge
rushes through in a giant spark: lightning. It often strikes the tallest
object around, because that is the shortest path.
A great deal of lightning moves within a cloud or between clouds. However, sometimes
it jumps to the earth. These bolts of lightning vary in the amount of electrons
they carry, but the average is about 15 coulombs.

\section{But...}

The idea that like charges repel and opposite charges attract raises some
big questions that you do not yet have the tools to answer fully.

However, you probably have these questions, so we will point you in the direction
of the answers.

The first is ``In any atom bigger than hydrogen, there are multiple protons in
the nucleus. Why do not the protons push each other out of the nucleus?''

We are not ready to talk about it, but there is a force called \textit{the strong nuclear force}, which pulls the protons and neutrons in the nucleus of the atom
toward each other. At very, very small distances, it is strong enough to overpower
the repulsive force due to the protons' charges.

Another question is ``Why do the electrons whiz around in a cloud so far from
the nucleus of the atom? Negatively charged electrons should cling to the protons
in the center, right?''

We are not ready to talk about this either, but quantum mechanics tells us that electrons
like to live in a certain specific energy level. Hugging protons is not one of
those levels.

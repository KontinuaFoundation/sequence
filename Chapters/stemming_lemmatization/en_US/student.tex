\chapter{Stemming and Lemmatization}
\index{stemming}
\index{lemmatization}

Search your phone for ``running'' and you probably want the songs that say ``run'' and ``runs'' too. Your phone
does not understand English, so how does it know those words go together? Before a program can do anything
with text, whether searching it, sorting it, or deciding that a message is spam, it has to turn the text into
something it can count. This chapter shows how, and it ends with every message in a small data set turned into
a row of numbers, ready for the spam filter in the next chapter.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Tokenizing}\index{tokenizing} splits text into words, called \newterm{tokens}. We also lowercase
everything, so ``FREE'' and ``free'' count as the same word, and drop punctuation.
\item \textbf{Stop words}\index{stop words} like ``the,'' ``a,'' and ``you'' appear in almost every message,
so they tell you little about any particular one. Many programs remove them.
\item \textbf{Stemming or lemmatization} merges different forms of the same word, which is the subject of the
rest of the chapter.
\end{itemize}
These steps are called \newterm{text normalization}\index{text normalization}.

\section{Stemming}
\newterm{Stemming} reduces a word to its \index{stem}\newterm{stem} by chopping off endings. A stemmer would
reduce ``jumping,'' ``jumped,'' and ``jumps'' all to ``jump.''

The stem does not have to be a real word. The most famous stemmer, written by Martin Porter in 1980, turns
both ``studies'' and ``studying'' into ``studi.'' That is fine: the point of a stem is to group related words,
not to be pretty. Stemmers are fast and simple, because they follow spelling rules and never look anything
up.

\section{Lemmatization}
\newterm{Lemmatization} reduces a word to its \index{lemma}\newterm{lemma}, the form you would look up in a
dictionary: ``running'' and ``ran'' both become ``run,'' and ``better'' becomes ``good.'' A lemmatizer needs
a large dictionary of word forms, and often needs to know how the word is used in the sentence. (Is ``saw'' the
past tense of ``see,'' or a tool?) That makes it slower and more complicated, but more accurate.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l l l}
\textbf{Word} & \textbf{Typical stem} & \textbf{Lemma} \\
\hline
jumping & jump & jump \\
studies & studi & study \\
running & run & run \\
ran & ran & run \\
better & better & good \\
mice & mice & mouse \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Example}: Would a stemmer or a lemmatizer group ``ran'' with ``running''?

\textbf{Solution}: Only the lemmatizer. ``Ran'' has no ending to chop, so a stemmer leaves it alone, and ``ran''
and ``run'' are spelled too differently for any spelling rule to connect them. A lemmatizer has ``ran'' in its
dictionary as a form of ``run.''

\section{A Stemmer in Python}\index{stemming!in Python}
Real stemmers have dozens of rules. Ours has six, but it shows the idea. Here is \filename{text.py}, which you
will use again in the next chapter:

\begin{minted}{python}
"""Tools for turning text into lists of word stems."""
import re

STOP_WORDS = {"a", "an", "and", "are", "at", "be", "by", "can", "do", "for",
              "from", "have", "i", "in", "is", "it", "me", "my", "of", "on",
              "or", "so", "the", "this", "to", "we", "you", "your"}

SUFFIXES = [("sses", "ss"), ("ies", "i"), ("ing", ""), ("ed", ""),
            ("ly", ""), ("s", "")]

def tokenize(text):
    """Split text into lowercase words, dropping punctuation."""
    return re.findall(r"[a-z0-9$]+", text.lower())

def stem(word):
    """Chop one common suffix off a word, as long as 3 letters remain."""
    for suffix, replacement in SUFFIXES:
        if word.endswith(suffix) and len(word) - len(suffix) >= 3:
            word = word[:len(word) - len(suffix)] + replacement
            # running -> runn -> run
            if suffix in ("ing", "ed") and word[-1] == word[-2] and word[-1] not in "lsz":
                word = word[:-1]
            return word
    return word

def prepare(text):
    """Tokenize, drop stop words, and stem."""
    return [stem(w) for w in tokenize(text) if w not in STOP_WORDS]
\end{minted}

\verb|re.findall| uses a \newterm{regular expression}\index{regular expression}, a tiny pattern language for
text. The pattern \verb|[a-z0-9$]+| means ``one or more letters, digits, or dollar signs in a row,'' so
everything else, including spaces and punctuation, acts as a separator. (We keep \verb|$| because it turns out to
be a useful clue for spotting spam.)

The order of the suffix list matters: the stemmer uses the first rule that fits, so ``classes'' becomes
``class'' (by the ``sses'' rule) instead of ``classe'' (by the ``s'' rule). The ``at least 3 letters'' check keeps
it from turning ``is'' into ``i'' or ``sing'' into ``s.'' Try it:

\begin{minted}{python}
from text import stem
words = ["jumping", "jumped", "jumps", "studies", "running", "ran",
         "classes", "happily", "connected", "connection"]
print([stem(w) for w in words])
\end{minted}
\begin{verbatim}
['jump', 'jump', 'jump', 'studi', 'run', 'ran', 'class', 'happi', 'connect',
'connection']
\end{verbatim}
Just like Porter's stemmer, ours turns ``studies'' into ``studi'' and cannot connect ``ran'' with ``run.''
It also misses ``connection,'' because it has no rule for ``-ion.''

\section{Bag of Words}\index{bag of words}
Now we can turn a whole collection of messages into numbers. The file \filename{messages.csv} contains 60 short
text messages of the kind a high school student might get, each labeled \verb|spam| (22 messages) or \verb|ham|
(38 messages; ``ham'' is the traditional name for messages that are not spam).

The simplest numerical description of a message is a \newterm{bag of words}: count how many times each stem
appears, and forget the order. ``Dog bites man'' and ``man bites dog'' have the same bag. That sounds like a big
loss, but for many tasks, including spam filtering, which words appear matters much more than their order.

Here is \filename{bag\_of\_words.py}:

\begin{minted}{python}
import csv
import numpy as np
from text import tokenize, stem, prepare, STOP_WORDS

with open("messages.csv", newline="") as f:
    rows = list(csv.DictReader(f))
texts = [row["text"] for row in rows]
print("Messages:", len(texts))

# How big is the vocabulary after each step?
raw = {w for t in texts for w in t.split()}
lower = {w for t in texts for w in tokenize(t)}
no_stop = {w for w in lower if w not in STOP_WORDS}
stems = {stem(w) for w in no_stop}
for name, words in [("split on spaces", raw), ("lowercase, no punctuation", lower),
                    ("without stop words", no_stop), ("stemmed", stems)]:
    print(f"{name:26s} {len(words):4d} distinct words")

# Bag of words: one row per message, one column per stem
vocab = sorted(stems)
column = {word: j for j, word in enumerate(vocab)}
counts = np.zeros((len(texts), len(vocab)), dtype=int)
for i, t in enumerate(texts):
    for word in prepare(t):
        counts[i, column[word]] += 1
print("Count matrix:", counts.shape)

# The first message as counts
print({vocab[j]: int(counts[0, j]) for j in np.nonzero(counts[0])[0]})

# The most common stems overall
totals = counts.sum(axis=0)
top = np.argsort(-totals)[:8]
print("Most common:", [(vocab[j], int(totals[j])) for j in top])
\end{minted}

\begin{verbatim}

split on spaces             368 distinct words
lowercase, no punctuation   311 distinct words
without stop words          285 distinct words
stemmed                     271 distinct words
Count matrix: (60, 271)
{'been': 1, 'claim': 1, 'click': 1, 'free': 1, 'iphone': 1, 'link': 1,
'now': 1, 'prize': 1, 'select': 1, 'winner': 1}
Most common: [('free', 15), ('now', 11), ('claim', 9), ('click', 8), ('today', 6),
('earn', 5), ('cash', 5), ('before', 4)]
\end{verbatim}

The first message, ``WINNER!! You have been selected for a FREE iPhone. Click the link to claim your prize
now,'' has become ten counts of 1, one for each stem it contains. Each step shrinks the vocabulary by merging things that should be the same. ``FREE,'' ``free,'' and ``free!!''
are one word now. The result is a $60 \times 271$ matrix of counts, with one row per message. Most entries
are zero, since each message uses only a handful of the 271 stems; a matrix like that is called
\newterm{sparse}\index{sparse matrix}.

Look at the most common stems: free, now, claim, click, cash. Every one of them smells like spam. That is
exactly the pattern the naive Bayes classifier in the next chapter will learn.

\section{Where These Ideas Are Used}
\index{stemming!applications of}\index{lemmatization!applications of}
Search engines stem both your query and their documents, so ``connected'' finds ``connecting.'' Spam filters
and other text classifiers use bags of words as features. Modern language models, like the ones behind chatbots,
skip stemming and split text into pieces of words called subword tokens, but the first step is the same:
turn text into numbers a program can work with.

\begin{Exercise}[label=stem1, title=Stem or lemma?]
For each pair of words, say whether a stemmer would likely give them the same stem, and whether a lemmatizer
would give them the same lemma: (a) ``happy'' and ``happiness''; (b) ``better'' and ``good''.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=stem1]
(a) A stemmer such as Porter's often maps both to ``happi,'' so they match. A lemmatizer keeps ``happiness'' as
its own noun and ``happy'' as an adjective, so they do not match. (b) A stemmer does not link ``better'' to
``good,'' because the spelling is too different. A lemmatizer that knows irregular forms can map ``better'' to
``good.''
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=stem2, title=Search Results]
A search engine stems every word in its documents and every word you type, using Porter's stemmer. You search
for ``connected.'' Which of these documents will match: one containing ``connection,'' one containing
``connecting,'' and one containing ``linked''?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=stem2]
The Porter stemmer reduces ``connected,'' ``connection,'' and ``connecting'' all to ``connect,'' so the first two
documents match. ``Linked'' means the same thing but shares no letters, so stemming cannot find it. Matching
synonyms takes a different technique, such as a thesaurus or word embeddings.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=stem3, title={Run the stemmer by hand}]
Use the stemming rules from this chapter on these words: dresses, hopping, filled, sing, quickly.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=stem3]
``dresses'' $\to$ ``dress'' (the ``sses'' rule). ``hopping'' $\to$ ``hopp'' $\to$ ``hop'' (the ``ing'' rule, then
the doubled letter is dropped). ``filled'' $\to$ ``fill'' (the ``ed'' rule; the doubled ``l'' is kept, because
``l'' is on the list of letters not to undouble). ``sing'' stays ``sing'': removing ``ing'' would leave only one
letter. ``quickly'' $\to$ ``quick'' (the ``ly'' rule).
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=stem4, title={A rule for -ion}]
Add a rule to \verb|SUFFIXES| so that ``connection'' and ``selection'' stem to ``connect'' and ``select.'' Where
in the list does it need to go, and does it break any word in the example list?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=stem4]
Add \verb|("ion", "")|. It can go anywhere before \verb|("s", "")|, since no word ends in both; putting it first is
simplest. ``connection'' becomes ``connect'' and ``selection'' becomes ``select.'' None of the other example words
end in ``ion,'' so their stems do not change. (It does hurt some other words: ``champion'' becomes ``champ'' and ``million''
becomes ``mill.'' The 3-letter check saves ``lion'' and ``onion.'' Every simple rule has exceptions.)
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=stem5, title={A tiny lemmatizer}]
Write a function \verb|lemmatize(word)| that looks the word up in a small dictionary of irregular forms
(ran, better, mice, was) and returns the word unchanged if it is not there. Why would a real lemmatizer need a dictionary with hundreds of thousands of entries?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=stem5]
\begin{minted}{python}
LEMMAS = {"ran": "run", "better": "good", "mice": "mouse", "was": "be"}

def lemmatize(word):
    return LEMMAS.get(word, word)
\end{minted}
\verb|dict.get| returns the second argument when the key is missing. A real lemmatizer needs an entry for every
form of every word in the language (plus rules for using context to tell ``saw'' the verb from ``saw'' the
tool), and English has hundreds of thousands of words.
\end{Answer}

\chapter{Diodes, Rectifiers, and Photovoltaics}

The phone in your pocket contains billions of tiny switches, all carved
from a material that is neither a good conductor nor a good insulator: a
\index{semiconductor}\newterm{semiconductor}. The simplest semiconductor
device is the \newterm{diode}\index{diode}, a one-way valve for current.
Understand the diode, and you are most of the way to understanding solar
cells, LEDs, transistors, and the chips inside every computer.

We are going to focus on semiconductors made of silicon. You can make semiconductors out of other materials, but silicon has proved to be very versatile and cost-effective.

\section{Silicon}

A silicon atom has 14 protons and 14 electrons.  The inner 10 electrons are arranged in two complete and stable shells.  The outer four electrons are in an incomplete shell, which allows them to form bonds with other atoms. These outer electrons are called \index{valence electron}\newterm{valence
electrons}, and they are the ones that form bonds. A complete outer shell would have eight electrons.\index{silicon}

Atoms can share electrons to get complete shells.  This is called \index{covalent bond}\newterm{covalent bonding}.  When atoms share electrons, they form a \newterm{covalent bond}.  The electrons are shared between the atoms, and the atoms are held together by the shared electrons.

In a silicon crystal, each silicon atom shares its outer four electrons with four neighboring atoms. With its four electrons and an electron borrowed from each of it four neighbors, each silicon atom has eight electrons in its outer shell. This is a complete shell, and the atoms are stable. With every electron
locked into a bond, almost none are free to move, so pure silicon conducts
electricity poorly.

Phosphorus has five valence electrons. If you put a phosphorus atom into silicon crystal, it shares four of its electrons with its neighbors, but it has one electron left over after the complete shell of eight is built. That extra electron is free to wander through the crystal and carry
current. The more phosphorus you add, the better the silicon conducts.
Adding small amounts of another element on purpose is called
\newterm{doping}.\index{phosphorus}\index{doping}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{doping-27.png}
    \caption{Phosphorus atom in a silicon crystal}
    \label{fig:n-doping}
\end{figure}

Because this kind of doping frees up some electrons, which are negatively charged, we call this \newterm{n-doping}.\index{n-doped}

Boron has only three valence electrons. If you put a boron atom into silicon crystal, it shares three of its electrons with its neighbors, which leaves a \index{hole (semiconductor)}"hole" that would like to be filled by an electron. We call this \newterm{p-doping}.\index{p-doped}\index{boron}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{doping-26.png}
    \caption{Boron atom in a silicon crystal}
    \label{fig:p-doping}
\end{figure}

Both n-doped and p-doped silicon are conductors of electricity.  In n-doped silicon, we say the loose electrons are the \index{charge carriers}\newterm{carriers} of the current. In p-doped silicon, we say the holes are the carriers.

Doped silicon is still almost entirely silicon. Often only about one atom
in a million is replaced with boron or phosphorus, and that is enough to
change its conductivity enormously.

\section{Diodes}

A diode is a semiconductor device that allows current to flow in one direction only.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{diode_Diode-14.png}
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{diode_Diode-15.png}
    \caption{Current flows in one direction through the diode, not in the other direction.}
    \label{fig:go-diode}
\end{figure}

Diodes can be made with silicon that has been n-doped and p-doped. A diode looks like this:

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{diodeReal.png}
    \caption{What Diodes Look Like}
    \label{fig:diode-real}
\end{figure}

We say that current flows from the positive terminal to the negative terminal of a battery. However, remember that the electrons are actually moving from the negative terminal to the positive terminal. This is a common source of confusion in any discussion of diodes.

One end of the diode is called the \newterm{anode}.  The anode is p-doped.  The other end is called the \newterm{cathode}.  The cathode is n-doped.\index{anode}\index{cathode}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{diodeDiagram2.png}
    \caption{A p-n Diode}
    \label{fig:diode-real-diodes-2}
\end{figure}

The region close to where the anode and cathode meet is called the \newterm{depletion region}. In this space, the spare electrons in the cathode (the n-doped material) migrate over to fill the holes in anode (the p-doped material). This leaves the anode side slightly negative (extra electrons) and the
cathode side slightly positive (missing electrons), creating an electric
field across the junction. For silicon, the voltage across this region is
about 0.7 volts. It acts like a small hill that pushes back on any more
electrons trying to cross from the cathode to the anode.\index{depletion region}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{diodeProcess-31.png}
    \caption{The depletion region in a diode}
    \label{fig:diode-depletion}
\end{figure}

What happens if we overpower that 0.7 volts? Push electrons into the
cathode with, say, 2 volts. This shrinks the depletion region, and the
diode becomes a conductor. This is called \newterm{forward bias}. For example, if the negative end of a battery is connected to the cathode of a diode and the positive end is connected to the anode, current will flow through the diode.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{diodeProcess-32.png}
    \caption{Electrons flow through the diode}
    \label{fig:diode-flow}
\end{figure}

If we reverse the battery (\newterm{reverse bias}), the depletion region
grows, and the diode becomes an insulator.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{diodeProcess-33.png}
    \caption{Reverse the electric field? No flow.}
    \label{fig:diode-no-flow}
\end{figure}

Here is the \index{diode!symbol}symbol for a diode:

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{Diode_symbol.png}
    \caption{Diode Symbol}
    \label{fig:diode-symbol}
\end{figure}

Conventional current flows in the direction of the arrow (electrons flow
the other way). It will not flow in the opposite direction.

\textbf{Light-emitting diodes.} When an electron crosses the junction of a
forward-biased diode and falls into a hole, it gives up energy. In
ordinary silicon diodes, that energy becomes heat. In an \newterm{LED},
made from materials like gallium arsenide, it comes out as a photon. The
energy drop sets the photon's energy, and so its color: about 1.8 eV for
red, 2.6 eV for blue. That is why LEDs need different voltages for
different colors.

\textbf{Worked example: current through a silicon diode.} A diode that is conducting drops about $0.7$ V. Put it in series with a $1$ k$\Omega$ resistor and a $5$ V supply. The resistor sees $5 - 0.7 = 4.3$ V, so the current is $I = \frac{4.3}{1000} = 4.3$ mA.

\subsection{Rectifiers}

One of the really useful things we can do with a diode is to convert alternating current (AC) into direct current (DC). This is called rectification. Remember that AC pushes and pulls the electrons back and forth in the wire. Rectification ensures that the electrons only move in one direction.\index{rectifier}

A \newterm{half-wave rectifier} uses a single diode to ensure that the current flows in one direction through the load.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{halfwave.png}
    \caption{Half-wave Rectifier}
    \label{fig:halfwave-rectifier}
\end{figure}

Current flows in the direction of the arrow on the diode. If the voltage is reversed, the diode will block the current from moving.\index{half-wave rectifier}

A \newterm{full-wave rectifier} uses four diodes to swap the direction of the current through the load when necessary.\index{full-wave rectifier}

\begin{figure}[H]

    \label{fig:fullwave-rectifier}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Photovoltaics}

A solar cell (or \newterm{photovoltaic} cell) is an LED run backward.
\index{solar cells}\index{photovoltaics}It is a large, thin diode with a
\index{p-n junction}p-n junction just under the surface, where sunlight can
reach it. When a photon with enough energy (more than about 1.1 eV for
silicon) is absorbed near the junction, it knocks an electron loose,
leaving a hole behind.

Now the built-in field of the depletion region takes over. It pushes the
freed electron toward the n side (the cathode) and the hole toward the p
side (the anode). Electrons pile up on the n side, and the cell becomes a
small battery. The electrons cannot cross back through the junction
against the field, so they take the long way: out through the wires,
through the load, and back to the p side, where they fill the holes.

Each silicon cell makes only about half a volt, so a solar panel connects
dozens of cells in series.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{solarcell.png}
    \caption{Solar Cell}
    \label{fig:solarcell}
\end{figure}

\begin{Exercise}[title={How many cells?}, label=diode_solar]
Each solar cell in a panel produces 0.5 V. How many cells must be connected
in series to make a panel that produces 30 V?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=diode_solar]
$\frac{30}{0.5} = 60$ cells, which is a very common panel size.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title={A Larger Supply}, label=diode_current]
A $12$ V supply, a silicon diode with a $0.7$ V drop, and a $2$ k$\Omega$ resistor are in series. Find the current.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=diode_current]
$I = \frac{12 - 0.7}{2000} = 5.65 \times 10^{-3}$ A, or $5.65$ mA.
\end{Answer}

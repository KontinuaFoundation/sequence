\chapter{Vectors}


\section{Vectors}
So far, we have described forces by their size alone: 10 newtons, 500
newtons. But a push to the left and a push to the right are very
different, even if they are the same size. To do math with things that
have both a size and a direction, like forces and velocities, we need
\newterm{vectors}.
\index{vectors}

Imagine jumping out of a plane (with a parachute, please). Several forces
act on you at once, each with its own size and direction. Gravity pulls you
straight down with a force proportional to your mass. If there were a wind from the west, it would push you toward the 
east. That force will be proportional to the square of the speed of the wind 
and approximately proportional to your size. Once you are falling, there will 
be resistance from the air that you are pushing through --- that force will 
point in the opposite direction from the direction you are moving and will be 
proportional to the square of your speed.
% Image added

\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{skydiver.png}

To find the net force, which tells you how you will accelerate, you must
add these forces together, directions and all. That takes vector math.

\subsection{Adding Vectors}

A vector is typically represented as a list of numbers, with each
number representing a particular dimension. For example, if you are
creating a \index{dimension}3-dimensional vector representing a force, it will have
three numbers representing the amount of force in each of the three
axes. For example, if a force of one newton is in the direction of the
$x$-axis, you might represent the vector as $v = [1, 0, 0]$. 
Another vector might be $u = [0.5, 0.9, 0.7]$ \index{vectors!adding}. You can 
see examples of 2-dimensional and 3-dimensional vectors in figures 
\ref{fig:2d_vectors} and \ref{fig:3d_vectors}.

If you know the vectors, you will just add them element-wise:

$$ u + v = [0.5, 0.9, 0.7] + [1.0, 0.0, 0.0] = [1.5, 0.9, 0.7] $$

These vectors have 3 components, so we say they are \newterm{3-dimensional}. 
Vectors can have any number of components. For example, the vector
 $[-12.2, 3, \pi, 10000]$ is 4-dimensional.

 You can only add two vectors if they have the same dimension.

 $$ [12, -4] + [-1, 5] = [11,1] $$

 Addition is commutative; if you have two vectors $a$ and $b$, then
 $a + b$ is the same as $b + a$.

 Addition is also associative: If you have three vectors $a$, $b$, and $c$,
 it does not matter which order you add them in. 
 That is, $a + (b + c) = (a + b) + c$.

 A 1-dimensional vector is just a number. We say it is a 
 \newterm{scalar}\index{scalar}, not a vector.

 \begin{Exercise}[title={Adding vectors}, label=adding_vectors]
Add the following vectors:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $[1, 2, 3] + [4, 5, 6]$
    \item $[-1, -2, -3, -4] + [4, 5, 6, 7]$
    \item $[\pi, 0, 0] + [0, \pi, 0] + [0, 0, \pi]$
\end{itemize}
\end{Exercise}

        \item $[1, 2, 3] + [4, 5, 6] = [5, 7, 9]$
        \item $[-1, -2, -3, -4] + [4, 5, 6, 7] = [3, 3, 3, 3]$
        \item $[\pi, 0, 0] + [0, \pi, 0] + [0, 0, \pi] = [\pi, \pi, \pi]$ 
    \end{itemize}
\end{Answer}

\subsection{Multiplying a vector with a scalar}

engines pointed the same way, and the total force is $9v$. That is
multiplying a vector by a scalar.\index{vectors!multiplying by a scalar}

goes in the same direction as $u$ but has a magnitude $a$ times as long as 
$u$. A visual is presented in figure \ref{fig:multiply}.

\subsection{Magnitude of a Vector}

The \newterm{magnitude} of a vector is just its length. We write the 
magnitude of a vector $v$ as $|v|$.\index{vectors!magnitude of}

We compute the magnitude using the Pythagorean theorem.  If $v = [3,4,5]$, 
then

\begin{equation*}
    |v| = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2 + 5^2} = \sqrt{50} \approx 7.07
\end{equation*}

(You might notice that the notation for the magnitude is exactly like the 
notation for absolute value. If you think of a scalar as a 1-dimensional 
vector, the absolute value and the magnitude are the same. For example, the 
absolute value of -5 is 5.  If you take the magnitude of the one-dimensional 
vector $[-5]$,you get $\sqrt{25} = 5$.)

Where does this equation come from? Consider a 2-dimensional vector, 
$\textbf{v} = \left[ 3, 4 \right]$. This means the vector represents 3 
units in the $x$-direction, and 4 units in the $y$-direction. We can then 
visualize a right triangle, with the vector being the hypotenuse and the legs 
being the $x$- and $y$-components of the vector (see figure 
\ref{fig:triangle}). As you recall, the length of the hypotenuse of a right 
triangle is the square root of the sum of the squares of the legs. That is:
$$c = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$$

where $c$ is the length of the hypotenuse and $a$ and $b$ are the lengths of
the legs. For $[3, 4]$, that is $\sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$.

In 3 dimensions, use the theorem twice: $[3, 4]$ along the floor has
length 5, and climbing 5 more straight up makes a new right triangle with
hypotenuse $\sqrt{5^2 + 5^2} = \sqrt{50}$. The same trick works in any
number of dimensions. 
\vspace*{1cm}

\begin{mdframed}[style = important, frametitle = {Magnitude of Vectors}]
For an $n$-dimensional vector, $\textbf{v} = \left[ x_1, x_2, x_3, \cdots, x_n 
\right]$, the magnitude of the vector is given by:
$$\left| \textbf{v} \right| = \sqrt{x_1^2 + x_2^2 + x_3^2 + \cdots + x_n^2}$$
\end{mdframed}

Notice that if you scale up a vector, its magnitude scales by the same amount. 
For example:

\begin{equation*}
|7[3,4,5]| = 7 \sqrt{50} \approx 7 \times 7.07    
\end{equation*}

Here is why that is true. Suppose we have a vector, $\textbf{u} = \left[a, b, 
c \right]$. Then the magnitude of $\textbf{u}$ is given by:
$$\left| \textbf{u} \right| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}$$

If we scale \textbf{u} to create \textbf{v} such that $\textbf{v} = k 
\textbf{u} = \left[ ka, kb, kc \right]$, where $k$ is some constant. Then the 
magnitude of \textbf{v} is given by:
$$\left| \textbf{v} \right| = \sqrt{\left( ka \right)^2 + \left( kb \right)^2 
+ \left( kc \right)^2}$$

We can expand and simplify this equation:
$$\left| \textbf{v} \right| = \sqrt{k^2 a^2 + k^2 b^2 + k^2 c^2}$$
$$\left| \textbf{v} \right| = \sqrt{k^2 \left( a^2 + b^2 + c^2 \right)}$$
$$\left| \textbf{v} \right| = \left( \sqrt{k^2} \right) \sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + 
c^2}$$
$$\left| \textbf{v} \right| = \left| k \right| \sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2} = 
\left| k \right| \left| \textbf{u} \right|$$

So, if you scale a vector, the magnitude of the resulting vector is the absolute value of the scale factor times the magnitude of the original vector. 

The rule then is: If you have any vector $v$ and any scalar $k$:
\begin{equation*}
    |k v| = |k| |v|
\end{equation*}

\subsubsection{Unit Vectors}

A \emph{unit vector} is a vector whose magnitude is \(1\).\index{unit vector}
For any non-zero vector \(\vec{\mathbf v}\), the unit vector \(\vec{\mathbf{u}}\) pointing in the
same direction is
\[
\vec{\mathbf u}\;=\;\frac{\mathbf v}{\lvert \mathbf v\rvert}.
\]

For example, if \(\mathbf v=[3,4,5]\) then
\[
\lvert\mathbf v\rvert
   \;=\;\sqrt{3^{2}+4^{2}+5^{2}}
   \;=\;\sqrt{50},
\]
so
\[
\vec{\mathbf u}
   \;=\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{50}}\,[3,4,5]
   \;=\;\Bigl[\tfrac{3}{\sqrt{50}},\,
              \tfrac{4}{\sqrt{50}},\,
              \tfrac{5}{\sqrt{50}}\Bigr].
\]

\[
\boxed{\displaystyle
\widehat{\mathbf u}=\frac{\mathbf v}{\lvert\mathbf v\rvert}},\qquad
\lvert\vec{\mathbf u}\rvert=1.
\]

\section{The Dot Product}
% Reference for diagrams:https://www.mathsisfun.com/algebra/vectors-dot-product.html

If you have two vectors $\textbf{u} = [u_1, u_2, \dots, u_n]$ and $\textbf{v} 
= [v_1, v_2,\dots, v_n]$, we define the \newterm{dot product} $\textbf{u} 
\cdot \textbf{v}$ as 
\begin{equation*}
     \textbf{u} \cdot \textbf{v} = (u_1 \times v_1) + (u_2 \times v_2) + \dots 
     + (u_n \times v_n)
\end{equation*} 
The output of the dot product is a \emph{scalar} quantity.


For example, 
\begin{equation*}
    [2,4, -3] \cdot [5, -1, 1] = 2 \times 5 + 4 \times -1 + -3 \times 1 = 3
\end{equation*}\index{dot product}

Multiply matching components and add. That hardly sounds exciting, but dot
products are \emph{incredibly} useful. The graphics chip that renders your
favorite video game spends much of its time computing dot products, billions
of them per second, to figure out how brightly each surface is lit. 

\begin{Exercise}[title={Basic dot products}, label=dot_products]
    Compute the dot product of each pair of vectors:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $[1, 2, 3]$, $[4, 5, -6]$
        \item $[\pi, 2\pi]$, $[2, -1]$
        \item $[0,0,0,0]$, $[10,10,10,10]$
    \end{itemize}
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=dot_products]
        \begin{itemize}
            \item $[1, 2, 3] \cdot [4, 5, -6] = 4 + 10 - 18 = -4$
            \item $[\pi, 2\pi] \cdot [2, -1] = 2\pi - 2\pi = 0$
            \item $[0,0,0,0] \cdot [10,10,10,10] = 0 + 0 + 0 + 0 = 0$ 
        \end{itemize}
\end{Answer}

\subsection{Properties of the dot product}

Sometimes we need an easy way to say ``The vector of appropriate length is 
filled with zeros.'' \index{zero vector}We use the notation $\vec{0}$ to represent this. Then, 
for any vector $\textbf{v}$, this is true:

$$\textbf{v} \cdot \vec{0} = 0$$

The dot product is commutative:

$$\textbf{v} \cdot \textbf{u} = \textbf{u} \cdot \textbf{v}$$

The dot product of a vector with itself is its magnitude squared:

$$ \textbf{v} \cdot \textbf{v} = |\textbf{v}|^2 $$

If you have a scalar $a$, then:

    $$(\textbf{v}) \cdot (a \textbf{u}) = a (\textbf{v} \cdot \textbf{u})$$

So, if $\textbf{v}$ and $\textbf{w}$ are vectors that go in the same direction,

    $$\textbf{v} \cdot \textbf{w} = |\textbf{v}| |\textbf{w}|$$

If $\textbf{v}$ and $\textbf{w}$ are vectors that go in opposite directions,

    $$\textbf{v} \cdot \textbf{w} = -|\textbf{v}| |\textbf{w}|$$
    
\index{perpendicular vectors}If $\textbf{v}$ and $\textbf{w}$ are vectors that are perpendicular to each
other, their dot product is zero:

  $$ \textbf{v} \cdot \textbf{w} = 0 $$

\subsection{Cosines and dot products}

Here is what makes the dot product so useful. For any two vectors
$\textbf{v}$ and $\textbf{u}$,

$$\textbf{v} \cdot \textbf{u} = |\textbf{v}| |\textbf{u}| \cos \theta$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between them.

So, for example, if two vectors $\textbf{v}$ and $\textbf{u}$ are 
perpendicular, the angle between them is $\pi/2$. The cosine of $\pi/2$ is 0. 
The dot product of any two perpendicular vectors is always 0. In fact, if the 
dot product of two non-zero vectors is 0, the vectors \textit{must be} 
perpendicular (see figure \ref{fig:perpendicular} for an example of 
perpendicular 2-dimensional vectors).

% cosine is not introduced before here
If you have two non-zero vectors $\textbf{v}$ and $\textbf{u}$, you can always 
compute the angle between them:\index{vectors!angle between}

$$\theta = \arccos{ \left( \frac{\textbf{v} \cdot \textbf{u}}{|\textbf{v}| 
|\textbf{u}|} \right)}$$

\index{arccosine}Arccos, short for arccosine and also written
$\cos^{-1}$, undoes cosine. Cosine takes an angle and gives the
$x$-coordinate of that point on the unit circle; arccos takes the
$x$-coordinate and gives back an angle. But which angle? Many angles share
the same cosine (figures \ref{fig:cosine-vectors} and \ref{fig:arccos}).

To make arccos a function, we keep only answers between $0$ and $\pi$.
That is exactly the range of possible angles between two vectors, so it
works out perfectly.

\textbf{Example}: What is the angle between $\textbf{u} = \left[ \sqrt{3}, 1 
\right]$ and $\textbf{v} = \left[ 0, -1 \right]$?

\textbf{Solution}: We know that $\textbf{u} \cdot \textbf{v} = \left| 
\textbf{u} \right| \left| \textbf{v} \right| \cos{\theta}$. Therefore, we also 
know that:
$$\cos{\theta} = \frac{\textbf{u} \cdot \textbf{v}}{\left| \textbf{u} \right| 
\left| \textbf{v} \right|}$$

First, let us compute the dot product:
$$\textbf{u} \cdot \textbf{v} = \sqrt{3} \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot -1 = -1$$

And therefore:
$$\cos{\theta} = \frac{-1}{\left| \textbf{u} \right| \left| \textbf{v} 
\right|}$$

Now, let us find the magnitudes of both vectors:
$$\left| \textbf{u} \right| = \sqrt{\left( \sqrt{3} \right)^2 + \left( 1 
\right)^2} = 2$$
$$\left| \textbf{v} \right| = \sqrt{\left( 0 \right)^2 + \left( -1 \right)^2} 
= 1$$

Substituting for the magnitudes, we find that:
$$\cos{\theta} = \frac{-1}{2 \cdot 1} = \frac{-1}{2}$$

To solve for $\theta$, we take the $\arccos$ of both sides:
$$\arccos{ \left( \cos{\theta} \right)} = \theta = \arccos{ \frac{-1}{2} }$$

What angles have a cosine of $-1 / 2$? We know that $2\pi / 3$, $4 \pi / 3$, 
$8 \pi / 3$, etc., all have a cosine of $-1 / 2$. Because the range of 
$\arccos$ is restricted to between $0$ and $\pi$, our result is:
$$\theta = \arccos{ \frac{-1}{2}} = \frac{2\pi}{3}$$.

Therefore, the angle between \textbf{u} and \textbf{v} is $2\pi / 3$ (or $120^{
\circ}$).

    What is the angle between these each pair of vectors:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $[1, 0]$, $[0, 1]$
        \item $[3,4]$, $[4,3]$
        \item $[ 2, -1, 2 ]$, $[-1, 2, -2 ]$

    \end{itemize}

\begin{Answer}[ref=cos_dot_products]
        \begin{itemize}
            \item $[1,0] \cdot [0,1] = 0$.  The angle must be $\pi/2$.
            \item $[3,4] \cdot [4, 3] = 24$. $|[3,4]| |[4,3]| \cos(\theta) = 24$. 
            $\cos(\theta) = \frac{24}{(5)(5)}$. $\theta = \arccos(\frac{24}{25}) 
            \approx 0.284 \text{ radians}$.
            \item $[2, -1, 2] \cdot [-1, 2, -2] = 4 - 2 - 4 = -2$. $|[2, -1, 2]| = \sqrt{4 + 1 + 4} = \sqrt{9} = 3$. $|[-1, 2, -2]| = \sqrt{1 + 4 + 4} = \sqrt{9} = 3$. $3(3) \cos{\theta} = -2$. $\theta = \arccos{ (-2 / 9)} \approx 1.795 \text{ radians}$.
            \item $[ -5, 0, -1] \cdot [2, 3, -4] = -10 + 0 + 4 = -6$. $|[-5, 0, -1]| = \sqrt{25 + 0 + 1} = \sqrt{26}$. $|[2, 3, -4]| = \sqrt{4 + 9 + 16} = \sqrt{29}$. $\sqrt{26} (\sqrt{29}) \cos{\theta} = -6$. $\theta = \arccos{(\frac{-6}{\sqrt{26}\sqrt{29}})} \approx 1.791 \text{ radians}$.
        \end{itemize}
\end{Answer}

\subsection{Work and Power}
%diagram here
In an earlier chapter, work was force times distance. Push a train with 10
newtons as it rolls 5 meters down the track, and you do 50 joules of work.

Now push the train sideways, straight at the side of the car, as it rolls
those same 5 meters. You do no work at all: the train did not move one
centimeter in the direction you pushed.

% \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{train.png}

Dot products handle both cases. The \newterm{work}\index{work!dot
product} you do is the dot product of your force vector and the
displacement vector (the vector from where the train started to where it
ended up):
$$W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{d} = |\vec{F}| |\vec{d}| \cos\theta$$
$$P = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{v} = |\vec{F}| |\vec{v}| \cos\theta$$

Likewise, \newterm{power}\index{power} is the dot product of the force
vector and the velocity vector. If you push a sled with 10 newtons while it
moves at 2 meters per second, but your push is 20 degrees off, you are not
delivering 20 watts. You are delivering $10 \times 2 \times \cos(20 \text{ degrees}) \approx 18.8$ watts of power.

\begin{Exercise}[title={Power at an angle}, label=power_angle]
A person pushes a skateboard with a force of $40$ N. The skateboard is moving forward at
$3$ m/s, but the person's push is angled $30^\circ$ above the direction of motion.
How much power is being transferred to the skateboard?
\end{Exercise}

\begin{Answer}[ref=power_angle]
Power is the dot product of the force vector and the velocity vector:
\[
P = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{v}
\]

Since the force and velocity are at an angle, we can use:
\[
P = |\vec{F}| |\vec{v}| \cos\theta
\]

Here, $|\vec{F}| = 40$ N, $|\vec{v}| = 3$ m/s, and $\theta = 30^\circ$:
\[
P = 40(3)\cos(30^\circ)
\]

Since $\cos(30^\circ) \approx 0.866$:
\[
P \approx 120(0.866) \approx 104 \text{ W}
\]

Only the part of the force pointing in the direction of motion transfers power
to the skateboard. The angled part still matters physically, but it does not all help
move the skateboard forward. So the power transferred to the skateboard is about $104$ watts.
\end{Answer}

The same idea explains potential energy. If a cart moves a distance $L$ up a ramp at angle $\theta$, its displacement is
$\vec{d} = [L\cos\theta, L\sin\theta]$. Gravity is $\vec{F}_g = [0, -mg]$.

Therefore,
$$\vec{F}_g \cdot \vec{d} = [0, -mg] \cdot [L\cos\theta, L\sin\theta]
= -mgL\sin\theta.$$

Since $L\sin\theta$ is the height $h$ the cart climbs, gravity does
$-mgh$ joules of work. That is why the cart gains $mgh$ of potential
energy: someone else had to do $mgh$ of work against gravity. Notice that
the angle of the ramp dropped out. Only the height matters.

\begin{Exercise}[title={Work done on a Box}, label={ex:work_pll}]
A force $\vec{F} = [12, 5]$ N pushes a box whose displacement is
$\vec{d} = [3, 0]$ m. How much work is done?
\end{Exercise}

\begin{Answer}[ref={ex:work_pll}]
The work done is the dot product of the force vector and the displacement vector:
\[
W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{d}
\]

Here, the force is $\vec{F} = [12, 5]$ N and the displacement is
$\vec{d} = [3, 0]$ m. Take the dot product:
\[
W = [12, 5] \cdot [3, 0] = 12(3) + 5(0) = 36 \text{ J}
\]

The horizontal part of the force does work because the box moves horizontally.
The vertical part of the force does no work because the box does not move
vertically. So the total work done is $36$ joules.
\end{Answer}


\section{Cross Product}
\subsection{The Cross Product}
The dot product of two vectors is a number that measures how much they
\emph{line up}. The \newterm{cross product} of two 3D vectors is a
\emph{vector}: it points perpendicular to both of them, and its length
measures how much they \emph{do not} line up. Physicists use it for
anything that twists: turning a wrench, a spinning top, the force a magnet
exerts on a moving charge.
\index{cross product}

The formula is often written as a grid with the unit vectors
$\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}$ in the top row. (In linear algebra,
you will recognize the grid as a \emph{determinant}.) For now, treat it as
a memory aid: to get each component, cover that component's column and
cross-multiply the four numbers that remain, then flip the sign of the
middle one.
\begin{mdframed}[style=important]
Given two vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$,
\[
\vec{\mathbf{a}} = \langle a_x, a_y, a_z \rangle, \quad
\vec{\mathbf{b}} = \langle b_x, b_y, b_z \rangle,
\]
their \emph{cross product} is defined as
\[
\vec{\mathbf{a}} \times \vec{\mathbf{b}} =
\begin{vmatrix}
\mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\
a_x & a_y & a_z \\
b_x & b_y & b_z
\end{vmatrix}
\]
Expanding this yields:
\[
\vec{\mathbf{a}} \times \vec{\mathbf{b}} =
(a_y b_z - a_z b_y)\,\mathbf{i}
- (a_x b_z - a_z b_x)\,\mathbf{j}
+ (a_x b_y - a_y b_x)\,\mathbf{k}.
\]
where $\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \text{and} \mathbf{k}$ are \index{unit vectors}unit vectors.
\end{mdframed}


\subsubsection{Geometric Interpretation}\index{cross product!geometric interpretation}
The cross product $\vec{\mathbf{a}} \times \vec{\mathbf{b}}$ has the following properties:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item It is perpendicular to both $\vec{\mathbf{a}}$ and $\vec{\mathbf{b}}$, making it \index{orthogonality}\emph{orthogonal} as well (orthogonality can be thought of as 3D perpendicularity).
    \item Orthogonality implies $\vec{\mathbf{a}} \times \vec{\mathbf{b}} = \vec{\mathbf{c}}$, $\vec{\mathbf{a}} \cdot \vec{\mathbf{c}} = 0$ and $\vec{\mathbf{b}} \cdot \vec{\mathbf{c}} = 0$. 
    \item \index{cross product!magnitude}Its magnitude is
    \[
    \|\vec{\mathbf{a}} \times \vec{\mathbf{b}}\| = \|\vec{\mathbf{a}}\|\,\|\vec{\mathbf{b}}\| \sin\theta,
    \]
    where $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec{\mathbf{a}}$ and $\vec{\mathbf{b}}$.
    \item The magnitude equals the area of the parallelogram spanned by $\vec{\mathbf{a}}$ and $\vec{\mathbf{b}}$.
    \item Two parallel vectors $\vec{\mathbf{a}}$ and $\vec{\mathbf{b}}$ result in the zero vector, $\vec{\mathbf{0}}$. 
\end{enumerate}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{cross_product_geometry.png}
    \caption{The cross product $\vec{\mathbf{a}} \times \vec{\mathbf{b}}$ is perpendicular to both $\vec{\mathbf{a}}$ and $\vec{\mathbf{b}}$, and its magnitude equals the area of the parallelogram they span.}
    \label{fig:cross-product-geometry}
\end{figure}

\begin{Exercise}[title = {Using the cross product}, label = cross1]
\index{cross product!example}Find the cross product of the vectors $\vec{v_1} = \langle4, 5, 6\rangle$ and $\vec{v_2} = \langle3, 7, -8\rangle$ 
\end{Exercise}

\begin{Answer}[ref = cross1]
Setting up the matrix, we get:
\[\begin{bmatrix}
    \vec{\mathbf{i}} & \vec{\mathbf{j}} & \vec{\mathbf{k}} \\ 
    4 & 5 & 6 \\ 
    3 & 7 & -8 
\end{bmatrix}
\]
\[\left(5(-8) - (7)(6)\right)\vec{\mathbf{i}} - ((4)(-8) - (6)(3))\vec{\mathbf{j}} + ((4)(7)-(5)(3))\vec{\mathbf{k}}
\]
\[
(-40 - 42 ) \vec{\mathbf{i}} - (-32 - 18)\vec{\mathbf{j}} + (28 - 15) \vec{\mathbf{k}}
\]
\[
-82 \vec{\mathbf{i}} +50\vec{\mathbf{j}} + 13 \vec{\mathbf{k}} \implies \langle-82, 50, 13\rangle
\]
Check: the answer should be perpendicular to both vectors.
$\langle 4, 5, 6\rangle \cdot \langle -82, 50, 13\rangle = -328 + 250 + 78 = 0$ and
$\langle 3, 7, -8\rangle \cdot \langle -82, 50, 13\rangle = -246 + 350 - 104 = 0$.
\end{Answer}

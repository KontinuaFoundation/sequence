\chapter{Electric Motors and Generators}
\index{Electric Motor}

Count the electric motors in your home: the refrigerator compressor, the
fan in your laptop, the washing machine, the vibrator in your phone, the
spinning disc in a microwave. There are probably more than fifty. Now
follow the electricity that runs them back to the power plant, and you will
find a generator, which is a motor running backward. In this chapter, you
will see that motors and generators are the same machine, built on the two
ideas from the last chapters: currents feel forces in magnetic fields, and
changing magnetic fields create currents.

\section{How a Motor Turns}

In the magnetism chapter, you saw that a wire carrying current $I$ across a
magnetic field $B$ feels a force $F = ILB$. Now bend the wire into a
rectangular loop and set it between the poles of a magnet. Current flows
one way along one side of the loop and the opposite way along the other
side. So the two sides are pushed in \emph{opposite} directions: one up,
one down. That pair of forces twists the loop. A twisting effect is called
a \newterm{torque}.

Seen from the end, the current flows out of the page on one side of the
loop and into the page on the other. The forces push one side up and the
other down, so the loop turns.
\end{center}

For a coil of $N$ turns and area $A$, the torque is largest when the plane
of the coil lines up with the field:
$$\tau_{\max} = NBIA$$

There is a catch. Once the loop has turned a half turn, the side that was
being pushed up is now on the other side, and it gets pushed back. The loop
would just rock back and forth. The fix is a \index{commutator}
\newterm{commutator}: a split ring on the shaft. Carbon \newterm{brushes}
press against it to carry current into the coil, and every half turn the
gap in the ring swaps the connections, reversing the current in the coil.
The push always points the same way around, and the motor keeps spinning.

This design, a coil (the \index{armature}\newterm{armature}) spinning
between magnets with a commutator and brushes, is a \index{brushed
motor}\newterm{brushed DC motor}. It powers toys, drills, and car window
lifts.

\textbf{Worked example.} A motor coil has 50 turns and an area of
$0.01$ m$^2$, carries 2 A, and sits in a 0.2 T field. What is the largest
torque?

\textbf{Solution.} $\tau_{\max} = NBIA = (50)(0.2)(2)(0.01) = 0.2$ N$\cdot$m.
That is about the twist you would make pushing with 2 newtons (the weight
of a cup of coffee) on the end of a 10 cm wrench.

\subsection{Other Kinds of Motors}
Brushes wear out and make sparks. A \newterm{brushless motor} flips the
design around: the magnets spin and the coils stay still, and a small
computer switches the current in the coils at just the right moments.
Drones, computer fans, and electric cars use them. A \newterm{stepper
motor} turns in small, precise steps, perfect for 3D printers. An
\newterm{AC induction motor}, invented by Nikola Tesla, uses alternating
current to make a rotating magnetic field, which induces currents in the
rotor and drags it around. It needs no brushes or magnets, so it is cheap
and nearly indestructible; it runs fans, pumps, and factory machines.

\section{Power and Efficiency}

A motor turns electrical power into mechanical power. The electrical power
going in is $P = VI$.

\textbf{Worked example.} A motor draws $2$ A from a $120$ V supply. What
electrical power does it use?

\textbf{Solution.} $P = VI = (120)(2) = 240$ W.

No motor is perfect. Some energy is lost to friction and to heat in the
wires. The \newterm{efficiency} is the mechanical power out divided by the
electrical power in. Large industrial motors can be more than 95\%
efficient. Compare that with a car engine, which turns only about 30\% of
the energy in its fuel into motion.

\begin{Exercise}[title={Power at Higher Current}, label=emg1]
The same motor now draws $5$ A from a $120$ V supply. What is its power?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=emg1]
$P = (120)(5) = 600$ W.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[title={Lifting with a motor}, label=emg_eff]
A winch motor draws 240 W and lifts a 10 kg box straight up at a steady
0.2 m/s. What mechanical power does it deliver? What is its efficiency?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=emg_eff]
Lifting at steady speed takes a force equal to the weight,
$(10)(9.8) = 98$ N. Mechanical power is force times speed:
$(98)(0.2) = 19.6$ W. The efficiency is $\frac{19.6}{240} \approx 8\%$.
This winch wastes most of its power as heat, probably in a gearbox.
\end{Answer}

\section{Generators}
\index{Electric Motor ! Generator}

\index{electric generator}Now run the motor backward. Instead of feeding it
current, turn its shaft by hand. As the coil spins between the magnets, the
magnetic flux through it rises and falls. By Faraday's law, the changing
flux induces an emf, and current flows through anything connected to it.
A generator turns mechanical energy into electrical energy.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{basicGenerator.png}
    \caption{A basic generator: a loop spins between the poles of a magnet.
    Brushes on the rotating rings carry the induced current to a light
    bulb.}
    \label{fig:basic-generator-diagram}
\end{figure}

Every small DC motor is also a generator. Connect a motor to a light bulb
and turn the shaft with a crank, and the bulb lights.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.35\textwidth]{basicGeneratorCrank.png}
    \caption{Turning the generator with a hand crank.}
    \label{fig:basic-generator-w-crank}
\end{figure}

You will notice something as you crank: the brighter the bulb, the harder
the crank is to turn. That is Lenz's law. The current in the coil feels a
force from the magnets that opposes the turning. The energy in the light
comes from the work your arm does.

When a coil of $N$ turns and area $A$ spins at a steady rate in a field $B$,
the angle of the coil changes steadily, so the flux follows a cosine and
the emf follows a sine wave:
$$\mathcal{E} = NBA\omega \sin(\omega t)$$
where $\omega$ is the rotation rate in radians per second. That is
alternating current! A generator naturally makes AC. Spin it 60 times a
second, and you get the 60 Hz power in your outlet.

\textbf{Worked example.} A generator coil has 100 turns, an area of
$0.01$ m$^2$, and spins 60 times per second in a 0.5 T field. What is its
peak emf?

\textbf{Solution.} 60 turns per second is $\omega = 2\pi(60) \approx 377$
rad/s. The peak emf is $NBA\omega = (100)(0.5)(0.01)(377) \approx 188$ V.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.35\textwidth]{basicGeneratorWheel.png}
    \caption{Any spinning wheel can drive a generator.}
    \label{fig:basic-generator-w-wheel}
\end{figure}

Almost all of the world's electricity comes from spinning generators. In a
hydroelectric dam, falling water spins the turbines. In a wind farm, the
wind does. In coal, natural gas, and nuclear plants, heat boils water, and
the high-pressure steam spins turbines. (Solar panels are the big
exception: they turn light directly into electricity with no moving parts.)

\begin{Exercise}[title={Spin it faster}, label=emg_gen]
The generator in the worked example is spun twice as fast. What happens to
its peak emf and to the frequency of the AC it produces?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=emg_gen]
Both double: the peak emf becomes about 377 V, and the frequency becomes
120 Hz. That is why power plants must spin their generators at a precise,
steady speed.
\end{Answer}

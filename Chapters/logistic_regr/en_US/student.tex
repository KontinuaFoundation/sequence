\chapter{Logistic Regression}

Will it rain tomorrow? Will this email get opened? Will you get into the
college you want? The answer is yes or no, but the honest prediction is a
probability: ``70\% chance of rain.'' Logistic regression turns numbers like
humidity, hours of study, or GPA and test scores into exactly that kind of
probability. We start with a single predictor, then let the model use several
at once.

\section{A Categorical Response}\index{logistic regression}\index{logistic regression!simple}
Linear regression predicts a quantitative response. \newterm{Logistic regression}
predicts a categorical response, most often a \emph{binary} outcome coded as 0 or 1.
The goal is to model $p = P(Y = 1)$, the probability of the positive outcome, as a function of a predictor $x$. For example, $x$ could be hours of study, and $Y = 1$ could mean that the student passes the test.

\section{The Model}
A straight line cannot be used for $p$, because a line can produce values below 0 or
above 1. Instead, the model uses the \newterm{log odds}, also called the logit:
$$\ln\left(\frac{p}{1 - p}\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x$$
The odds $\frac{p}{1-p}$ take any value from 0 to $\infty$, and their logarithm takes any
real value. Solving for $p$ gives
$$p(x) = \frac{e^{\beta_0 + \beta_1 x}}{1 + e^{\beta_0 + \beta_1 x}}$$
This always lies between 0 and 1. The probability of the negative outcome is
$1 - p(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{\beta_0 + \beta_1 x}}$.

\section{Estimating the Parameters}\index{maximum likelihood}
Linear regression chooses parameters by least squares. Logistic regression chooses
them by \newterm{maximum likelihood}: the estimates are the values that make the
observed outcomes most probable under the model. Software does this calculation for you.

\section{Interpreting the Coefficients}
Take the model $\beta_0 = -4$ and $\beta_1 = 1$. Then
$$p(x) = \frac{e^{-4 + x}}{1 + e^{-4 + x}}$$
At $x = 3$, $p = \frac{e^{-1}}{1 + e^{-1}} \approx 0.269$. At $x = 4$, $p = 0.5$. At
$x = 5$, $p \approx 0.731$. So the probability rises from 27\% to 73\% as $x$ goes from
3 to 5.

The coefficient $\beta_1$ controls how fast the odds change. Each one-unit increase in $x$
multiplies the odds by $e^{\beta_1} = e \approx 2.718$. The odds of the positive
outcome at $x = 3$ are $e^{-1} \approx 0.368$, and at $x = 4$ they are $1$.

\section{Predicting a Class}
To classify, choose a threshold, often 0.5. Predict $Y = 1$ when $p(x) \ge 0.5$. In this model,
that is when $x \ge 4$. The threshold is a choice, and changing it changes the errors the model makes.
A spam filter might demand $p \ge 0.95$ before hiding a message, because
hiding a real email is worse than letting one piece of spam through.

\section{More Than One Predictor}\index{logistic regression!multiple}
A model with one predictor is called \newterm{simple logistic regression}. Real yes-or-no outcomes usually
have several causes: whether you get into a college depends on your GPA, test scores, activities, and essays
all at once. \newterm{Multiple logistic regression} extends the model to several predictors
$x_1, \ldots, x_p$. The model for the log odds is
$$\ln\left(\frac{P(Y = 1)}{1 - P(Y = 1)}\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_p x_p$$
Equivalently,
$$P(Y = 1 \mid x) = \frac{e^{\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_p x_p}}{1 + e^{\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_p x_p}}$$

\subsection{Interpreting Several Coefficients}
Each coefficient describes one predictor while the others are held constant. A one-unit increase in $x_i$
multiplies the odds of $Y = 1$ by $e^{\beta_i}$.

\subsection{A Worked Example}
Suppose a student's chance of passing an exam is modeled with hours studied $x_1$ and prior course
completion $x_2$ (1 if yes, 0 if no), with $\beta_0 = -3$, $\beta_1 = 0.5$, and $\beta_2 = 1$. The linear predictor is
$$\eta = -3 + 0.5 x_1 + x_2$$
For a student who studies 4 hours and completed the prior course, $\eta = -3 + 2 + 1 = 0$, so
$P(\text{pass}) = \frac{1}{1 + e^0} = 0.5$. For a student who studies 2 hours and did not complete the
prior course, $\eta = -2$, so $P(\text{pass}) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{2}} \approx 0.119$. The odds ratio for
hours studied is $e^{0.5} \approx 1.649$.

\section{The Divide by 4 Rule}
The coefficients can be used to estimate effects quickly. The logistic curve has slope $\frac{1}{4}$ at its midpoint,
where $P = 0.5$. So near $P = 0.5$, a one-unit increase in $x_i$ changes the probability by about $\frac{\beta_i}{4}$.
This is an approximation. It is most accurate when the probability is near 0.5 and the coefficient is small.

\section{Checking the Model}
Compare models with and without a predictor using a likelihood ratio test or an information criterion. Check
that predictors are not strongly correlated with each other, since correlated predictors make the individual
coefficients unstable.

\begin{Exercise}[label=slog1, title=Compute a probability]
Using $\beta_0 = -4$ and $\beta_1 = 1$, find $p(2)$.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=slog1]
$p(2) = \frac{e^{-2}}{1 + e^{-2}} \approx \frac{0.1353}{1.1353} \approx 0.119$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=slog2, title=Find the threshold value]
With $\beta_0 = -4$ and $\beta_1 = 1$, for which $x$ is $p(x) = 0.5$?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=slog2]
$p(x) = 0.5$ when $e^{\beta_0 + \beta_1 x} = 1$, so $\beta_0 + \beta_1 x = 0$. Thus $x = 4$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=slog3, title=Interpret the odds ratio]
If $\beta_1 = 0.5$, by what factor do the odds of $Y = 1$ change when $x$ increases by 1?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=slog3]
The odds are multiplied by $e^{0.5} \approx 1.649$, an increase of about 65\%.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=slog4, title=From odds to probability]
A model gives log odds of $0.8$ for a particular student passing. What are the
odds, and what is the probability of passing?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=slog4]
The odds are $e^{0.8} \approx 2.23$, meaning passing is about 2.23 times as likely as
failing. The probability is $\frac{2.23}{1 + 2.23} \approx 0.69$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=mlog1, title=Compute a probability]
Using the model above, find $P(\text{pass})$ for a student who studies 6 hours and did not complete the prior course.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=mlog1]
$\eta = -3 + 0.5(6) + 0 = 0$, so $P = 0.5$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=mlog2, title=Odds ratio]
In the same model, by what factor do the odds of passing change when a student completes the prior course, holding hours fixed?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=mlog2]
The odds are multiplied by $e^{\beta_2} = e^{1} \approx 2.718$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=mlog3, title=Divide by 4]
In the worked example, the first student (4 hours, completed the prior course)
has $P(\text{pass}) = 0.5$. Use the divide-by-4 rule to estimate their
probability of passing if they study one more hour. Then compute it exactly.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=mlog3]
The rule says the probability rises by about $\frac{0.5}{4} = 0.125$, to about $0.625$.
Exactly, $\eta = 0.5$ and $P = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-0.5}} \approx 0.622$. The rule is
very close because the student starts at $P = 0.5$.
\end{Answer}

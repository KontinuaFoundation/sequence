\chapter{One-Hot Encoding}

\section{Categorical Data}\index{categorical variable}
A \newterm{categorical variable} takes values that are labels rather than numbers. Color (red, blue, green),
size (small, medium, large), and city are categorical. Most learning algorithms need numbers as input, so
categories must be converted first. Why not just number them, red $= 1$,
blue $= 2$, green $= 3$? Because then the model would believe that green is
``bigger'' than red, and that blue sits exactly halfway between them. Colors
have no such order. Think of a survey question about your favorite subject. Each answer becomes its own yes or no column.

\section{The Encoding}\index{one-hot encoding}
\newterm{One-hot encoding} creates one binary column for each category. The column is 1 when the row has that
category and 0 otherwise. A variable with $n$ categories becomes $n$ columns, and each row has exactly one 1.

For the variable \emph{color} with the values red, blue, and green:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
Color & is\_red & is\_blue & is\_green \\
\hline
red & 1 & 0 & 0 \\
\hline
blue & 0 & 1 & 0 \\
\hline
green & 0 & 0 & 1 \\
\hline
red & 1 & 0 & 0 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\section{The Dummy Variable Trap}
One column is redundant. If you know is\_red and is\_blue, you already know is\_green: it is 1 exactly
when the other two are 0, so is\_green $= 1 -$ is\_red $-$ is\_blue. In a regression with an intercept,
the three columns add up to the column of ones, so the model cannot tell their effects apart. Dropping one column, such as is\_green, avoids this problem in linear and
logistic regression. Keeping all $n$ columns is fine for many tree-based and neural network methods.

\section{When to Use It}
Use one-hot encoding for nominal categories, where the labels have no order. For ordered categories, such as
small, medium, and large, an integer code that respects the order may be more appropriate.

\begin{Exercise}[label=oh1, title=Encode a variable]
Write the one-hot encoding of the pets cat, dog, fish, cat, using three columns in that order.
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=oh1]
$(1,0,0)$, $(0,1,0)$, $(0,0,1)$, $(1,0,0)$.
\end{Answer}

\begin{Exercise}[label=oh2, title=Dropping a column]
A regression uses a color variable with three categories encoded as three columns. Why should one column be dropped?
\end{Exercise}
\begin{Answer}[ref=oh2]
The three columns add up to 1 in every row, so they are linearly dependent with the intercept. This makes the
coefficients unidentifiable. Dropping one column fixes this, and the dropped category becomes the baseline.
\end{Answer}
